*An ongoing working archive accompanying [Essential Fatty Acids: Supply, Stress, and Adaptation](/essays/essential-fatty-acids-supply-stress-and-adaptation/). Originally begun in March 2020 and continuously expanded, this document maps essential fatty acid and linoleic acid research from Evans & Burr (1927) and Burr & Burr (1929) to present day.*

Each entry links directly to peer-reviewed literature and states what the experiments establish, what they do not establish, and where findings genuinely conflict. Entries are organized uniformly by biological system and will continue to evolve as new chapters of the series are written.

*Abbreviations: EFA, essential fatty acid; EFAD, essential fatty acid deficiency; LA, linoleic acid; ALA, α-linolenic acid; AA, arachidonic acid; EPA, eicosapentaenoic acid; DHA, docosahexaenoic acid; OXLAM, oxidized linoleic acid metabolite; TEWL, transepidermal water loss; TPN, total parenteral nutrition.*

## Part I: Foundational History, Mitochondrial Bioenergetics, Cell Fate, and Lipoproteins

### 1. Foundational History, Deficiency Models, and Essentiality (1927–1978)

#### Burr & Burr (1929, 1930) — Discovery of fat deficiency disease and linoleic acid essentiality

**Direct animal evidence that overturned the view of fat as mere fuel.** Burr and Burr {{< cite "burr1929" >}} fed weanling rats purified, fat-free diets replete in known vitamins and minerals. The animals developed growth arrest and weight decline, dandruff and scaly skin, inflamed hind paws, hemorrhagic spots progressing to caudal (tail) necrosis, markedly elevated water consumption (later explained by transepidermal water loss across a damaged skin barrier; see {{< cite "zheng2011" >}}), renal cortical and intertubular hemorrhage with urinary tract pathology (hematuria was noted across early fat-free studies), impaired ovulation and reproductive failure, and early death.

In their follow-up study, Burr and Burr {{< cite "burr1930" >}} demonstrated that saturated fats (including butter, coconut oil, and hydrogenated oils) and oleic acid failed to cure the syndrome, whereas small amounts of **linoleic acid** (18:2n-6) restored growth, skin integrity, water balance, and reproduction. This established that warm-blooded animals cannot synthesize linoleic acid *de novo* and coined the concept of essential fatty acids. What these classic rat experiments established was physiological necessity under near-total exclusion; they did not define optimal human intakes, distinguish parent LA functions from downstream arachidonate functions, or evaluate modern high-LA dietary levels.

#### Hansen et al. (1958, 1963) — Infant EFAD and human linoleic acid requirement trials

**Direct clinical trial evidence in infants, with boundaries on adult extrapolation.** Hansen and colleagues {{< cite "hansen1958" >}} documented dry, thickened, desquamating skin, perianal irritation, and poor growth in young infants fed skim-milk formulas severely depleted in linoleic acid (<0.1% of energy). Adding linoleic acid resolved the skin lesions and normalized biochemical markers.

In a larger controlled evaluation of 428 infants fed five milk mixtures varying in fat type and linoleic acid content, Hansen and colleagues {{< cite "hansen1963" >}} showed that infants receiving <0.1% of calories as LA developed clinical skin abnormalities and required higher caloric intake per unit of weight gain, alongside elevated serum trienoic-to-tetraenoic acid ratios (consistent with Holman's biochemical criterion {{< cite "holman1960" >}}). Intakes of roughly 1% of energy as LA prevented overt deficiency signs, and 2–4% of energy (approximating human milk at the time) normalized both clinical status and serum fatty-acid profiles. These trials provide strong direct evidence of human LA essentiality during rapid growth; they do not establish that intakes well above ~2–4% of energy confer additional benefit, nor do they directly measure adult maintenance requirements.

#### Sinclair (1956) — The "relative EFA deficiency" hypothesis of Western disease

**Historical synthesis and hypothesis; not a controlled test of causation.** In a famous 1956 letter to *The Lancet*, Hugh Sinclair {{< cite "sinclair1956" >}} argued that rising rates of atherosclerosis and ischemic heart disease in Western populations reflected not simply total fat intake, but a **relative deficiency of essential fatty acids** exacerbated by saturated fats and unnatural isomers formed during industrial hydrogenation. Mechanistically, he proposed that cholesterol is normally esterified with polyunsaturated EFAs for physiological transport and clearance, and that esterification with saturated or unnatural fatty acids produces clearance-resistant arterial deposits.

Sinclair's letter proved prescient in highlighting fat *quality* (including early warnings about trans fats and hydrogenation) and anticipating the central role of cholesteryl linoleate in plasma lipid transport {{< cite "glomset1968" >}}. However, his 1956 paper was a theoretical argument drawing on ecological comparisons (such as Inuit diets) and rodent deficiency observations—not an interventional demonstration that increasing dietary LA prevents human atherosclerosis or mortality. Subsequent decades of lipid biology confirmed parts of the transport biochemistry while showing that human cardiovascular outcomes cannot be deduced from deficiency models alone (see clinical trial conflicts below).

#### Collins et al. (1971) — First documented adult human EFAD on fat-free parenteral nutrition

**Direct adult human case evidence, later corroborated by controlled cohorts.** Collins and colleagues {{< cite "collins1971" >}} reported the first well-documented adult human case of linoleic acid deficiency: a 44-year-old man maintained on fat-free intravenous nutrition for 100 days following massive small-bowel resection. Despite adequate non-fat calories, he developed a scaly dermatitis and profound biochemical deficiency—with eicosatrienoic acid (Mead acid, 20:3n-9; {{< cite "mead1956" >}}) rising to ~10% of serum phospholipids. Intravenous infusion of a soybean-oil emulsion (Intralipid) cleared the rash, lowered Mead acid, restored arachidonate, and led the authors to estimate a minimum adult intravenous LA requirement of at least ~7.5 g/day in that setting.

Paired with subsequent controlled fat-free feeding trials in healthy adults {{< cite "wene1975" >}} and surgical cohorts {{< cite "fleming1976" >}}{{< cite "goodgame1978" >}}, Collins's report confirmed that adult humans can develop symptomatic LA deficiency when insulin-driven lipolysis suppression during continuous glucose infusion traps adipose LA stores. Like other TPN studies, it defines an extreme physiological boundary rather than a risk under ordinary mixed oral diets.

#### Evans & Burr (1927, 1928) — Highly purified fat-free diets and the pre-1929 discovery path

**Direct experimental animal history that set the stage for Burr & Burr (1929).** Throughout the 1910s and early 1920s, leading nutritionists (including Osborne and Mendel) concluded that animals could thrive without dietary fat if supplied with calories and fat-soluble vitamins, because crude cornstarch and unextracted casein carried enough residual lipid to mask deficiency. While studying vitamin E at the University of California, Herbert Evans and George Burr {{< cite "evans1927" >}} switched rats to rigorously purified diets made of recrystallized sucrose, alcohol- and ether-extracted casein, salts, and vitamin concentrates. On these ultra-purified diets, animals exhibited stunted growth, irregular ovulation, and skin lesions that could not be cured by known vitamins.

In their third report in the series, Evans and Burr {{< cite "evans1928" >}} demonstrated that adding natural fats (such as lard) to the purified fat-free ration restored normal growth and prevented the deficiency syndrome—narrowing the curative factor to the saponifiable lipid fraction rather than a trace unsaponifiable vitamin. That breakthrough directly set up George and Mildred Burr's subsequent isolation of the full deficiency syndrome {{< cite "burr1929" >}} and identification of linoleic acid as the curative nutrient {{< cite "burr1930" >}}. What the 1927–1928 papers prove is how analytical purification uncovered a real mammalian dietary requirement; they did not yet identify which specific fatty acid was responsible or distinguish structural from signaling functions.

#### Holman's quantitative multi-species dose-response & requirement framework (1960–1978)

**Direct multi-species biochemical dose-response studies and quantitative syntheses.** Building on Mead's identification of 5,8,11-eicosatrienoic acid (20:3n-9, Mead acid; {{< cite "mead1956" >}}) and his own initial rat threshold paper {{< cite "holman1960" >}}, Ralph Holman systematically mapped tissue fatty-acid responses across graded dietary LA intakes. In male weanling rats, Mohrhauer and Holman {{< cite "mohrhauer1963jlr" >}} fed pure ethyl linoleate, linolenate, and arachidonate across logarithmic dose tiers and showed that hepatic arachidonate rises steeply from 0% to ~1% of calories as LA before plateauing, while 20:3n-9 falls reciprocally (parallel to their *Journal of Nutrition* competition study {{< cite "mohrhauer1963" >}}). Extending this to sex differences, Pudelkewicz, Seufert, and Holman {{< cite "pudelkewicz1968" >}} showed that female rats require somewhat less dietary LA (~0.5% of calories) than males (~1.3% of calories) to normalize growth and keep the triene:tetraene ratio below 0.4, demonstrating that minimum LA needs vary by sex and physiological demand.

Translating the biochemical index to humans using serum samples from Hansen's infant trials {{< cite "hansen1963" >}}, Holman, Caster, and Wiese {{< cite "holman1964" >}} fit an exponential dose-response equation relating infant serum triene:tetraene ratios to dietary linoleate intake, concluding that ~1% of calories represents the minimum infant requirement and ~4% of calories approximates human milk sufficiency. In his comprehensive monographs and reviews, Holman {{< cite "holman1971" >}}{{< cite "holman1978" >}} synthesized requirement data across rats, mice, swine, poultry, dogs, and humans—establishing ~1–2% of total energy as the conserved mammalian baseline to prevent biochemical EFAD, while noting that requirements shift upward with saturated fat, cholesterol, or *trans*-fat load. **What Holman's curves prove and do not prove:** They establish an objective biochemical floor for avoiding EFAD and confirm that tissue arachidonate largely saturates at modest LA intakes; they do *not* prove that intakes far above 2–4% of energy confer additional benefit, nor does the classic 0.4 triene:tetraene cutoff capture isolated n-3 (ALA/DHA) deficiency or sub-clinical oxylipin shifts.

#### Industrial refining, partial hydrogenation (*trans* fats competing with LA desaturation), and the 20th-century seed-oil shift

- **Separating the seed-oil transition from industrial *trans* fats**

**Historical food-supply modeling paired with direct mechanistic enzymology and human clinical trials.** Using USDA economic disappearance records across 373 food commodities from 1909 to 1999, Blasbalg and colleagues {{< cite "blasbalg2011" >}} estimated that U.S. apparent consumption of soybean oil rose more than 1,000-fold during the 20th century, lifting apparent linoleic acid availability from **2.79% to 7.21% of energy** while ALA rose only modestly (0.39% to 0.72% of energy), widening the dietary LA:ALA ratio from 6.4 to 10.0. This secular supply shift aligns closely with the 136% rise in U.S. adult adipose LA biopsies from 1959 to 2008 {{< cite "guyenet2015" >}}. *Methodological boundary:* Food disappearance data overestimate absolute human intake (due to frying discard, spoilage, and plate waste), though they reliably track direction and magnitude of commodity shifts.

Crucially, mid-century seed-oil expansion occurred alongside widespread industrial **partial hydrogenation** (shortening and stick margarines), which Sinclair had warned about early {{< cite "sinclair1956" >}}. In rats, Hill, Holman, and colleagues {{< cite "hill1982" >}} demonstrated that feeding partially hydrogenated vegetable oil containing *trans* octadecenoic isomers produced a more severe essential fatty acid deficiency than saturated fat alone by suppressing hepatic microsomal **Δ⁶- and Δ⁵-desaturase** activities and impairing conversion of LA to arachidonate—and raising dietary LA did not prevent *trans* isomer accumulation in tissues. In human premature infants, Koletzko {{< cite "koletzko1992" >}} observed significant inverse correlations between plasma *trans* fatty acids and both n-6/n-3 long-chain PUFA levels and birth weight (observational evidence consistent with perinatal desaturase interference, though not proof of causation on its own). In adult humans, a landmark randomized crossover trial by Mensink and Katan {{< cite "mensink1990" >}} proved that replacing 10% of energy from *cis*-oleic acid with *trans*-oleic acid simultaneously **raised LDL-cholesterol and lowered HDL-cholesterol**—a lipoprotein profile worse than saturated fat. **Why this history matters for LA debates:** Industrial partial hydrogenation both stripped native LA from seed oils and created metabolic antagonists of LA desaturation and lipoprotein clearance; because 1960s–1970s intervention margarines in trials like SDHS and MCE {{< cite "ramsden2013" >}}{{< cite "ramsden2016" >}} likely carried *trans* fats, historical seed-oil epidemiology and mid-century RCTs cannot be interpreted without disentangling unhydrogenated LA from partially hydrogenated oils.

#### Fat-free feeding and deficiency

**Direct human evidence, under specific feeding conditions.** Wene, Connor, and DenBesten {{< cite "wene1975" >}} gave healthy men calorie-maintaining, fat-free glucose/amino-acid diets by continuous intravenous or nasogastric feeding. Plasma fatty-acid changes indicating deficiency developed within two weeks. This demonstrates that adequate calories and existing body fat do not necessarily prevent biochemical deficiency during continuous fat-free feeding.

Clinical signs are a separate endpoint. In Goodgame, Lowry, and Brennan {{< cite "goodgame1978" >}}, two of 32 patients receiving intravenous nutrition developed skin lesions suggestive of EFA deficiency. These studies support the note in the context of fat-free nutritional support; they do not show that an ordinary glucose-containing meal causes deficiency.

#### The experimental diet matters

**Direct animal evidence, with a qualification about tallow.** Ling and colleagues {{< cite "ling2010" >}} compared fat-free diets with diets containing hydrogenated coconut oil in rats. Both produced biochemical EFA deficiency within two weeks, although their effects on individual tissues and fatty acids differed.

Iritani, Ikeda, and Fukuda {{< cite "iritani1984" >}} used **hydrogenated beef tallow** in a linoleic-acid-deficient feeding protocol after prior depletion. That distinction matters: this supports the use of deliberately EFA-poor experimental fats and diets, not the claim that ordinary tallow inevitably induces deficiency regardless of the rest of the diet.

#### How quickly deficiency develops

**Strong evidence for rapid biochemical change; symptoms are less uniform.** Goodgame and colleagues {{< cite "goodgame1978" >}} found abnormal linoleic- and eicosatrienoic-acid measurements after one week of fat-free intravenous nutrition; all patients studied at four weeks had an elevated triene:tetraene ratio. Only two developed the reported skin lesions.

Wene and colleagues {{< cite "wene1975" >}}, likewise demonstrated biochemical deficiency during calorie-maintaining feeding in healthy men. These findings support a timescale of days to weeks in those settings. They do not establish a universal timetable for visible symptoms in people eating mixed diets, or make biochemical deficiency and symptomatic illness interchangeable.

#### Essential precursors and conditional requirements

**Human evidence for distinct precursor needs; downstream requirements depend on context.** The fat-free feeding study by Wene and colleagues {{< cite "wene1975" >}}, demonstrated linoleate depletion and biochemical deficiency. Holman, Johnson, and Hatch {{< cite "holman1982" >}} described an omega-3 deficiency that developed despite a linoleate-rich feeding regimen and improved when an ALA-containing emulsion was supplied. The two fatty-acid families are not interchangeable.

Human tracer experiments by Burdge and Wootton {{< cite "burdge2002" >}}, demonstrate conversion of ALA into longer-chain products. Conditional essentiality concerns situations in which synthesis cannot meet a particular requirement; it should not imply that AA, EPA, and DHA have identical status in every life stage or disease. Species differences in conversion also do not mean ordinary mice possess a pathway for manufacturing LA or ALA from scratch.

### 2. Mitochondrial Bioenergetics, Cardiolipin, Dynamics, and Cell Fate

#### Tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin (L4-CL), cristae architecture, and respiratory supercomplexes

**Strong biochemical and biophysical evidence across yeast, rodents, and human tissues.** Cardiolipin is a dimeric phospholipid localized almost exclusively to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where its small polar headgroup and four bulky acyl chains confer conical geometry that promotes the high negative membrane curvature of mitochondrial cristae {{< cite "schlame2017" >}}. In mammalian heart and skeletal muscle—and to a substantial though slightly lower extent in liver—post-synthetic remodeling enriches cardiolipin predominantly with linoleic acid, so that **tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin (L4-CL)** accounts for roughly 70–80% of cardiac cardiolipin {{< cite "schlame2002" >}}{{< cite "schlame2017" >}}.

Functional studies demonstrate that cardiolipin is required to stabilize respiratory chain supercomplexes (such as complexes III and IV in yeast {{< cite "pfeiffer2003" >}} and I–III₂–IV respirasomes in mammalian cells {{< cite "mckenzie2006" >}}), anchoring electron-transport efficiency and limiting proton leaks across cristae folds. Crucially, lipidomic and modeling work by Oemer and colleagues {{< cite "oemer2020" >}} showed that tissue-specific cardiolipin acyl diversity is not dictated solely by enzyme transcription; rather, the fatty-acid composition of the surrounding cellular phospholipid pool—strongly shaped by dietary LA availability—directly governs cardiolipin assembly. This establishes a concrete structural role for linoleate in oxidative tissues, though it does not imply that supra-physiological LA intakes linearly improve mitochondrial function once cardiolipin pools are replete.

#### Tafazzin remodeling, Barth syndrome, and dietary responsiveness of cardiolipin

**Direct human genetic evidence paired with translational feeding studies.** Barth syndrome—an X-linked disorder characterized by cardiomyopathy, skeletal myopathy, neutropenia, and growth delay—is caused by mutations in *TAFAZZIN* (formerly *TAZ*), which encodes a phospholipid–lysophospholipid transacylase that remodels nascent cardiolipin into L4-CL {{< cite "vreken2000" >}}{{< cite "schlame2012" >}}. Schlame and colleagues {{< cite "schlame2002" >}} demonstrated severe depletion of tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin in cardiac and skeletal muscle from Barth syndrome patients, and McKenzie and colleagues {{< cite "mckenzie2006" >}} showed destabilization of respiratory chain supercomplexes in patient lymphoblasts. Further work in tafazzin-knockdown cardiac mitochondria indicates that the bioenergetic defect extends beyond supercomplex geometry to impaired CoA-dependent oxidation of pyruvate and palmitoylcarnitine {{< cite "le2020" >}}. Notably, Schlame and colleagues {{< cite "schlame2012" >}} showed that tafazzin's preference for forming L4-CL reflects the physical packing state of non-bilayer lipid regions in curved membranes rather than rigid active-site substrate specificity alone.

Beyond rare genetic disease, dietary LA availability measurably alters cardiolipin profiles. In spontaneously hypertensive heart failure (SHHF) rats, Chicco and colleagues {{< cite "chicco2008" >}} found that a linoleate-rich high-fat diet preserved cardiac L4-CL, supported cytochrome oxidase activity, and decreased mortality compared with lard-rich or low-fat diets. In human adults, a randomized controlled trial by Cole and colleagues {{< cite "cole2022" >}} confirmed that consuming a linoleic-acid-rich oil increased circulating and PBMC tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin species. What these studies do *not* establish is that dietary LA supplementation alone can bypass defective tafazzin remodeling in Barth syndrome, or that raising PBMC L4-CL in healthy adults translates directly into lower clinical heart-failure risk.

#### Linoleic acid oxidation: β-oxidation, CPT1, auxiliary enzymes, peroxisomes, and carbon recycling

**Direct tracer and genetic evidence in rodents and humans.** Whole-body tracer studies refute the assumption that dietary PUFAs are simply shunted passively into storage. In rats given ¹⁴C-labeled fatty acids, Leyton, Drury, and Crawford {{< cite "leyton1987" >}} found that linoleic acid was oxidized more rapidly than palmitic or stearic acid, though less rapidly than oleic acid or α-linolenic acid (ALA). In normal-weight men given ¹³C-labeled dietary fats over 9 hours, DeLany and colleagues {{< cite "delany2000" >}} observed a consistent hierarchy of fractional oxidation: laurate (12:0) > linolenate (18:3n-3) > elaidate > oleate (18:1) > **linoleate (18:2n-6)** > palmitate (16:0) > stearate (18:0)—showing that LA is burned more readily than long-chain saturates yet somewhat more conserved postprandially than ALA or oleate.

At the enzymatic level, Gavino and Gavino {{< cite "gavino1991" >}} showed that rat liver outer mitochondrial carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT1) exhibits distinct kinetic selectivity toward long-chain polyunsaturated acyl-CoAs including linoleoyl-CoA. Because LA's *cis*-9,*cis*-12 double bonds cannot pass directly through the standard β-oxidation spiral, complete mitochondrial breakdown requires auxiliary enzymes—most notably Δ³,Δ²-enoyl-CoA isomerase and mitochondrial 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase (DECR1). Miinalainen and colleagues {{< cite "miinalainen2009" >}} demonstrated that *Decr1*-null mice accumulate partially degraded unsaturated acylcarnitines (including decadienoylcarnitine from LA) and suffer fasting hypoglycemia and cold intolerance. Peroxisomal β-oxidation also processes polyunsaturated fatty acids through partial chain-shortening cycles before transferring shortened acyl units to mitochondria {{< cite "poirier2006" >}}. Finally, Cunnane and colleagues {{< cite "cunnane2003" >}} synthesized isotope-tracer evidence showing that a substantial fraction of acetyl-CoA released from LA and ALA β-oxidation is **recycled into *de novo* synthesis of saturated fatty acids and cholesterol**, particularly during growth. Thus, dietary LA enters an active multi-organelle flux rather than remaining static in membranes—though acute 9-hour oxidation studies do not predict long-term body-fat accumulation under chronic caloric surplus.

#### Mitochondrial coupling, proton leak, and the membrane-pacemaker hypothesis

**Direct animal evidence for EFAD uncoupling; mixed mechanistic support for a simple PUFA–proton-leak rule.** Early work by Klein and Johnson {{< cite "klein1954" >}} demonstrated reduced P:O ratios (uncoupled oxidative phosphorylation) in liver mitochondria from fat-deficient rats—providing an early bioenergetic explanation for why EFA-deficient animals exhibit elevated resting oxygen consumption alongside poor growth and impaired cold tolerance {{< cite "rafael1988" >}}.

In normal physiology, Hulbert and Else {{< cite "hulbert1999" >}} proposed the "membrane pacemaker" hypothesis, observing across vertebrate species that higher mitochondrial membrane polyunsaturation (often enriched in DHA and/or LA depending on tissue and species) correlates with higher standard metabolic rate and higher inner-membrane proton leak. However, direct biophysical testing by Brookes, Hulbert, and Brand {{< cite "brookes1997" >}} showed that protein-free liposomes constructed from mitochondrial inner-membrane phospholipids of eight vertebrate species had virtually identical, low proton permeability regardless of large differences in fatty-acid unsaturation—accounting for only a small fraction of intact mitochondrial proton leak. Consequently, membrane fatty-acid composition appears to influence proton conductance primarily by modulating the activity of inner-membrane proteins (such as adenine nucleotide translocase or uncoupling proteins) rather than by making the lipid bilayer itself passively leaky. Neither line of work establishes that raising dietary LA in humans raises basal metabolic rate or promotes fat loss.

#### Brown adipose tissue (BAT) thermogenesis: cardiolipin, UCP1, and LA-derived lipokines

**Strong mechanistic evidence in mice, with emerging human biomarker translation.** Brown adipose tissue dissipates chemical energy as heat via uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1). Patch-clamp recordings of brown-fat inner mitochondrial membranes by Fedorenko, Lishko, and Kirichok {{< cite "fedorenko2012" >}} demonstrated that long-chain free fatty acids act as essential H⁺/anion symport cofactors required to activate UCP1 proton transport. Sustarsic and colleagues {{< cite "sustarsic2018" >}} further showed that *de novo* cardiolipin synthesis in brown and beige adipocytes is essential for UCP1 stability, mitochondrial respiration, and systemic glucose and energy homeostasis in mice, while Snoke and colleagues {{< cite "snoke2023" >}} reported that an LA-enriched diet increased interscapular BAT tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin, OXPHOS protein content, and uncoupling capacity during diet-induced weight gain in mice.

Independently of structural cardiolipin, Lynes and colleagues {{< cite "lynes2017" >}} identified **12,13-diHOME**—a cytochrome P450 / soluble epoxide hydrolase oxylipin derived specifically from linoleic acid—as a cold-induced lipokine in both mice and humans that stimulates fatty-acid uptake into BAT and enhances cold tolerance. These findings reinforce that EFAD impairs non-shivering thermogenesis {{< cite "rafael1988" >}} and show that LA participates in both BAT membrane architecture and signaling. They do not establish that eating more linoleic acid meaningfully increases BAT thermogenesis or prevents obesity in free-living humans.

#### Cardiolipin externalization as an "eat-me" signal for selective mitophagy (PLS3 and LC3 binding)

**Direct cell-biological and biophysical mechanistic evidence in mammalian neurons and cell lines.** Under healthy conditions, cardiolipin (predominantly L4-CL in oxidative tissues; {{< cite "schlame2017" >}}) is confined almost exclusively to the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM). Chu and colleagues {{< cite "chu2013" >}} discovered that sub-lethal mitochondrial stressors (such as rotenone, 6-hydroxydopamine, or staurosporine in primary cortical neurons and SH-SY5Y cells) trigger rapid **externalization of cardiolipin to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM)** via **phospholipid scramblase-3 (PLS3)**. Once exposed on the cytosolic face of the OMM, cardiolipin acts as an "eat-me" signal recognized directly by basic residues in the N-terminal domain of the autophagosome protein **LC3 (MAP1LC3)**, recruiting the autophagic machinery to eliminate damaged mitochondria before full bioenergetic collapse.

Knockdown of cardiolipin synthase or PLS3—or mutation of LC3's cardiolipin-binding sites—blocked mitophagy without preventing general autophagy. **What this proves and does not prove:** It proves that intact (non-peroxidized) externalized cardiolipin serves as a conserved receptor-independent mitophagy signal that competes temporally with apoptosis; it does *not* show that altering dietary LA intake modulates neuronal mitophagy *in vivo* in humans, nor is externalized cardiolipin the only mitophagy pathway (it operates alongside PINK1/Parkin-mediated ubiquitin signaling).

#### Intrinsic apoptosis: two-step cytochrome *c* release and cytochrome *c* cardiolipin peroxidase activity

**Strong biochemical, oxidative-lipidomic, and cellular evidence.** Approximately 15–20% of mitochondrial cytochrome *c* is tightly bound to the IMM via electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions with cardiolipin. Ott and colleagues {{< cite "ott2002" >}} demonstrated in isolated mitochondria that apoptotic release of cytochrome *c* requires a **two-step process**: first, detachment of cardiolipin-bound cytochrome *c* into a soluble intermembrane-space pool (triggered by cardiolipin oxidation or electrostatic disruption), and second, Bax/Bak-mediated permeabilization of the outer mitochondrial membrane. Neither step alone sufficed for full release.

Kagan and colleagues {{< cite "kagan2005" >}} identified the chemical engine behind Step 1: upon binding to cardiolipin, cytochrome *c* undergoes a conformational change that breaks its Met80–heme iron coordination, converting it from an electron shuttle into a **cardiolipin-specific peroxidase** (*cardiolipin oxygenase*). Using H₂O₂, this complex selectively oxidizes polyunsaturated acyl chains of cardiolipin (predominantly linoleoyl chains in L4-CL) into cardiolipin hydroperoxides *before* caspase activation. Because oxidized cardiolipin loses binding affinity for cytochrome *c* and redistributes to the OMM, peroxidation both liberates cytochrome *c* and facilitates OMM pore formation. **Boundary of the evidence:** This reveals a structural trade-off of enriching cardiac and somatic cardiolipin with oxidizable linoleic acid (L4-CL)—the same bis-allylic reactivity that enables controlled apoptotic signaling under stress creates vulnerability under chronic oxidative overload. However, these cell-free and cultured-cell experiments do not establish that higher physiological dietary LA intakes accelerate spontaneous apoptosis in healthy human tissues.

#### Cardiolipin in mitochondrial fusion and fission (L-OPA1 heterotypic fusion and Drp1 activation)

**Direct reconstituted-membrane, biochemical, and genetic evidence.** Mitochondrial morphology is continuously remodeled by opposing fusion and fission GTPases, both of which depend directly on cardiolipin. For inner-membrane fusion, Ban and colleagues {{< cite "ban2017" >}} reconstituted purified human **long-form OPA1 (L-OPA1)** in proteoliposomes and demonstrated a **heterotypic fusion mechanism**: L-OPA1 anchored on one membrane binds directly to **cardiolipin** on the opposing membrane to drive GTP-independent tethering followed by GTP-hydrolysis-dependent membrane fusion. Fusion efficiency depended critically on cardiolipin concentration and acyl-chain composition (favored by unsaturated, conical cardiolipin species), providing a quality-control filter that prevents damaged, cardiolipin-depleted mitochondria from fusing back into the healthy network.

For mitochondrial fission, Bustillo-Zabalbeitia and colleagues {{< cite "bustillozabalbeitia2014" >}} identified a **four-lysine motif** within the variable B-insert domain of **dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1)** that selectively binds cardiolipin over other anionic phospholipids, triggering Drp1 oligomerization and markedly stimulating its GTPase activity. In parallel, Macdonald and colleagues {{< cite "macdonald2014" >}} confirmed that cardiolipin promotes Drp1 assembly from dimeric equilibrium intermediates into helical oligomers that tubulate and constrict membranes for scission. Because cellular cardiolipin acyl composition responds to ambient phospholipid LA pools {{< cite "oemer2020" >}}{{< cite "schlame2017" >}}, these findings connect linoleoyl-cardiolipin geometry to both sides of mitochondrial dynamics. **What they do not prove:** Most mechanistic kinetics come from *in vitro* liposome reconstitutions and cultured cells; how much ordinary dietary shifts in LA vs. DHA alter OPA1- or Drp1-driven mitochondrial morphology *in vivo* in healthy humans remains unquantified.

#### Cardiolipin depletion and peroxidation in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury and cardiac aging

**Direct animal heart-perfusion, isolated-mitochondria, and lipid-rescue studies.** Because cardiac mitochondria rely heavily on L4-CL for supercomplex stability and high-flux oxidative phosphorylation, cardiolipin is an early casualty of ischemic and age-related oxidative stress. In isolated perfused rabbit hearts subjected to 30–45 minutes of ischemia, Lesnefsky and colleagues {{< cite "lesnefsky2001" >}} showed that **subsarcolemmal mitochondria (SSM)**—unlike interfibrillar mitochondria (IFM)—suffer a selective, early loss of cardiolipin accompanied by impaired cytochrome *c* oxidase (Complex IV) respiration, while other membrane phospholipids remain unchanged. Extending this to reperfusion in rat hearts, Paradies and colleagues {{< cite "paradies2004" >}} found that mitochondrial **Complex I activity** fell 25% during ischemia and 48% after reperfusion in lockstep with cardiolipin loss (28% and 50%, respectively), driven by ROS-induced cardiolipin peroxidation. Crucially, fusing exogenous intact cardiolipin liposomes into reperfused mitochondria completely restored Complex I activity, whereas peroxidized cardiolipin or other phospholipids failed to rescue it.

A strikingly similar lesion develops chronically with age: Petrosillo and colleagues {{< cite "petrosillo2009" >}} showed that aged rat heart mitochondria display elevated H₂O₂ generation, declining Complex I activity, lower total cardiolipin, and accumulated oxidized cardiolipin—and again, exogenous intact cardiolipin (but not oxidized cardiolipin) almost completely normalized Complex I activity. Together with evidence that the lipid peroxidation aldehyde 4-HNE directly adducts and inhibits cytochrome *c* oxidase {{< cite "chen1998" >}}, these data illustrate the **cardiolipin paradox**: heart mitochondria require linoleoyl-rich cardiolipin for peak OXPHOS efficiency (and dietary LA preserves L4-CL in hypertensive rat heart failure {{< cite "chicco2008" >}}{{< cite "mulligan2012" >}}), yet cardiolipin's polyunsaturated chains are primary targets of ischemic and age-associated ROS. What these rodent models do *not* prove is that lowering dietary LA prevents cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury or aging in humans—since depleting LA below requirement impairs L4-CL synthesis itself.

#### Mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) and calcium handling (MCU) modulation by cardiolipin and fatty acids

**Direct genetic, biochemical, and animal dietary-intervention evidence.** Cardiolipin governs both physiological mitochondrial Ca²⁺ entry and pathological Ca²⁺ overload. Using yeast reconstituted with human uniporter components plus Barth syndrome cellular and cardiac models, Ghosh and colleagues {{< cite "ghosh2020" >}} demonstrated that **cardiolipin is essential for the physical stability and channel activity of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU)**; cardiolipin deficiency caused rapid MCU turnover and blunted mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uptake (adding a calcium-signaling defect to the CoA/supercomplex defects in tafazzin deficiency {{< cite "schlame2002" >}}{{< cite "le2020" >}}).

Conversely, when cardiolipin is oxidized, it promotes lethal pore opening. Petrosillo and colleagues {{< cite "petrosillo2006" >}} showed in isolated rat heart mitochondria that micromolar **cardiolipin hydroperoxide** lowers the Ca²⁺ threshold for **mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP)** opening, triggering matrix swelling, membrane-potential collapse, and cytochrome *c* release—effects blocked by cyclosporin A and bongkrekic acid (implicating the adenine nucleotide translocase, ANT). Independently of cardiolipin peroxidation, free unesterified long-chain fatty acids (including linoleic, oleic, and palmitic acids) can also promote protonophoric uncoupling and sensitize mPTP opening in isolated liver mitochondria {{< cite "wieckowski1998" >}}. **Where dietary PUFA species diverge:** In normal and hypertrophied rat myocardium, Khairallah and colleagues {{< cite "khairallah2010" >}} demonstrated that dietary supplementation with **DHA** (replacing n-6 arachidonate/linoleate in mitochondrial phospholipids) significantly *delayed* Ca²⁺-induced mPTP opening, whereas EPA did not—showing that phospholipid unsaturation alone does not predict mPTP sensitivity and that n-6 vs. n-3 acyl chains exert structurally distinct effects on inner-membrane pore regulation. These findings prove intricate lipid control over mitochondrial Ca²⁺ thresholds in rodents and cells, but do not yet translate into validated dietary mPTP prescriptions for humans.

#### Iron-dependent PUFA-phospholipid peroxidation (`ACSL4`, `LPCAT3`, `GPX4`, `FSP1`): why AA/AdA vs. LA differentially sensitize cells

**Strong genetic, lipidomic, and chemical-biology evidence in mammalian cells and mouse models.** Dixon and colleagues {{< cite "dixon2012" >}} defined **ferroptosis** as a distinct, iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of regulated cell death driven by lethal accumulation of membrane lipid hydroperoxides upon cystine deprivation or system x_c⁻ inhibition. Yang and colleagues {{< cite "yang2014" >}} identified the selenoenzyme **glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)**—which uniquely reduces phospholipid hydroperoxides directly within intact membranes—as the central guardian against ferroptosis (cooperating *in vivo* with vitamin E in hepatocytes {{< cite "carlson2016" >}}). Parallel CRISPR and expression-cloning screens by Bersuker and colleagues {{< cite "bersuker2019" >}} and Doll and colleagues {{< cite "doll2019" >}} subsequently uncovered a second, glutathione-independent defense arm: **ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1, formerly AIFM2)**, a plasma-membrane oxidoreductase that uses NAD(P)H to regenerate reduced coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinol) as a radical-trapping antioxidant halting lipid peroxide chain propagation.

Crucially, not all polyunsaturated fatty acids sensitize membranes equally. Haploid genetic screening by Dixon and colleagues {{< cite "dixon2015" >}} and CRISPR screening by Doll and colleagues {{< cite "doll2017" >}} identified **ACSL4** (which preferentially activates long-chain n-6 PUFAs—especially arachidonic acid [AA, 20:4n-6] and adrenic acid [AdA, 22:4n-6]—into acyl-CoAs) and **LPCAT3** (which inserts them into membrane phospholipids) as essential executors of ferroptosis; deleting *Acsl4* renders cells strongly ferroptosis-resistant even when GPX4 is lost. Using quantitative redox lipidomics, Kagan and colleagues {{< cite "kagan2017" >}} demonstrated that **doubly- and triply-oxygenated phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) containing AA and AdA**—rather than parent linoleic acid (18:2n-6)—constitute the primary execution signals of ferroptosis, while Yang and colleagues {{< cite "yang2016" >}} showed that peroxidation proceeds via hydrogen abstraction at bis-allylic methylene carbons (blocked by deuterating bis-allylic sites) and scales sharply with bis-allylic site count (LA has **1** bis-allylic CH₂ group, whereas AA and AdA have **3**). **What this proves and does not prove:** It proves that n-6 PUFA-PE peroxidation is a bona fide regulated cell-death mechanism relevant to ischemia-reperfusion, neurodegeneration, and cancer biology. However, because human tissue AA levels plateau across wide Western dietary LA ranges {{< cite "rett2011" >}} and cells are guarded by both GPX4 and FSP1 plus vitamin E, these cellular pathways do *not* prove that ordinary variations in dietary LA trigger spontaneous ferroptosis in healthy, antioxidant-replete human tissues.

#### Monounsaturated fatty acid (oleic acid via `ACSL3`) suppression of ferroptosis and the PUFA–MUFA membrane-packing trade-off

**Direct cellular genetic and lipidomic evidence.** Because monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) such as oleic acid (18:1n-9) contain **zero bis-allylic carbons**, they cannot propagate lipid peroxidation chains. Magtanong and colleagues {{< cite "magtanong2019" >}} demonstrated that supplementing mammalian cells with exogenous MUFAs (oleic or palmitoleic acid) potently suppresses ferroptosis induced by erastin or GPX4 inhibition—even in cells with intact ACSL4. This protection depended specifically on **acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 3 (ACSL3)**, which activates MUFAs for esterification into plasma-membrane phospholipids, thereby competitively displacing oxidizable AA/AdA-containing phospholipids from the membrane (distinct from ACSL3-independent MUFA protection against saturated-fat-induced lipotoxic apoptosis, and independent of lipid-droplet sequestration).

**Boundary and tension in the literature:** Paired with the ACSL4/PE lipidomic studies {{< cite "doll2017" >}}{{< cite "kagan2017" >}}, Magtanong and colleagues {{< cite "magtanong2019" >}} establish a direct enzymatic tug-of-war at membrane remodeling: **ACSL4/LPCAT3** loads oxidizable n-6 LC-PUFAs (pro-ferroptotic) while **ACSL3** loads peroxidation-resistant MUFAs (anti-ferroptotic). What remains unresolved is the extent to which dietary oleic-to-linoleic ratios in humans meaningfully shift the ACSL3/ACSL4 balance inside vulnerable organs (e.g., ischemic heart, kidney tubules, or tumors) *in vivo*, where systemic plasma fatty acid pools are heavily buffered by endogenous lipogenesis and adipose turnover.

### 3. Lipid Transport, Lipoproteins, Hepatic Handling, Adipose Turnover, and Clinical Conflicts

#### Cholesteryl linoleate and LCAT esterification in plasma lipid transport

**Foundational human biochemical evidence.** Glomset {{< cite "glomset1968" >}} elucidated the central role of plasma **lecithin:cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT)** in converting free cholesterol on nascent HDL particles into hydrophobic cholesteryl esters by transferring a fatty acyl chain from the *sn*-2 position of phosphatidylcholine. This esterification traps cholesterol inside the lipoprotein core, maintaining the concentration gradient needed for cholesterol efflux from peripheral tissues (reverse cholesterol transport).

Testing purified substrate specificity, Sgoutas {{< cite "sgoutas1972" >}} demonstrated that human plasma LCAT exhibits its highest acyl-transfer activity toward **linoleic acid (18:2)** at the *sn*-2 position of phosphatidylcholine—far exceeding saturated or monounsaturated acyl chains. As a result, cholesteryl linoleate accounts for roughly half of all cholesteryl esters circulating in human plasma. This biochemistry explains why Sinclair {{< cite "sinclair1956" >}} noticed altered cholesteryl ester composition in deficiency states and why plasma cholesteryl-ester LA serves as a responsive short-to-medium-term biomarker of dietary LA intake. However, preferential LCAT esterification with LA reflects normal lipoprotein physiology and does not by itself determine whether cholesteryl linoleate inside arterial intimal LDL is protective or prone to oxidative modification in atherosclerosis.

#### Dietary linoleic acid lowering of LDL-cholesterol via hepatic LDL receptor regulation

**Strong human RCT evidence for LDL-C lowering; direct mechanistic evidence in animal models.** In a landmark meta-analysis of 60 controlled human feeding trials, Mensink and colleagues {{< cite "mensink2003" >}} quantified that isocaloric replacement of carbohydrates or saturated fatty acids with *cis*-polyunsaturated fatty acids (predominantly linoleic acid) lowers serum LDL-cholesterol, lowers the total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio, and reduces apolipoprotein B.

Mechanistic work in hamsters—a species with human-like hepatic LDL transport—explains much of this effect. Spady and Dietschy {{< cite "spady1985" >}} showed that dietary saturated triacylglycerols (in the presence of cholesterol) severely suppressed hepatic receptor-mediated LDL clearance (by up to ~90%), whereas polyunsaturated triacylglycerols did not. Follow-up studies by Woollett, Spady, and Dietschy {{< cite "woollett1992" >}} and Horton, Cuthbert, and Spady {{< cite "horton1993" >}} demonstrated that specific saturated fatty acids (notably 14:0 and 16:0) downregulate hepatic LDL receptor protein and mRNA levels—likely via shifts in intracellular hepatic free-cholesterol regulatory pools—whereas unsaturated fats including linoleic acid maintain higher hepatic LDLR mRNA, protein abundance, and receptor-mediated LDL clearance from plasma. While the LDL-lowering effect of replacing SFA with LA in humans is among the most reproducible findings in nutritional science, whether LDL-C reduction achieved specifically via concentrated LA oils uniformly reduces cardiovascular mortality remains contested (see Note 14 below).

#### Hepatic VLDL, SREBP-1c suppression of *de novo* lipogenesis, and human liver-fat trials vs. rodent models

**Direct molecular and human RCT evidence, with a clear conflict against rodent soybean-oil models.** At the transcriptional level, Xu and colleagues {{< cite "xu1999" >}} demonstrated that dietary polyunsaturated fatty acids (including linoleic acid and marine n-3 PUFAs) suppress hepatic **sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1 (SREBP-1)**—specifically lowering nuclear SREBP-1c abundance and coordinately downregulating lipogenic genes such as fatty acid synthase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, and stearoyl-CoA desaturase, thereby reducing hepatic *de novo* lipogenesis and VLDL-triglyceride output.

Two controlled human trials tested whether this translates to hepatic steatosis. In a 10-week isocaloric randomized trial in abdominally obese adults, Bjermo and colleagues {{< cite "bjermo2012" >}} found that an n-6 PUFA-rich diet (sunflower oil/margarine) reduced MRI-measured liver fat and lowered PCSK9 and total/LDL cholesterol compared with a butter-rich saturated fat diet, without increasing systemic inflammatory or oxidative stress markers. In a 7-week overfeeding trial (+750 kcal/day from muffins) in healthy young adults, Rosqvist and colleagues {{< cite "rosqvist2014" >}} found that although both groups gained similar weight (~1.6 kg), palm-oil (SFA) overfeeding produced significantly greater liver fat and visceral fat accumulation, whereas sunflower-oil (LA) overfeeding produced roughly threefold greater lean-mass gain and less hepatic lipid storage.

**Where the literature conflicts:** These 7-to-10-week human trials stand in tension with rodent studies such as Deol and colleagues {{< cite "deol2015" >}}, in which C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat soybean/coconut oil blend developed worse fatty liver and metabolic dysfunction than mice fed predominantly coconut oil. Potential reasons include species differences in hepatic PUFA metabolism, background diet composition (purified rodent chows vs. whole-food human diets), oil formulation (soybean vs. sunflower oil; heated vs. unheated), and study duration. Neither short-term human MRI trials nor mouse feeding studies settle long-term multi-year effects of high LA intakes on human steatohepatitis (MASH).

#### Adipose tissue storage, multi-year turnover, secular trends, and selective mobilization

**Direct human longitudinal, historical, and mechanistic lipolysis evidence.** Building on radiocarbon evidence that human adipose triglycerides turn over roughly every 1.6 years on average {{< cite "arner2011" >}}, direct dietary-intervention data from Dayton and colleagues {{< cite "dayton1966" >}} tracked elderly institutionalized men switching to a high-LA vegetable-oil diet over five years. Adipose linoleic acid rose exponentially from ~11% to ~32% of total fatty acids with an equilibration **half-time of approximately 680 days** (~1.9 years). Because humans cannot synthesize LA *de novo*, adipose tissue LA therefore serves as a high-fidelity integrated biomarker of dietary LA intake over 1–3 years.

Compiling U.S. adult adipose biopsy studies published between 1959 and 2008, Guyenet and Carlson {{< cite "guyenet2015" >}} documented a 136% secular increase in subcutaneous adipose linoleic acid—from an average of ~9.1% to ~21.5% of total adipose fatty acids—closely tracking U.S. soybean oil disappearance (*R*² = 0.81). During fasting or lipolysis, fatty acids are not released in strict proportion to their stored abundance: Raclot and Groscolas {{< cite "raclot1993" >}} demonstrated in rat adipocytes that mobilization increases with unsaturation and decreases with chain length, meaning LA is mobilized somewhat more readily than long-chain saturated fats (palmitate, stearate) though less readily than EPA or ALA. Even with modest preferential mobilization, however, large adipose depots buffer tissue LA over years—which is why acute clinical EFAD recovery (weeks) and full depot remodeling (years) operate on completely different timescales.

#### The human clinical conflict: cholesterol lowering vs. mortality in recovered RCTs vs. biomarker cohorts

**Strong conflicting lines of human evidence that cannot be dismissed by either side.** The central controversy surrounding dietary linoleic acid today rests on a stark divergence between re-analyzed historical substitution trials and modern observational and pooled RCT evidence.

**Recovered LA-specific RCTs:** Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2013" >}} recovered unpublished mortality data from the Sydney Diet Heart Study (SDHS, 1966–1973; 458 men with prior coronary events randomized to replace saturated fat with safflower oil and safflower-oil margarine). Despite a ~13% drop in serum total cholesterol, the intervention group had higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.00–2.64), cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.03–2.80), and CHD mortality (HR 1.74, 95% CI 1.04–2.92). Similarly, Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2016" >}} recovered unpublished data from the Minnesota Coronary Experiment (MCE, 1968–1973; 9,423 institutional participants randomized to corn oil/margarine vs. saturated fat). Serum cholesterol fell by 13.8% in the corn-oil group, yet there was no mortality benefit; in covariate-adjusted post-hoc analyses, each 30 mg/dL (0.78 mmol/L) reduction in serum cholesterol was associated with a 22% *higher* risk of death (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.14–1.32), driven particularly by participants aged ≥65. *Key limitations of these recovered trials:* both interventions included 1960s–1970s margarines likely containing industrial *trans* fats (a major confounder debated intensely by both sides); MCE had short median follow-up (~1 year) due to institutional discharge/turnover; and post-hoc cholesterol-lowering vs. mortality associations in elderly institutionalized patients are vulnerable to reverse causation (weight loss and underlying illness lower cholesterol while raising mortality).

**Prospective biomarker cohorts and broader meta-analyses:** Pulling in the opposite direction, Marklund and colleagues {{< cite "marklund2019" >}} pooled individual-level data from 30 prospective cohorts across 13 countries (68,659 participants) measuring circulating or adipose LA biomarkers—avoiding dietary-recall error. Higher LA biomarker levels were associated with *lower* risk of total CVD (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.88–0.99 per interquintile range), cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.70–0.85), and ischemic stroke (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79–0.98), with no signal of harm. Earlier, Mozaffarian, Micha, and Wallace {{< cite "mozaffarian2010" >}} pooled 8 RCTs (13,614 participants) replacing SFA with mixed PUFA oils (containing both LA and ALA) and found a 19% reduction in CHD events (RR 0.81). More recently, a Cochrane systematic review by Hooper and colleagues {{< cite "hooper2018" >}} pooling 19 RCTs (6,461 participants) specifically examining increased omega-6 fat found lower serum total cholesterol, little or no effect on all-cause mortality (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.88–1.12; low-certainty evidence), and a possible small reduction in myocardial infarction (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.76–1.02; low-certainty evidence). *Key limitations of these contrasting data:* observational biomarker pools cannot fully exclude residual confounding or metabolic reverse causation (e.g., insulin resistance and inflammation altering LA turnover/desaturation), while broader PUFA trial meta-analyses mix LA-only oils with ALA-containing oils and suffer from heterogeneous trial quality.

**Boundary of the evidence:** Taken together, these studies prove that **lowering LDL-cholesterol via dietary LA does not automatically guarantee lower all-cause mortality** across all formulations and populations, while also showing **no consistent epidemiological or pooled-RCT proof that ordinary dietary LA intakes are uniquely cardiotoxic**. Separating pure unheated LA from *trans*-fat-bearing margarines, oxidized frying oils, and omega-3 deficiency remains an unresolved empirical problem.

#### The oxidized LDL hypothesis: cholesteryl linoleate oxidation (9-/13-HODE) in human plaques vs. why vitamin E RCTs failed

**Direct human biochemical, feeding-trial, plaque-pathology, and mega-RCT evidence with a classic translational conflict.** Steinberg and colleagues {{< cite "steinberg1989" >}} formulated the **oxidative modification hypothesis of atherosclerosis**: native LDL does not trigger uncontrolled macrophage cholesterol loading, whereas oxidatively modified LDL is rapidly internalized via scavenger receptors to generate arterial foam cells, while also provoking endothelial injury and inflammation. Quantifying LDL composition and peroxidation kinetics, Esterbauer and colleagues {{< cite "esterbauer1992" >}} documented that **linoleic acid**—predominantly packaged as **cholesteryl linoleate** via LCAT {{< cite "glomset1968" >}}{{< cite "sgoutas1972" >}}—accounts for ~80–85% of the polyunsaturated fatty acids inside a human LDL particle (~1,100–1,300 LA molecules per particle), making cholesteryl linoleate the quantitatively dominant substrate of LDL lipid peroxidation and downstream aldehydes.

Direct human feeding and lesion studies confirmed that dietary LA modulates *ex vivo* LDL oxidizability and that oxidized linoleate accumulates in human plaques. In a controlled human feeding trial, Reaven and colleagues {{< cite "reaven1993" >}} showed that LDL isolated from mildly hypercholesterolemic adults on a linoleate-rich diet was significantly more susceptible to copper- and cell-mediated oxidation and macrophage degradation than LDL from an oleate-rich diet, with LDL LA percentage strongly correlating with conjugated-diene formation (consistent with later clinical evidence that lowering dietary LA reduces circulating HODEs {{< cite "ramsden2012" >}}). In excised human atherosclerotic plaques, Kühn and colleagues {{< cite "kuhn1992" >}} identified esterified **9-HODE and 13-HODE** (hydroxyoctadecadienoic acids derived specifically from linoleate) as the predominant oxygenated lipids, and Folcik and colleagues {{< cite "folcik1995" >}} demonstrated chiral enrichment of *13(S)-HODE* in early/developing human lesions—proving that enzymatic **15-lipoxygenase** activity contributes alongside non-enzymatic free-radical oxidation (linking also to HODE-driven macrophage PPARγ/CD36 activation {{< cite "nagy1998" >}}).

**Why vitamin E / antioxidant RCTs failed—and where the conflict stands:** Despite compelling plaque chemistry, large randomized placebo-controlled trials of vitamin E failed to reduce cardiovascular events. In the **HOPE trial** (9,541 high-risk patients followed for 4.5 years), Yusuf and colleagues {{< cite "yusuf2000" >}} found no reduction in MI, stroke, or cardiovascular death with 400 IU/day natural vitamin E (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.95–1.16). A landmark meta-analysis by Vivekananthan and colleagues {{< cite "vivekananthan2003" >}} pooling 7 large vitamin E RCTs (81,788 patients) confirmed zero benefit on all-cause mortality or cardiovascular death (while β-carotene slightly increased mortality). Mechanistic analyses identified three concrete reasons why vitamin E trials did not cleanly refute or validate LDL oxidation:
1. **Endogenous plaque vitamin E is not depleted:** Suarna, Stocker, and colleagues {{< cite "suarna1995" >}} showed that human atherosclerotic plaques already contain abundant, unexhausted α-tocopherol and ascorbate *coexisting* with oxidized cholesteryl linoleate.
2. **Tocopherol-mediated peroxidation (TMP):** Bowry and Stocker {{< cite "bowry1993" >}} proved chemically that within the confined core of an LDL particle lacking sufficient aqueous co-antioxidants (like ascorbate or ubiquinol-10), the α-tocopheroxyl radical can paradoxically act as a **chain-transfer pro-oxidant**, propagating peroxidation of cholesteryl linoleate.
3. **Enzymatic pathways & disease stage:** Steinberg and Witztum {{< cite "steinberg2002" >}} emphasized that vitamin E poorly blocks enzymatic lipoxygenase/myeloperoxidase oxidation inside the arterial intima and that mega-trials enrolled older patients with decades-old fibrotic plaques rather than testing early lesion initiation.

**Boundary of the evidence:** Cholesteryl linoleate is indisputably the main substrate of LDL oxidation in human lesions, and linoleate-enriched LDL oxidizes faster *ex vivo* than oleate-enriched LDL. However, *ex vivo* copper-oxidation lag times do not automatically translate into clinical cardiovascular events (as seen in the unresolved RCT vs. cohort divergence in Pass 1 Note 14), and failed vitamin E trials prove that systemic α-tocopherol supplementation is an ineffective clinical probe of arterial lipoprotein oxidation.

#### Intestinal absorption, chylomicron assembly, and lymphatic vs. portal transport of linoleic acid

**Direct physiological cannulation studies and mechanistic reviews.** Following pancreatic lipase hydrolysis of dietary triacylglycerols into 2-monoacylglycerols and free fatty acids, linoleic acid enters enterocytes across the brush border, binds intestinal fatty acid-binding proteins, and is re-esterified into triacylglycerols at the endoplasmic reticulum for packaging with apoB-48, phospholipids, and cholesteryl esters into nascent **chylomicrons**, which undergo Golgi processing and exocytosis into intestinal lacteals {{< cite "tso1986" >}}. Because chylomicrons bypass the liver first-pass via the thoracic duct into systemic venous circulation, dietary LA is delivered initially to peripheral capillary beds (adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, heart, and mammary gland) via lipoprotein lipase before chylomicron remnants reach the liver.

Quantifying lymphatic vs. portal partitioning in mesenteric-lymph- and portal-vein-cannulated rats, McDonald and colleagues {{< cite "mcdonald1980" >}} confirmed that long-chain fatty acids including **linoleic acid** are transported predominantly via the lymphatic route in chylomicrons—yet demonstrated that portal venous transport is not strictly zero: unsaturated long-chain fatty acids (especially LA and oleate) showed greater portal absorption than saturated stearate or palmitate when luminal lipid load was low or lymphatic chylomicron flow was impaired. Conversely, severe EFA deficiency itself damages enterocyte membrane fluidity and impairs both intraluminal micellar processing and intracellular chylomicron assembly and secretion {{< cite "clark1973" >}}{{< cite "levy1992" >}}. **What this establishes:** Dietary LA is both a major cargo of chylomicron lymphatic delivery and a structural requirement of the enterocyte membranes that assemble chylomicrons; acute postprandial lipemia therefore routes absorbed LA directly to peripheral tissues before hepatic remodeling.

#### HDL reverse cholesterol transport, macrophage ABCA1 degradation by unsaturated fatty acids, and CETP cholesteryl-ester remodeling

**Strong mechanistic biochemistry paired with landmark human clinical trials.** Once LCAT preferentially transfers *sn*-2 linoleic acid from phosphatidylcholine onto free cholesterol on HDL {{< cite "glomset1968" >}}{{< cite "sgoutas1972" >}}, **cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP)** mediates neutral-lipid heteroexchange in human plasma: moving hydrophobic cholesteryl esters (predominantly **cholesteryl linoleate**) from HDL to VLDL, IDL, and LDL in exchange for triacylglycerols {{< cite "tall1993" >}}{{< cite "barter2003" >}}. Thus, CETP is a primary conduit by which linoleate-rich cholesteryl esters formed on HDL populate circulating LDL particles, routing cholesterol either to hepatic LDL receptors for clearance or—when LDL is elevated—into the arterial wall.

At the cellular efflux step initiating reverse cholesterol transport, Wang and Oram {{< cite "wang2002" >}} made an unexpected mechanistic finding: exposing cultured macrophages to physiological-to-elevated concentrations of **unsaturated free fatty acids** (including **linoleic acid** and oleic acid)—but *not* saturated palmitate or stearate—markedly inhibited **ABCA1-mediated cholesterol and phospholipid efflux** to apoA-I by accelerating post-translational **degradation of ABCA1 protein** (without changing *ABCA1* mRNA). This raised concern that elevated circulating unsaturated NEFAs (e.g., in insulin resistance) could impair macrophage cholesterol export.

**Where *in vitro* efflux models diverge from human trials:** In human controlled feeding meta-analyses {{< cite "mensink2003" >}} and RCTs {{< cite "vafeiadou2015" >}}, replacing dietary saturated fat with LA-rich PUFA produces minimal change (or a slight drop) in absolute HDL-C while substantially lowering LDL-C and improving the **total:HDL cholesterol ratio**—without evidence that dietary LA impairs net human reverse cholesterol transport. Likewise, pharmacological CETP inhibition demonstrated that simply trapping cholesteryl esters inside HDL does not reliably prevent atherosclerosis: in the **ILLUMINATE trial** (15,067 high-risk patients), Barter and colleagues {{< cite "barter2007" >}} stopped **torcetrapib** early because despite a +72.1% surge in HDL-C and −24.9% drop in LDL-C, cardiovascular events (HR 1.25) and all-cause mortality (HR 1.58) *increased* (driven substantially by off-target aldosterone/blood-pressure toxicity). A decade later, the **REVEAL trial** (30,449 patients on intensive atorvastatin) by Bowman and colleagues {{< cite "bowman2017" >}} showed that the clean CETP inhibitor **anacetrapib** produced a modest 9% relative reduction in major coronary events (rate ratio 0.91, 95% CI 0.85–0.97)—an effect size quantitatively explained by its **non-HDL/apoB-cholesterol lowering** rather than its >100% elevation in HDL-C. **Boundary of the evidence:** CETP tightly links HDL cholesteryl-linoleate synthesis to LDL core composition, and unsaturated NEFAs destabilize macrophage ABCA1 *in vitro*, but human clinical outcomes track apoB particle burden far more reliably than static HDL-C concentration.

#### Fasting lipolytic flux and ketogenesis: linoleic acid as a ketogenic substrate, selective mobilization, and PPARα regulation

**Direct human arteriovenous flux and RCT evidence paired with mouse knockout genetics.** During an overnight fast, adipose lipolysis supplies non-esterified fatty acids (NEFAs) to the liver for β-oxidation and ketogenesis. Extending rodent adipocyte selectivity studies {{< cite "raclot1993" >}} directly into living humans, Halliwell, Fielding, Frayn, and colleagues {{< cite "halliwell1996" >}} sampled arterialized blood and subcutaneous abdominal adipose venous drainage alongside fat biopsies in healthy adults over a 6-hour post-overnight fast. Relative mobilization from human adipose triacylglycerols **increased with unsaturation and decreased with chain length**, confirming *in vivo* that linoleic acid is mobilized somewhat more readily than palmitate or stearate—consistent with tracer evidence that LA is oxidized faster than long-chain saturates {{< cite "leyton1987" >}}{{< cite "delany2000" >}}, alongside substantial carbon recycling of LA-derived acetyl-CoA during growth {{< cite "cunnane2003" >}}.

Once delivered to the liver, PUFAs including LA act not only as β-oxidative substrates yielding acetyl-CoA but also as endogenous ligands for **PPARα** {{< cite "forman1997" >}}, which Kersten and colleagues {{< cite "kersten1999" >}} proved in *Ppara*-null mice is the master transcriptional switch required for the adaptive fasting response (upregulating mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation and ketogenic enzymes; *Ppara*-null mice suffer severe fasting hypoketonemia, hypoglycemia, hypothermia, and hepatic steatosis). Testing whether dietary fat saturation alters human ketogenesis directly, Fuehrlein and colleagues {{< cite "fuehrlein2004" >}} randomized 20 healthy adults for 5 days to weight-maintaining ketogenic diets (70% fat, 15% carbohydrate, 15% protein) enriched in either **polyunsaturated fat** (60% PUFA, 15% SFA) or **saturated fat** (60% SFA, 15% PUFA). Serum **β-hydroxybutyrate rose markedly more on the PUFA ketogenic diet** (+8.4 mg/dL, *P* = 0.0004) than on the SFA ketogenic diet (+3.1 mg/dL, *P* = 0.07), accompanied by improved insulin sensitivity and lower fasting glucose without the LDL-cholesterol spike seen in the SFA arm.

**Key mechanistic nuance:** Although PUFA-rich diets enhance acute ketosis in humans, complete mitochondrial oxidation of LA is *not* strictly required to sustain fasting ketogenesis. As shown in *Decr1*-null mice lacking mitochondrial 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase {{< cite "miinalainen2009" >}}, blocking unsaturated fatty-acid β-oxidation produces fasting hypoglycemia and cold intolerance but leaves **ketogenesis unimpaired**, because saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids mobilized simultaneously from adipose tissue supply sufficient hepatic acetyl-CoA. **What these studies prove and do not prove:** They prove that LA is actively mobilized during human fasting, acts as an efficient ketogenic fuel and PPARα agonist, and yields stronger short-term ketosis than SFA in a 5-day human trial; they do *not* establish long-term safety or superiority of very-high-PUFA ketogenic diets over months or years.

#### Metabolic context and fatty liver

**Support for model dependence; not for dismissing adverse findings.** Wang and colleagues {{< cite "wang2006" >}} found different patterns of hepatic elongase/desaturase expression in chemically induced diabetes, diet-induced glucose intolerance, and genetically obese mice. The metabolic background is therefore a meaningful experimental variable.

However, Deol and colleagues {{< cite "deol2015" >}} reported fatty liver and metabolic disease in C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat soybean/coconut-oil mixture compared with a predominantly coconut-oil diet. This was not simply a comparison in mice already made diabetic. Neither study establishes that pre-existing EFA dysfunction explains away the liver injury, nor that all PUFAs have the same effects. The oil mixture, dose, background diet, species, and measured outcome all need to travel with the conclusion. The cholesterol analogy is an interpretation, not a result of these experiments.

#### Recovery is not the same as emptying fat stores

**Repletion evidence supports the first idea, but not the inference about turnover.** Goodgame and colleagues {{< cite "goodgame1978" >}} reported rapid correction of biochemical abnormalities after intravenous fat was supplied. Holman, Johnson, and Hatch {{< cite "holman1982" >}} described disappearance of neurological symptoms after an ALA-containing emulsion corrected a child's omega-3 deficiency. Neither report supplies a universal recovery time for every symptom.

Arner and colleagues {{< cite "arner2011" >}} used radiocarbon measurements to study human adipose triglycerides and found turnover on a much longer timescale—about six renewals during a fat cell's average ten-year life. This was not a PUFA-specific depletion experiment. Correcting a limiting supply and replacing stored fat are different processes; fast recovery cannot by itself establish how long either total fat or a particular PUFA remains in adipose tissue.

## Part II: Barriers, Organ Systems, Gut Microbiome, Hematology, and Musculoskeletal Tissue

### 4. Epidermal Water Barrier, Skin, Sebum, Hair, and Wound Healing

#### Linoleic acid's direct structural role in the epidermal water barrier (independent of arachidonic acid and prostaglandins)

**Direct animal and ultrastructural evidence for a specific structural requirement.** Burr and Burr {{< cite "burr1929" >}} {{< cite "burr1930" >}} established that rigid fat exclusion in rats produces scaly skin, caudal necrosis, impaired growth, and elevated water consumption, and identified linoleic acid (LA) as curative. Later work (historically crystallized by Basnayake and Sinclair, 1956, in *Biochemical Problems of Lipids*) showed that cutaneous barrier breakdown drives massive transepidermal water loss (TEWL). Elias and Brown {{< cite "elias1978" >}} linked that physiological leak to epidermal ultrastructure: EFA-deficient mice and rats showed defective lamellar body lipid deposition and disrupted intercellular lipid sheets in the stratum corneum.

Crucially, this barrier role does not depend solely on converting LA into arachidonic acid (AA) or prostaglandins. Houtsmuller and van der Beek {{< cite "houtsmuller1981" >}} demonstrated that topical columbinic acid (18:3 Δ5t,9c,12c—a structural analog that cannot serve as a prostaglandin precursor) normalized TEWL and skin scaling in EFA-deficient rats. Hansen and Jensen {{< cite "hansen1985" >}} fed purified fatty-acid esters to EFA-deficient rats and showed that barrier restoration tracked specifically with esterification of linoleate (or structurally compatible cis-polyunsaturated chains such as columbinate) into epidermal **acylglucosylceramide** and **acylceramide** (Ceramide EOS), whereas oleate failed to restore the water barrier. This separates LA's direct architectural role in stratum corneum lipids from downstream eicosanoid signaling. It does not imply that supra-physiological dietary LA further tightens an already replete human skin barrier.

#### The enzymatic assembly line from linoleic acid to the corneocyte lipid envelope (ELOVL4, PNPLA1, 12R-LOX/eLOX3, SDR9C7) and human congenital ichthyoses

**Direct genetic and biochemical proof in mice and humans, updating the 2011 model.** The existing annotation cites Zheng and colleagues {{< cite "zheng2011" >}}, who identified 12R-lipoxygenase (12R-LOX) and epidermal lipoxygenase-3 (eLOX3) as linoleate-oxidizing enzymes required for corneocyte lipid envelope construction. Subsequent work mapped both the upstream synthesis and the downstream covalent-binding step:

- **Ultra-long-chain scaffold (`ELOVL4`):** Vasireddy and colleagues {{< cite "vasireddy2007" >}} showed that mice lacking functional ELOVL4 cannot synthesize very-long-chain fatty acids (≥C28) or epidermal ω-O-acylceramides and die within hours of birth from catastrophic epidermal water loss.
- **Linoleate transacylation (`PNPLA1`):** Two back-to-back studies—Ohno and colleagues {{< cite "ohno2017" >}} and Hirabayashi and colleagues {{< cite "hirabayashi2017" >}}—identified PNPLA1 as the transacylase that specifically transfers linoleic acid from triglycerides onto ω-hydroxyceramides to produce ω-O-acylceramide (Ceramide EOS). *Pnpla1*-deficient mice lose acylceramides and suffer lethal neonatal barrier failure.
- **Covalent protein attachment (`SDR9C7`):** Takeichi and colleagues {{< cite "takeichi2020" >}} refined the mechanism proposed in {{< cite "zheng2011" >}}. After 12R-LOX and eLOX3 oxidize the esterified linoleate moiety on acylceramide into a 9,10-trans-epoxy-11E-13-alcohol, the dehydrogenase **SDR9C7** oxidizes that alcohol into a reactive **epoxy-enone** intermediate capable of covalently coupling the lipid to corneocyte envelope proteins. Both *Sdr9c7*-null mice and human patients with *SDR9C7* mutations lacked covalently bound ceramides on the corneocyte envelope.

**Human genetic proof (ARCI).** Human autosomal recessive congenital ichthyosis (ARCI) confirms this pathway is indispensable in people, not merely rodents. Jobard and colleagues {{< cite "jobard2002" >}} mapped non-bullous congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma to loss-of-function mutations in *ALOX12B* (12R-LOX) and *ALOXE3* (eLOX3), and Grall and colleagues {{< cite "grall2012" >}} identified *PNPLA1* mutations as a cause of ARCI in both golden retriever dogs and humans. Together with *SDR9C7* mutations {{< cite "takeichi2020" >}}, these disorders prove that enzymatic processing of esterified linoleic acid is required for normal human stratum corneum cohesion. They do not establish that acquired skin dryness or common dermatoses are caused by dietary LA deficiency.

#### Atopic dermatitis: altered stratum corneum acylceramides vs. negative oral supplementation trials

**Structural association in human skin; clinical conflict with oral trials.** Ishikawa and colleagues {{< cite "ishikawa2010" >}} profiled stratum corneum ceramides in patients with atopic dermatitis (AD) and documented altered ceramide class distribution—including reduced long-chain acylceramide species (EOS)—correlated with impaired barrier function and elevated TEWL.

However, a biochemical defect in diseased skin does not automatically yield to oral precursor supplementation. In a Cochrane systematic review of 27 randomized controlled trials involving 1,596 adults and children, Bamford and colleagues {{< cite "bamford2013" >}} found no clinically meaningful benefit of oral evening primrose oil or borage oil (sources of LA and γ-linolenic acid, GLA) over placebo for global eczema symptoms, clinical signs, or quality of life. These findings illustrate a recurring principle across these notes: local barrier-lipid remodeling in inflammatory skin disease cannot be equated with systemic dietary EFA deficiency, and oral LA/GLA supplementation has not been shown to resolve AD.

#### Sebaceous gland composition, follicular linoleate dilution, and alopecia in deficiency

**Human observational and clinical evidence, with mechanistic limits.** Downing and colleagues {{< cite "downing1986" >}} proposed the sebum-dilution hypothesis for acne: people with acne typically have normal systemic EFA status, yet their sebum exhibits unusually low linoleic acid concentrations because elevated sebaceous lipogenesis dilutes the fixed amount of linoleate present in early sebocytes. They proposed that linoleate-poor sebum induces a localized follicular EFA deficiency, promoting follicular hyperkeratosis and comedogenesis. While this hypothesis explains the inverse correlation between sebum excretion rate and sebum linoleate fraction, it does not prove that dietary LA supplementation cures acne or that follicular linoleate dilution acts alone without androgen, microbial, and inflammatory drivers.

For cutaneous deficiency and hair loss, clinical reports in fat-free parenteral nutrition show that human skin and hair respond directly to linoleate repletion—even topically. Press, Hartop, and Prottey {{< cite "press1974" >}} corrected biochemical and cutaneous EFA deficiency in adults via daily cutaneous application of linoleic-acid-rich sunflower-seed oil. Skolnik, Eaglstein, and Ziboh {{< cite "skolnik1977" >}} reported a 19-year-old man on fat-free parenteral hyperalimentation who developed scaly scalp dermatitis, alopecia, and hair depigmentation alongside an elevated triene:tetraene ratio; topical LA / safflower oil reversed both the biochemical indices and the cutaneous/hair manifestations. Unlike multi-nutrient malnutrition cases {{< cite "kim2005" >}}, parenteral-deficiency reports link LA repletion more tightly to hair and skin recovery, though single-case and small-series reports cannot generalize to common androgenetic or telogen hair loss in EFA-replete adults.

#### PPARα, PPARβ/δ, and PPARγ activation: linking epidermal fatty acids and oxylipins to differentiation and lamellar body secretion

**Direct animal, organ-culture, and knockout evidence, complementing the structural acylceramide pathway (`hansen1985`, `ohno2017`, `takeichi2020`).** Linoleic acid and its lipoxygenase/oxidized derivatives do not serve merely as passive building blocks for stratum corneum acylceramides; free polyunsaturated fatty acids and oxidized LA metabolites (such as 9- and 13-HODE) are endogenous ligands for peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (`PPARα`, `PPARβ/δ`, and `PPARγ`) {{< cite "forman1997" >}} {{< cite "nagy1998" >}}. In the epidermis, Mao-Qiang, Elias, and Feingold {{< cite "maoqiang1993" >}} first established that blocking epidermal fatty-acid synthesis with TOFA impaired permeability barrier recovery and disrupted lamellar body lipid organization, confirming that bulk and essential fatty acids are required for secretory organelle assembly.

Subsequent UCSF studies demonstrated that all three PPAR isoforms actively orchestrate epidermal maturation and barrier repair:
- **PPARα:** Hanley and colleagues {{< cite "hanley1998" >}} showed that activators of PPARα—including exogenous fatty acids such as linoleic acid and synthetic agonists—stimulated keratinocyte differentiation markers (involucrin, transglutaminase-1) and accelerated fetal and postnatal stratum corneum formation.
- **PPARβ/δ:** Schmuth and colleagues {{< cite "schmuth2004" >}} demonstrated that PPARβ/δ activation drives terminal keratinocyte differentiation, promotes lipid accumulation, and accelerates barrier recovery after acute disruption in mice.
- **PPARγ:** Mao-Qiang and colleagues {{< cite "maoqiang2004" >}} proved that PPARγ activators likewise stimulate keratinocyte differentiation and barrier homeostasis, with anti-proliferative effects in mouse epidermis.
- **Lamellar body secretion & processing:** Man and colleagues {{< cite "man2006" >}} resolved the cellular mechanism across PPAR and LXR activators, showing that liposensor activation accelerates barrier recovery by simultaneously stimulating epidermal lipid synthesis, increasing **lamellar body secretion**, and upregulating extracellular post-secretory lipid-processing enzymes (such as β-glucocerebrosidase).

**Evidence boundaries:** These experiments prove that fatty-acid/oxylipin-responsive PPAR signaling integrates lipid availability with lamellar-body exocytosis and cornified-envelope maturation in rodents and cultured human keratinocytes. However, most mechanistic proofs relied on pharmacological PPAR agonists applied topically alongside fatty-acid comparisons; they do not prove that raising oral dietary LA above adequate baselines supercharges human epidermal PPAR signaling or cures barrier dermatoses.

#### Psoriasis & hyperproliferative skin: 12R-LOX, 13-HODE depletion, and stratum corneum ceramide remodeling

**Direct biochemical and human-tissue analyses; causal primacy remains upstream of lipids.** Psoriatic plaques exhibit both epidermal hyperproliferation and severe barrier breakdown—a phenotype accompanied by striking shifts in epidermal LA/AA lipoxygenase metabolism and ceramide structure:
- **12R-lipoxygenase upregulation:** Boeglin, Kim, and Brash {{< cite "boeglin1998" >}} cloned and characterized human skin **12R-lipoxygenase (`ALOX12B`)** and showed its expression and unusual *R*-chirality products (notably 12R-HETE alongside linoleate oxidation) are strongly linked to human psoriatic scale biochemistry—the same enzyme whose loss-of-function mutations cause congenital ichthyosis {{< cite "jobard2002" >}}.
- **Loss of anti-proliferative 13-HODE:** In contrast, 15-lipoxygenase-derived **13-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (13-HODE)** from LA acts as an endogenous brake on keratinocyte proliferation. Miller and Ziboh {{< cite "miller1990" >}} demonstrated in guinea pig epidermis that topical n-3 PUFA-induced epidermal hyperproliferation tracked specifically with depletion of endogenous epidermal 13-HODE. Cho and Ziboh {{< cite "cho1994" >}} then showed that topical 13-HODE reversed epidermal hyperproliferation by selectively inhibiting protein kinase C-β (PKC-β) activity after incorporation into membrane/diacylglycerol pools.
- **Acylceramide remodeling:** Profiling human psoriatic scales, Motta and colleagues {{< cite "motta1993" >}} documented marked alterations in stratum corneum ceramide fractions—most notably a pronounced depletion of linoleate-bearing acylceramide (Ceramide 1 / EOS) alongside shifts in phytosphingosine-containing ceramides—closely paralleling the increased transepidermal water loss of plaques.

**Evidence boundaries:** Together, these studies show that psoriatic hyperproliferation involves simultaneous loss of linoleate-bearing acylceramides, depletion of anti-proliferative 13-HODE signaling, and aberrant 12R-LOX expression. Because psoriasis is fundamentally an IL-23/Th17-driven immune disease, these lipid abnormalities reflect secondary tissue reprogramming and local barrier failure rather than systemic dietary LA deficiency.

#### Hair follicle prostaglandin balance (`PGD2` vs. `PGE2`/`PGF2α`) and sebaceous `FADS2` / sapienic acid vs. linoleic acid

**Direct human tissue, explant, and enzymatic evidence.** Downstream of LA→AA conversion, human hair follicles possess complete local prostaglandin synthesis and receptor machinery—and different prostanoids act in diametrically opposite directions:
- **Prostaglandin metabolism in the follicle:** Colombe and colleagues {{< cite "colombe2007" >}} demonstrated that isolated human hair follicles express COX-1, COX-2, and specific PGE, PGF, and PGD synthases, establishing autonomous local prostanoid turnover within the pilosebaceous unit.
- **PGD₂ inhibits hair growth:** In a landmark translational study, Garza and colleagues {{< cite "garza2012" >}} found that **prostaglandin D₂ synthase (`PTGDS`)** and **PGD₂** were markedly elevated in bald compared with haired scalp of men with androgenetic alopecia. Topical PGD₂ inhibited hair elongation in both mice and human scalp explants specifically through the **GPR44 (`PTGDR2`/DP2)** receptor, not `PTGDR1`.
- **PGF₂α / PGE₂ promote hair growth:** Conversely, prostaglandins of the E and F series stimulate follicular anagen growth. Khidhir and colleagues {{< cite "khidhir2013" >}} proved that **bimatoprost** (a prostamide/PGF₂α analog already known clinically to lengthen eyelashes) directly stimulated growth of organ-cultured human scalp hair follicles and murine pelage regrowth via prostamide/FP-receptor-dependent signaling.

**Sebaceous gland `FADS2` and linoleate catabolism (deepening `downing1986`).** Human sebum is biochemically unusual among mammalian tissues: its dominant monounsaturated fatty acid is **sapienic acid (16:1 Δ6)**, while linoleic acid is scarce. Ge and colleagues {{< cite "ge2003" >}} solved the enzymatic origin of sapienic acid by showing that **FADS2 (Δ6-desaturase)**—classically known as the rate-limiting enzyme converting LA to γ-linolenic acid—is heavily expressed in differentiating human sebocytes, where it preferentially desaturates **palmitic acid (16:0)** into sapienic acid (16:1 Δ6). Complementing Downing's dilution hypothesis {{< cite "downing1986" >}}, Pappas, Anthonavage, and Gordon {{< cite "pappas2002" >}} demonstrated using radiolabeled precursors in human sebaceous glands that sebocytes actively **β-oxidize linoleic acid** into two-carbon acetyl units (which are recycled into squalene and wax-ester synthesis) rather than elongating or storing LA.

**Evidence boundaries:** Hair follicle prostanoid balance proves that *which* downstream synthase and receptor is locally active (`PGD2`/GPR44 vs. `PGF2α`/`PGE2`) determines growth versus miniaturization—not total dietary LA supply. Likewise, sebaceous `FADS2` redirection and LA β-oxidation explain why human sebum is naturally LA-poor without implying that eating more LA will reliably raise follicular LA or prevent acne.

#### Clinical topical linoleic acid in wound healing: phases of repair vs. human ulcer trials

**Direct rodent wound-phase experiments contrasted with human RCTs and Cochrane meta-analysis (extending `hulsey1980` and `cardoso2004`).** Beyond the severe wound-contraction failure seen in systemic EFAD {{< cite "hulsey1980" >}} and early topical comparisons in healthy mice {{< cite "cardoso2004" >}}, rodent studies have mapped how LA modulates specific phases of repair:
- **Inflammatory phase:** Pereira and colleagues {{< cite "pereira2008" >}} applied topical oleic or linoleic acid to surgical wounds in rats and showed that LA accelerated early wound closure, increased local neutrophil influx and proinflammatory cytokine/chemokine release (IL-1β, IL-6, CINC-2αβ) during the acute inflammatory phase, and promoted faster resolution of necrotic debris.
- **Diabetic wound angiogenesis:** Rodrigues and colleagues {{< cite "rodrigues2016" >}} administered oral LA to streptozotocin-diabetic rats and observed enhanced angiogenesis (VEGF expression and neovascularization), faster wound closure, and increased collagen deposition compared with untreated diabetic controls.

**Human clinical evidence (pressure, venous, and diabetic ulcers) is far more cautious and conflicted:**
- **Pressure ulcer prevention (positive vs. non-inferiority trials):** In a multicenter double-blind RCT (*n* = 331), Torra i Bou and colleagues {{< cite "torraibou2005" >}} found that topical application of a **hyperoxygenated fatty acid (HOFA)** compound (predominantly linoleic acid, ~60–70%) lowered pressure ulcer incidence compared with a placebo triisostearin compound (7.32% vs. 17.37%, *p* = 0.006). However, in a larger multicenter triple-blind non-inferiority RCT of 831 immobilized home-care patients, Lupiáñez-Pérez and colleagues {{< cite "lupianezperez2015" >}} demonstrated that ordinary **topical olive oil** (predominantly oleic acid, low LA) was **non-inferior** to commercial HOFAs for preventing pressure ulcers—challenging claims that hyperoxygenated linoleate possesses unique clinical superiority over simpler emollient/lipid barrier oils.
- **Systematic reviews of chronic wounds:** In the Cochrane systematic review of dressings and topical agents for preventing pressure ulcers, Moore and Webster {{< cite "moore2018" >}} graded the certainty of evidence for topical fatty acids versus standard care or alternative compounds as **low to very low** due to imprecision, small sample sizes, and risk of bias. Similarly, Pieper and Caliri {{< cite "pieper2003" >}} reviewed the clinical literature on topical essential fatty acid / LA oils widely used in Latin America for venous, diabetic, and pressure ulcers, concluding that despite biological plausibility and positive uncontrolled case series, rigorous randomized human trials demonstrating accelerated healing of open chronic venous or diabetic ulcers remain scarce.

**Evidence boundaries:** Topical LA/lipid preparations clearly support intact skin hydration and can modulate acute inflammatory/angiogenic signaling in rodent wounds, but human clinical evidence does *not* establish topical LA as uniquely superior to oleic-acid-rich olive oil for pressure ulcer prevention or as a proven primary therapy for open venous or diabetic foot ulcers.

#### Skin, hair, and metabolic rate

**The skin connection is well supported; the metabolic inference is too broad.** Zheng and colleagues {{< cite "zheng2011" >}} identified a linoleate-dependent pathway required to construct the epidermal water barrier. A human report by Kim and colleagues {{< cite "kim2005" >}} described skin disease and alopecia with combined fatty-acid, protein, and borderline zinc deficiencies. Because several nutrients were replaced, that case cannot isolate EFA deficiency as the cause of the hair loss.

Rafael, Patzelt, and Elmadfa {{< cite "rafael1988" >}} found higher resting respiration but impaired cold tolerance in EFA-deficient rats; additional linoleate above the control intake did not raise basal metabolism. A higher metabolic rate was not synonymous with better function. Skin or hair improvement alone cannot establish the direction of whole-body metabolic change.

#### Similar signs do not establish a shared cause

**Clinical overlap, with limited specificity.** In 460 people with diagnosed hypothyroidism, Keen, Hassan, and Bhat {{< cite "keen2013" >}}, documented dry skin and diffuse hair loss among the common findings. The combined-nutrient-deficiency case reported by Kim and colleagues {{< cite "kim2005" >}} illustrates that nutritional illness can also affect skin and hair.

These observations support the resemblance at the level of outward signs. They do not show that the underlying mechanism is the same, that EFA deficiency causes hypothyroidism, or that thinning of the outer eyebrows specifically identifies EFA deficiency. The eyebrow detail is not established by these citations.

#### Wound healing

**Direct animal evidence, with important differences between wounds.** Hulsey and colleagues {{< cite "hulsey1980" >}} compared normal and EFA-deficient rats across skin incisions, intestinal anastomoses, and burns. Control burns healed by 21 days, whereas deficient animals' burns remained incompletely healed at 120 days; wound contraction was impaired. Some other endpoints, including intestinal bursting pressure, did not differ significantly.

The study supports impaired healing, especially of skin, under experimental deficiency. It did not directly establish increased wound infection or greater eventual scarring. Those are additional proposed consequences, not outcomes that can be assigned to this paper.

### 5. Kidney, Water Balance, and Renal Physiology

#### Classic renal pathology and the two-mechanism explanation of polydipsia

**Direct historical animal evidence, later split into cutaneous and renal components.** In their original descriptions of fat-free feeding, Burr and Burr {{< cite "burr1929" >}} {{< cite "burr1930" >}} documented gross renal degeneration, hematuria, and markedly increased water consumption—often accompanied by *reduced* rather than increased urine volume. Borland and Jackson (1931, *Archives of Pathology* 11:687–708) detailed the histopathology in fat-free rats, describing renal tubular degeneration, necrosis, and calcification/concretion.

Later work clarified that the striking polydipsia of EFAD is driven largely by non-renal evaporative loss through a broken epidermal water barrier {{< cite "elias1978" >}} {{< cite "hansen1985" >}} rather than simple diabetes insipidus, while the kidneys themselves respond with altered prostaglandin signaling and heightened water conservation. Historical rodent fat-free pathology demonstrates essentiality for renal integrity under extreme depletion; it does not establish that ordinary variations in human dietary LA cause tubular necrosis or hematuria.

#### Renal medullary prostaglandins, urine concentrating ability, and sodium/water handling

**Direct animal physiology, complementing `maranesi1993`.** In the renal inner medulla and papilla, arachidonic acid derived from LA fuels local prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂) synthesis, which modulates medullary blood flow, sodium excretion, and collecting-duct sensitivity to arginine vasopressin (AVP). Rosenthal, Simone, and Silbergleit {{< cite "rosenthal1974" >}} showed that dietary EFA/prostaglandin-precursor deprivation impaired renal natriuresis and diuresis and altered blood pressure responses in rats. Dunham and colleagues {{< cite "dunham1978" >}} demonstrated that EFAD depleted rat renal medullary arachidonate and sharply depressed medullary prostaglandin biosynthesis.

Hansen {{< cite "hansen1981" >}} directly connected these lipid changes to urine concentration: EFA-deficient rats produced highly concentrated urine with markedly reduced urinary PGE₂ excretion, and re-feeding EFAs restored both urinary PGE₂ and water excretion. Because medullary PGE₂ normally dampens the hydro-osmotic action of AVP in the collecting duct, losing PGE₂ under EFAD—combined with evaporative water loss through the skin—shifts the kidney toward maximal antidiuresis and impaired natriuretic buffering. As noted in the existing B6-interaction annotation {{< cite "maranesi1993" >}}, renal PGE₂ responds to micronutrient cofactors as well as dietary EFA supply, and rodent EFAD experiments cannot be extrapolated directly to healthy humans on mixed diets.

#### The renal immune paradox: EFA deficiency suppresses macrophage-driven glomerulonephritis

**Direct experimental animal evidence illustrating the dual edge of omega-6 eicosanoids.** Although chronic EFAD damages renal tubular and barrier physiology, a classic series of Washington University studies revealed a striking paradox in acute inflammatory kidney disease. Lefkowith and Schreiner {{< cite "lefkowith1987" >}} found that EFAD selectively depleted resident macrophages (Ia⁺ cells) from rat glomeruli and cortex and inhibited angiotensin II–stimulated renal eicosanoid synthesis.

In experimental nephrotoxic glomerulonephritis, Schreiner, Rovin, and Lefkowith {{< cite "schreiner1989" >}} showed that EFAD blocked macrophage infiltration into glomeruli (without blocking neutrophil influx), prevented azotemia and sodium retention, and reduced proteinuria by roughly 85%. Harris and colleagues {{< cite "harris1990" >}} extended this protection to non-immune puromycin aminonucleoside nephrosis, where EFAD attenuated interstitial macrophage infiltration and acute renal dysfunction. These experiments prove that LA-derived AA eicosanoids and chemoattractants actively amplify renal inflammatory injury in rodents—making EFAD paradoxically protective in acute nephritis—without implying that inducing systemic EFA deficiency is a safe or viable therapy for human kidney disease.

#### Macula densa `COX-2`/`PGE2` control of renin release and LA biomarkers in chronic/diabetic kidney disease

- **Omega-6 prostaglandins govern the renin-angiotensin set point; higher human LA biomarkers track *slower* kidney decline**

**Direct renal physiology in rodents paired with large human prospective cohorts (extending `rosenthal1974`, `dunham1978`, `hansen1981`).** Beyond medullary water-handling and glomerular inflammation (Pass 1), AA derived from LA controls the upstream trigger of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) at the juxtaglomerular apparatus:
- **Macula densa COX-2 and renin secretion:** Harris and colleagues {{< cite "harris1994" >}} discovered that **cyclooxygenase-2 (`COX-2`)** is constitutively localized to macula densa cells and thick ascending limb epithelium in the rat kidney and is markedly upregulated by dietary salt restriction. Traynor and colleagues {{< cite "traynor1999" >}} proved functional causality by showing that selective pharmacological COX-2 inhibition blunted macula densa–stimulated renin secretion. Yang and colleagues {{< cite "yang2000" >}} confirmed the cellular trigger in cultured mouse macula densa cells: low luminal chloride directly induced COX-2 expression and stimulated **PGE₂** release, which acts paracrinely on adjacent juxtaglomerular granular cells (via cAMP-coupled EP2/EP4 receptors) to drive renin exocytosis.

**Human diabetic nephropathy and CKD biomarker associations:** Because LA fuels AA/eicosanoids—and COX-2/PGE₂ also drives glomerular hyperfiltration in early diabetes—a plausible mechanistic concern is that higher LA status might accelerate diabetic or chronic kidney disease. Human clinical cohort data consistently point in the opposite direction:
- **Type 2 diabetes & CKD:** In a cross-sectional study of patients with type 2 diabetes, dos Santos and colleagues {{< cite "dossantos2018" >}} found that *lower* dietary intake of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid was significantly associated with higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease.
- **Longitudinal eGFR decline in post-MI & diabetic patients:** In the prospective Alpha Omega Cohort (~40-month follow-up), van Westing and colleagues {{< cite "vanwesting2021" >}} measured baseline plasma cholesteryl-ester fatty acids and reported that higher circulating **linoleic acid** was associated with a slower annual decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), with the strongest inverse associations in subgroups with **diabetes** and pre-existing CKD.
- **UK Biobank prospective CKD cohort:** In a large-scale prospective analysis of >73,000 participants without baseline CKD and 6,735 with established CKD in the UK Biobank, Koh and colleagues {{< cite "koh2024" >}} found that higher plasma polyunsaturated fatty acids—including plasma LA percentage—were inversely associated with incident CKD and adverse kidney outcomes.

**Evidence boundaries:** Macula densa studies prove that AA-derived PGE₂ is a physiological regulator of renin release (explaining why NSAIDs suppress renin and perturb renal hemodynamics). Human biomarker cohorts show that higher circulating LA is consistently associated with *better*, not worse, renal trajectories—even in diabetes—but observational plasma lipid fractions remain vulnerable to confounding by overall diet quality, adiposity, insulin sensitivity, and endogenous desaturase activity; no large RCT has tested whether raising dietary LA prevents human diabetic nephropathy.

### 6. Vascular, Endothelial, Cardiac Electrophysiology, and Hematology

#### Endothelial cell membranes, prostacyclin (PGI₂), and vessel-wall 13-HODE

**Direct cell-culture biochemistry; whole-vessel net effects are context-dependent.** Vascular endothelial cells balance vasodilatory, anti-aggregatory prostacyclin (PGI₂, derived from AA) against platelet thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂), while also generating LA-derived lipoxygenase products. Spector and colleagues {{< cite "spector1980" >}} enriched cultured human umbilical vein endothelial cells with linoleic acid and found a substantial *decrease* in thrombin- or ionophore-stimulated PGI₂ release, accompanied by linoleate incorporation into phospholipids and displacement of AA. Kaduce, Spector, and Bar {{< cite "kaduce1982" >}} confirmed this suppression in cultured bovine pulmonary artery endothelial cells, implicating both AA displacement and direct competitive inhibition.

At the same time, Buchanan and colleagues {{< cite "buchanan1985" >}} identified **13-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (13-HODE)**—an endogenous 15-lipoxygenase metabolite of linoleic acid synthesized by vascular endothelial cells—as a vessel-wall chemorepellant factor that reduces platelet adhesion to intact endothelium. Thus, even within a single cell type, LA is neither purely pro-thrombotic nor purely anti-thrombotic: excess free LA can blunt AA-derived PGI₂ in vitro while supporting endothelial 13-HODE synthesis. Cultured monolayer experiments do not determine which pathway dominates across intact human vasculature.

#### Endothelial permeability, oxidative stress, nitric oxide, and the cell-culture vs. human-trial conflict

**A sharp methodological conflict between in vitro free-fatty-acid exposure and human dietary trials.** In cultured porcine and human endothelial monolayers, Toborek and colleagues {{< cite "toborek1996" >}} showed that unesterified linoleic acid cross-amplified TNF-α–induced oxidative stress, disrupted intracellular calcium, and increased transendothelial albumin permeability. Toborek and colleagues {{< cite "toborek2002" >}} further reported that among tested fatty acids, LA selectively activated NF-κB and AP-1 and upregulated inflammatory adhesion molecules and cytokines (VCAM-1, ICAM-1, MCP-1, TNF-α) in human endothelial cells. For endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), non-esterified fatty acids also impair signaling in vitro—for example, Davda and colleagues {{< cite "davda1995" >}} demonstrated concentration-dependent eNOS inhibition by oleic acid in bovine pulmonary artery endothelial cells, showing that non-esterified fatty acid stress on eNOS is not unique to LA.

Human vascular data paint a much more restrained picture. In the randomized, controlled **DIVAS** trial (16 weeks in adults at moderate cardiovascular risk), Vafeiadou and colleagues {{< cite "vafeiadou2015" >}} replaced saturated fat with either MUFA or n-6 PUFA (predominantly LA). Neither unsaturated diet altered brachial flow-mediated dilation (FMD) or arterial stiffness compared with the saturated-fat diet, while replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fats improved LDL cholesterol, lowered circulating E-selectin (an endothelial activation marker), and reduced blood pressure. Similarly, in the cross-sectional **INTERMAP** study across four countries, Miura and colleagues {{< cite "miura2008" >}} found a modest, statistically significant inverse association between dietary LA intake and systolic/diastolic blood pressure (−1.42 / −0.91 mm Hg per 2 SD higher intake among non-intervened adults).

Taken together, acute exposure of cultured endothelial cells to high concentrations of unesterified LA demonstrates real oxidative and permeability vulnerability—particularly alongside inflammatory cytokines—but does not predict endothelial dysfunction or hypertension in human dietary substitution trials. Conversely, neutral 16-week FMD results and observational blood-pressure correlations do not rule out localized subendothelial oxidation of LA-rich lipoproteins over decades.

#### Myocardial membrane remodeling, cardiac cardiolipin, arrhythmia, and human cardiovascular biomarkers

**Direct animal heart-failure and ischemia evidence alongside global human biomarker pooling.** In healthy mammalian myocardium, inner mitochondrial membrane cardiolipin is heavily enriched in tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin (L₄CL), which stabilizes respiratory supercomplexes and oxidative phosphorylation (complementing {{< cite "le2020" >}} and {{< cite "lopaschuk2010" >}}). Mulligan and colleagues {{< cite "mulligan2012" >}} showed that in aging spontaneously hypertensive heart failure (SHHF) rats—which selectively lose cardiac L₄CL—dietary supplementation with high-linoleate safflower oil preserved ~90% of L₄CL levels, attenuated mitochondrial state-3 respiration deficits, and improved left-ventricular contractile performance (fractional shortening) compared with control or lard-fed animals.

By contrast, membrane enrichment with LA-rich oils is less protective against ischemic arrhythmia than marine n-3 fatty acids. McLennan, Abeywardena, and Charnock {{< cite "mclennan1988" >}} found in rats undergoing coronary artery occlusion and reperfusion that tuna fish oil prevented ventricular fibrillation during both occlusion and reperfusion, whereas sunflower-seed oil (LA-rich) reduced arrhythmias during occlusion but did not match fish oil during reperfusion.

In humans, Marklund and colleagues {{< cite "marklund2019" >}} harmonized individual-level data across 30 prospective cohort studies in 13 countries (FORCE consortium) and found that higher circulating and adipose biomarkers of linoleic acid were consistently associated with lower risk of incident cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular mortality, and ischemic stroke—with no excess risk for arachidonic acid. Those biomarker findings sit in tension with post-mortem coronary phospholipid observations in sudden cardiac death {{< cite "luostarinen1993" >}} and with cell-level peroxidation hypotheses {{< cite "chen1998" >}}; observational biomarker cohorts cannot exclude residual confounding or distinguish dietary substitution effects from endogenous lipid-handling differences, just as rodent heart-failure or isolated-lipid models cannot directly prescribe optimal human intakes.

#### Cardiac electrophysiology & ion channels: direct modulation of Na⁺, L-type Ca²⁺, and K⁺ currents by free PUFAs

**Direct patch-clamp electrophysiology in isolated cardiomyocytes and expression systems (deepening `mclennan1988`).** Pass 1 noted that fish-oil diets outperformed LA-rich sunflower oil against reperfusion ventricular fibrillation in rats {{< cite "mclennan1988" >}}. Patch-clamp electrophysiology clarifies why **free (unesterified)** PUFAs modulate cardiac excitability directly, and where LA matches or diverges from marine n-3 PUFAs:
- **Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (I<sub>Na</sub>):** Xiao and colleagues {{< cite "xiao1995" >}} showed in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes that low-micromolar unesterified PUFAs (EPA, DHA, and AA) rapidly and reversibly suppressed voltage-gated Na⁺ currents and shifted steady-state inactivation toward more hyperpolarized potentials—increasing the threshold for action-potential firing—whereas saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids were largely inactive.
- **L-type Ca²⁺ channels (I<sub>Ca,L</sub>):** In a companion study, Xiao and colleagues {{< cite "xiao1997" >}} demonstrated that unesterified PUFAs—explicitly including **linoleic acid (18:2 n-6)** alongside ALA, AA, EPA, and DHA—reversibly inhibited voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ currents and blunted intracellular Ca²⁺ transients in both neonatal and adult rat ventricular myocytes, suppressing calcium-overload-driven triggered arrhythmias.
- **Cardiac delayed-rectifier K⁺ channels (I<sub>Ks</sub> / Kv7.1):** More recently, Liin and colleagues {{< cite "liin2015" >}} mapped the electrostatic mechanism by which negatively charged PUFA headgroups interact directly with the positively charged voltage-sensor domain (S4) of the cardiac **I<sub>Ks</sub> (`Kv7.1`/`KCNE1`)** potassium channel, shifting its activation curve to shorten prolonged action potentials in arrhythmia models; tuning headgroup charge and acyl-chain polyunsaturation (especially n-3 and modified PUFA analogs) governs channel-opening potency.

**Evidence boundaries:** These electrophysiological studies prove that *free* unesterified PUFAs—including LA at micromolar concentrations—directly modulate cardiac Na⁺, Ca²⁺, and K⁺ channels in vitro. However, two major caveats block naive clinical extrapolation: (1) in intact hearts, fatty acids are predominantly esterified in phospholipids or albumin-bound rather than freely floating at micromolar concentrations except during acute ischemia/lipolysis, and (2) while free LA blocks I<sub>Ca,L</sub> in acute patch-clamp assays {{< cite "xiao1997" >}}, whole-animal dietary feeding shows marine n-3 PUFAs consistently outperform LA-rich oils against ischemic ventricular fibrillation {{< cite "mclennan1988" >}} because dietary LA simultaneously feeds arrhythmogenic/ischemia-sensitive AA and CYP/sEH oxylipin pathways.

#### Endothelial EDHFs: vasodilatory AA-derived EETs vs. cardiotoxic LA-derived EpOMEs/DiHOMEs

**Direct vascular pharmacology, human arterial physiology, and transgenic mouse ischemia models (extending `spector1980`, `buchanan1985`, `moghaddam1997`, `zheng2001`).** Alongside nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin (PGI₂), resistance arteries and coronary microvessels rely heavily on **endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factors (EDHFs)** to relax vascular smooth muscle:
- **EETs as coronary EDHFs:** Campbell and colleagues {{< cite "campbell1996" >}} identified **epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs)**—cytochrome P450 epoxygenase metabolites of arachidonic acid—as endogenous EDHFs in bovine coronary arteries that hyperpolarize smooth muscle cells by opening large-conductance calcium-activated potassium (`BK_Ca`) channels. Fisslthaler and colleagues {{< cite "fisslthaler1999" >}} identified **cytochrome P450 2C (`CYP2C`)** as the endothelial EDHF synthase in coronary arteries.
- **Confirmation in human arteries:** Translating this directly to human vasculature, Archer and colleagues {{< cite "archer2003" >}} demonstrated that **11,12-EET** acts as the EDHF in human internal mammary arteries via smooth-muscle `BK_Ca` activation, and Larsen and colleagues {{< cite "larsen2006" >}} proved that EETs (and their soluble epoxide hydrolase metabolites, DHETs) potently dilate **human coronary arterioles** via `BK_Ca` channels.
- **The substrate-switching conflict (CYP2C8 + LA → 9,10-DiHOME):** Crucially, the very same endothelial CYP2C epoxygenases epoxidize **linoleic acid** into **9,10- and 12,13-EpOMEs**, which soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH) converts into **DiHOMEs**. Edin and colleagues {{< cite "edin2011" >}} expressed human endothelial **CYP2C8** in mice and found—paradoxically—that instead of protecting the heart via AA-derived EETs, endothelial CYP2C8 *worsened* cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury in isolated hearts specifically by increasing **LA-derived 9,10-DiHOME** formation, which impaired post-ischemic functional recovery and coronary perfusion (contrasting with the protective role of physiological 12,13-DiHOME signaling in `pinckard2021` and highlighting dose/isomer/ischemia context).

**Evidence boundaries:** These papers prove that omega-6 CYP epoxygenase biology is substrate- and isomer-specific: AA-derived EETs are bona fide vasodilatory EDHFs in human arteries, whereas excessive sEH-mediated conversion of LA epoxides into DiHOMEs during ischemia-reperfusion can depress cardiac recovery in rodents. They do not establish that normal dietary LA intakes eliminate human EET-mediated vasodilation.

#### Hematology & platelets: erythrocyte membrane fragility/deformability and the platelet `TXA2`/`PGI2` bleeding-time balance

**Direct human metabolic-ward feeding, erythrocyte biophysics, and rodent EFAD experiments (complementing `bistrian1981` and `rachmilewitz1976`).** Red blood cells (erythrocytes) lack nuclei and *de novo* fatty-acid synthesis, making their phospholipid bilayer a direct mirror of circulating lipid supply:
- **Rapid human membrane remodeling:** Farquhar and Ahrens {{< cite "farquhar1963" >}} proved in human metabolic-ward feeding studies that switching dietary fat (e.g., corn oil vs. butterfat) rapidly remodeled human erythrocyte phospholipid fatty-acid patterns within weeks, demonstrating active lipid exchange between plasma lipoproteins and mature red-cell membranes.
- **Osmotic fragility in EFAD:** In rats, Huang, Dufour, and Davignon {{< cite "huang1983" >}} showed that essential fatty acid deficiency markedly depleted erythrocyte linoleate and arachidonate (replacing them with oleate and Mead acid) and altered **erythrocyte osmotic fragility**—defects reversed by administering methyl linoleate.
- **Peroxidative hemolysis in high-LA / low-vitamin-E states:** At the opposite extreme, Horwitt's landmark human Elgin Project studies {{< cite "horwitt1960" >}} demonstrated that feeding high levels of linoleic-acid-rich vegetable oil dramatically increased erythrocyte membrane LA content and accelerated plasma α-tocopherol depletion, triggering marked **hydrogen-peroxide–induced erythrocyte hemolysis** and shortened red-cell survival when vitamin E intake did not scale with dietary PUFA load.
- **Membrane fluidity and deformability:** Membrane unsaturation also governs microvascular rheology; Popp-Snijders and colleagues {{< cite "poppsnijders1986" >}} demonstrated in humans that enriching erythrocyte membranes with long-chain marine n-3 PUFAs lowered whole-suspension erythrocyte viscosity (increasing red-cell deformability) compared with baseline diets—showing that membrane deformability responds specifically to polyunsaturated chain length and double-bond geometry rather than total unsaturation alone.

**Direct human clinical feeding trials, population hemostasis studies, and biochemical mechanism.** Moncada and Vane {{< cite "moncada1979" >}} established the classic hemostatic equilibrium: platelets convert arachidonic acid via COX-1 into pro-aggregatory, vasoconstrictive **thromboxane A₂ (`TXA₂`)**, whereas vascular endothelium converts AA into anti-aggregatory, vasodilatory **prostacyclin (`PGI₂`)**. Because LA is the dietary precursor to AA, a persistent popular assumption holds that eating more LA automatically promotes hyper-aggregable platelets and thrombosis. Direct experimental and human clinical data contradict that simple linear model:
- **Human high-LA trials often reduce—or do not worsen—platelet aggregation:** In a classic human dietary intervention across psychiatric hospitals in Helsinki and clinical wards, Hornstra and colleagues {{< cite "hornstra1973" >}} found that men consuming a diet high in linoleic acid (soybean/sunflower oil replacing saturated fat) exhibited significantly *reduced* platelet aggregation/thrombosis tendency (filtration pressure / filtragometer index) compared with saturated-fat controls. In rats, McGregor, Morazain, and Renaud {{< cite "mcgregor1980" >}} showed that dietary LA remodeling of platelet phospholipids altered thrombin-induced aggregation in a non-linear fashion relative to saturated fat.
- **The marine n-3 contrast (bleeding time & `TXA2` suppression):** The clearest shift toward prolonged bleeding time comes not from lowering LA alone, but from enriching platelets with marine **EPA/DHA**. Dyerberg and colleagues {{< cite "dyerberg1978" >}} proposed that EPA displaces platelet AA to yield biologically weak `TXA₃` while preserving vasodilatory `PGI₃`, and Dyerberg and Bang {{< cite "dyerberg1979" >}} confirmed markedly **prolonged bleeding times** and reduced ADP/collagen platelet aggregation in Greenland Inuit whose platelets were enriched in EPA and depleted of AA. In a controlled human trial of patients with atherosclerosis, Knapp and colleagues {{< cite "knapp1986" >}} directly compared high-dose fish oil against **safflower oil (high-LA)** control: fish oil markedly depressed in vivo urinary `TXB₂` metabolites and prolonged bleeding time, whereas LA-rich safflower oil did *not* increase urinary thromboxane excretion or shorten bleeding time.
- **Direct LA vs. ALA/n-3 feeding comparison:** In a controlled human feeding study in healthy adults, Freese and Mutanen {{< cite "freese1997" >}} compared LA-rich sunflower oil against ALA-rich flaxseed oil and marine n-3 diets, observing only modest differences in hemostatic variables and platelet aggregation—reinforcing that in humans on mixed diets, increasing dietary LA does not linearly inflate platelet AA or `TXA₂` generation (consistent with `rett2011`).

**Evidence boundaries:** Together, hematology and platelet trials prove (1) both EFAD and unbuffered high-LA/low-vitamin-E states compromise red-cell membrane stability via opposite mechanisms (structural depletion vs. peroxidation), and (2) while AA-derived `TXA₂` drives platelet aggregation and marine n-3 fatty acids clearly blunt `TXA₂` and prolong bleeding time, human dietary LA substitution trials have *not* demonstrated a pro-thrombotic surge in platelet `TXA₂` or shortened bleeding time.

### 7. Gastrointestinal Tract, Gut Microbiome, Pulmonary, and Ocular Tissues

#### Intestinal barrier, tight junctions, and inflammatory bowel disease (deficiency vs. excess)

**Direct animal, cell-culture, and human cohort evidence showing a non-monotonic relationship.** Existing notes establish that low-LA diets remodel enterocyte brush-border membranes and villus/crypt architecture in rats {{< cite "christon1991" >}} and that blocking LA→AA conversion in *Fads2*-null mice causes intestinal ulceration prevented by AA {{< cite "stroud2009" >}}. In human T84 intestinal epithelial monolayers, Willemsen and colleagues {{< cite "willemsen2008" >}} showed an important parent-vs.-product split: downstream long-chain PUFAs (DGLA, AA, EPA, DHA) increased basal transepithelial electrical resistance and blunted IL-4–induced permeability, whereas parent LA and ALA were ineffective.

At the opposite end of intake, excess dietary LA has been linked to barrier breakdown and colitis. Deol and colleagues {{< cite "deol2023" >}} fed mice high-fat diets based on conventional high-LA soybean oil versus low-LA genetically modified soybean oil or olive oil; only the high-LA soybean oil diet increased intestinal epithelial permeability, worsened colitis across wild-type and *Il10*-null models, suppressed intestinal endocannabinoids, altered *Hnf4a* isoform ratios, and expanded an endogenous adherent-invasive *Escherichia coli* (AIEC) strain able to use LA as a carbon source. In humans, a prospective nested case-control analysis of >203,000 European adults in the EPIC cohort by Tjønneland and colleagues {{< cite "tjonneland2009" >}} found that the highest quartile of dietary LA intake was associated with a higher odds of incident ulcerative colitis (OR = 2.49 vs. lowest quartile). These findings show that intestinal epithelium requires downstream PUFAs for homeostasis yet may be vulnerable to high-LA diets in susceptible hosts or microbial contexts; observational food-frequency data and high-fat mouse diets do not by themselves establish a universal human toxic threshold.

#### Fat malabsorption, chylomicron assembly, and lithogenic bile in EFA deficiency

**Direct animal physiology in rats and hamsters.** Beyond mucosal morphology, EFA deficiency impairs the gut-liver axis responsible for absorbing dietary fat. Clark and colleagues {{< cite "clark1973" >}} established EFA-deficient rats as an experimental model of fat malabsorption, demonstrating impaired intestinal lipid absorption. Levy and colleagues {{< cite "levy1992" >}} dissected the defect into two distinct phases: *intraluminally*, EFA-deficient rats exhibited reduced bile flow and decreased biliary secretion of bile salts, phospholipids, and cholesterol; *intracellularly*, their enterocytes showed impaired fatty-acid esterification and markedly reduced synthesis and lymphatic secretion of chylomicron triglycerides.

In hamsters—whose biliary physiology models human cholesterol gallstone formation—Robins and Fasulo {{< cite "robins1973" >}} demonstrated that an EFA-deficient diet selectively lowered hepatic excretion of taurocholate, lecithin (phospholipids), and water while leaving cholesterol excretion intact, generating cholesterol-supersaturated (lithogenic) bile and gallstones. Because fat malabsorption can itself *cause* clinical EFAD (as in short-bowel syndrome or cystic fibrosis), Levy and colleagues' findings point to a bidirectional loop in severe deficiency. They do not show that modest variations in dietary LA cause gallstones or malabsorption in EFA-replete humans.

#### Pulmonary surfactant remodeling, cystic fibrosis, and linoleic-acid-derived leukotoxins in ARDS

**Direct animal surfactant/injury experiments and human CF observational evidence, building beyond `nakamura1980`, `quinlan1994`, and `freedman2004`.** Prior to Nakamura and colleagues {{< cite "nakamura1980" >}}, Kyriakides and colleagues {{< cite "kyriakides1976" >}} showed that EFAD in rats altered pulmonary surfactant composition by reducing palmitate and depleting disaturated phosphatidylcholines (principally dipalmitoyl-PC, the primary surface-tension-lowering lipid in alveoli) while increasing oleate and palmitoleate—changes that reversed within 7–14 days of dietary linoleate repletion.

In human **cystic fibrosis (CF)**, where plasma/tissue EFA abnormalities are linked to CFTR dysfunction {{< cite "freedman2004" >}}, Maqbool and colleagues {{< cite "maqbool2008" >}} studied 77 children with CF and pancreatic insufficiency alongside 23 healthy controls over 12 months. Serum phospholipid LA (rather than the triene:tetraene ratio alone) was positively associated with growth z-scores and baseline pulmonary function (FEV₁ % predicted), with children maintaining serum LA ≥21 mol% showing better clinical metrics. Because CF involves chronic airway inflammation, pancreatic insufficiency, and altered lipid turnover, this observational association supports LA as a clinical status marker rather than proving LA supplementation alone restores lung function.

Conversely, in **acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)**—where declining plasma LA tracks oxidative stress {{< cite "quinlan1994" >}}—oxidized LA metabolites can directly injure the lung. Zheng and colleagues {{< cite "zheng2001" >}} demonstrated in mice that **leukotoxin-diol** (DiHOME, formed when soluble epoxide hydrolase hydrates the LA epoxide leukotoxin/EpOME) caused severe ARDS-like alveolar edema, hemorrhage, and mortality, whereas equivalent doses of parent leukotoxin caused only minor perivascular changes. This illustrates that LA is simultaneously required to support normal saturated-surfactant phospholipid turnover and capable of generating potent pulmonary toxicants when enzymatically epoxidized and hydrolyzed during severe inflammation.

#### Ocular surface, meibomian gland lipids, and dry eye disease

**Direct lipidomic and genetic evidence in humans and mice; conflicting clinical supplementation trials.** Like the stratum corneum, the ocular tear film relies on an outer lipid layer to retard water evaporation. Butovich, Wojtowicz, and Molai {{< cite "butovich2009" >}} used HPLC-MS to characterize human meibomian gland secretions (meibum) and identified **(O-acyl)-ω-hydroxy fatty acids (OAHFAs)** and very-long-chain wax esters as major amphiphilic structural lipids bridging the aqueous tear phase and outer nonpolar lipid layer. Miyamoto and colleagues {{< cite "miyamoto2020" >}} proved the functional necessity of this polarity gradient: mice lacking the ω-hydroxylase *Cyp4f39* (the mouse ortholog of human *CYP4F22*, which also initiates epidermal acylceramide synthesis) lost OAHFAs and developed shortened tear break-up time, corneal epithelial damage, and overt dry eye disease.

An important biochemical caveat prevents over-extrapolating the skin-barrier story to the eye: whereas epidermal acylceramides selectively require **linoleic acid** as the esterified ω-acyl chain {{< cite "hansen1985" >}} {{< cite "ohno2017" >}}, meibum OAHFAs are predominantly esterified with **oleic acid (18:1)** rather than LA. Clinically, oral EFA trials in dry eye remain unsettled. In a small trial of patients with inflammatory aqueous-deficient dry eye, Barabino and colleagues {{< cite "barabino2003" >}} reported that oral LA plus GLA improved subjective symptoms, lissamine green staining, and conjunctival inflammation compared with controls. However, in the large, multicenter, 12-month randomized **DREAM** trial (*n* > 500 with moderate-to-severe dry eye), Asbell and colleagues {{< cite "asbell2018" >}} found that 3,000 mg/day of marine n-3 fatty acids produced no greater improvement in symptoms (OSDI) or ocular surface signs than an olive-oil placebo. Together, these studies show that while ocular tear-film stability shares ω-hydroxy-lipid biochemistry with the epidermis, routine oral PUFA supplementation has not been proven uniformly effective for human dry eye.

#### Bacterial hydration and biohydrogenation of linoleic acid (`HYA` and `CLA`): tight junctions, GPR40/GPR120, GLP-1, and metabolic resistance

**Landmark microbiological, biochemical, and germ-free/knockout mouse evidence.** One of the most transformative modern discoveries in lipid biology is that dietary linoleic acid is not simply absorbed untouched by the host; gut bacteria actively hydrate and saturate LA—likely evolving this pathway to neutralize the antimicrobial toxicity of free cis-PUFAs (`kabara1972`, `zheng2005`)—generating bioactive intermediates that signal directly to host receptors:
- **Elucidation of the *Lactobacillus* saturation pathway:** Kishino and colleagues {{< cite "kishino2013" >}} mapped the complete multi-enzyme PUFA saturation machinery in the gut commensal *Lactobacillus plantarum* (beginning with CLA-HY, a linoleate hydratase), converting LA through **10-hydroxy-cis-12-octadecenoic acid (HYA)**, oxo-fatty acids (KetoA), and **conjugated linoleic acid (CLA)** isomers into oleic/stearic-like products. Comparing germ-free and specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice, they proved that gut microbes substantially shape hydroxy- and oxo-fatty acid pools within host organs—not merely the gut lumen.
- **Human gut commensal CLA diversity:** Earlier work by Devillard and colleagues {{< cite "devillard2007" >}} isolated human colonic bacteria (including *Roseburia* and *Bifidobacterium* species) and showed distinct taxonomic routes converting LA into *cis*-9, *trans*-11 CLA, *trans*-10, *cis*-12 CLA, and vaccenic acid, establishing that microbial LA metabolism varies strongly across individual human microbiomes.
- **Intestinal tight junctions and colitis protection (HYA → GPR40):** Miyamoto and colleagues {{< cite "miyamoto2015" >}} demonstrated that **HYA** acts as a selective agonist of the free fatty acid receptor **GPR40 (`FFAR1`)** on intestinal epithelial cells. In TNF-α–challenged Caco-2 monolayers and DSS-induced colitis mice, HYA preserved tight-junction proteins (**occludin**, **ZO-1**), suppressed myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK) and TNFR2 upregulation, and restored barrier integrity partially through the **GPR40–MEK–ERK** signaling cascade.
- **Host resistance to obesity and metabolic inflammation (HYA → GPR40/GPR120 → GLP-1):** Extending this axis to whole-body metabolism, Miyamoto and colleagues {{< cite "miyamoto2019" >}} showed that supplementing high-fat diets with HYA—or colonizing mice with *Lactobacillus* expressing the LA-hydratase enzyme—conferred marked resistance to high-fat-diet-induced obesity, improved glucose tolerance, and stimulated intestinal **GLP-1** secretion and peristalsis via **GPR40 (`FFAR1`)** and **GPR120 (`FFAR4`)** (confirmed using *Gpr40*- and *Gpr120*-knockout mice). Crucially, unlike excess unconverted LA—which enriched tissue arachidonic acid and amplified adipose inflammation—HYA diverted dietary LA away from the AA-eicosanoid cascade while activating anti-inflammatory incretin signaling.

**Evidence boundaries & tension with Pass 1 (`deol2023`):** These findings create a striking biological duality in the gut: whereas high-LA soybean oil expands adherent-invasive *E. coli* (AIEC) and worsens colitis in susceptible mice {{< cite "deol2023" >}}, lactic-acid commensals converting LA into HYA *protect* tight junctions and counter diet-induced obesity {{< cite "miyamoto2015" >}} {{< cite "miyamoto2019" >}}. The net physiological effect of dietary LA on the gut therefore depends heavily on the resident microbiome's enzymatic capacity. Most causal HYA/CLA barrier and GLP-1 proofs come from rodent and cell models; while small exploratory human crossover trials of HYA supplementation on postprandial glycemia have been initiated, large-scale peer-reviewed human clinical RCTs confirming long-term weight-loss or IBD efficacy remain lacking.

#### Biliary phosphatidylcholine (`16:0/18:2` PC) and enterocyte phospholipid remodeling (`LPCAT3`) in fat absorption

**Direct human biliary lipidomics and mouse genetic knockouts (deepening `clark1973`, `levy1992`, `robins1973`).** Pass 1 established that frank EFA deficiency impairs both biliary lipid secretion and chylomicron assembly, producing lithogenic bile and fat malabsorption {{< cite "clark1973" >}} {{< cite "levy1992" >}} {{< cite "robins1973" >}}. Two foundational molecular discoveries explain *why* biliary and enterocyte membranes specifically depend on omega-6 phosphatidylcholine species:
- **16:0/18:2 PC dominates human bile:** Using HPLC analysis of human gallbladder bile across healthy controls, gallstone patients, and individuals with obesity, Hay, Cahalane, Timofeyeva, and Carey {{< cite "hay1993" >}} proved that **1-palmitoyl-2-linoleoyl-phosphatidylcholine (`16:0/18:2` PC)** is quantitatively the single most abundant molecular lecithin species secreted into human bile (far ahead of 16:0/18:1 and 16:0/20:4 PC).
- **Biliary PC protects against bile-salt toxicity (`ABCB4`/`Mdr2`):** Smit and colleagues {{< cite "smit1993" >}} disrupted the murine *Mdr2* (ortholog of human *ABCB4*) canalicular phospholipid floppase gene, completely abolishing phosphatidylcholine secretion into bile while leaving bile-salt secretion intact. Without PC to form mixed micelles with detergent bile salts, *Mdr2*-null mice developed severe cholangiocyte injury, portal inflammation, and progressive liver disease—demonstrating why hepatic delivery of `16:0/18:2` PC into bile is vital for hepatobiliary integrity and cholesterol solubilization.
- **Enterocyte PUFA remodeling drives chylomicron assembly (`LPCAT3`):** At the absorptive enterocyte, Rong and colleagues {{< cite "rong2015" >}} knocked out **lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (`Lpcat3`)**—the enzyme that selectively esterifies omega-6 PUFAs (preferentially arachidonate and linoleate) into the *sn*-2 position of phosphatidylcholine. *Lpcat3*-deficient mice developed severe neonatal lipid malabsorption, enterocyte triglyceride accumulation, and failed chylomicron secretion because losing polyunsaturated PC chains reduced endoplasmic-reticulum membrane fluidity and impaired triglyceride transfer into nascent lipoproteins.

**Evidence boundaries:** Together, `hay1993`, `smit1993`, and `rong2015` explain the molecular basis of both intraluminal (biliary mixed-micelle) and intracellular (chylomicron packaging) fat-absorption failure in EFAD {{< cite "levy1992" >}}. They prove that adequate omega-6 acyl chains in hepatic and intestinal PC are structurally indispensable for lipid digestion and transport, without implying that supraphysiological dietary LA improves bile quality or fat absorption in EFA-replete adults.

#### Airway smooth muscle & asthma: leukotriene bronchoconstriction vs. `PGE2` bronchodilation and the epidemiological LA/asthma conflict

**Direct human bronchial provocation, receptor pharmacology, and conflicting human observational cohorts (extending `samuelsson1983`).** Arachidonic acid derived from LA feeds two opposing airway-smooth-muscle pathways:
- **Bronchoconstriction (cysteinyl leukotrienes):** As established by Samuelsson {{< cite "samuelsson1983" >}}, 5-lipoxygenase converts AA into cysteinyl leukotrienes (`LTC₄`, `LTD₄`, `LTE₄`)—formerly known as slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis (SRS-A)—which are among the most potent endogenous bronchoconstrictors and mucus secretagogues in human asthma (the direct target of clinical CysLT₁ antagonists such as montelukast).
- **Bronchodilation & anti-inflammation (`PGE2` → `EP4`):** Paradoxically, another AA/COX product—**prostaglandin E₂ (`PGE₂`)**—is a powerful endogenous *bronchoprotective* signal in human lungs. Pavord and colleagues {{< cite "pavord1993" >}} demonstrated in patients with atopic asthma that inhaled `PGE₂` virtually abolished both early- and late-phase allergen-induced bronchoconstriction and prevented allergen-induced airway hyperresponsiveness. Buckley and colleagues {{< cite "buckley2011" >}} then identified the specific human receptor mediating this effect: unlike rodents (which use EP2), human airway smooth muscle relaxes to `PGE₂` specifically via the **EP4 receptor**.

**The epidemiological LA/asthma debate (genuine conflict in human data):**
- **The hypothesis:** Black and Sharpe {{< cite "black1997" >}} proposed an influential ecological hypothesis: rising Western intake of LA-rich vegetable oils (and declining oily fish / n-3 intake) over the late 20th century drove increased AA/leukotriene and `PGE₂`-mediated Th2 skewing, explaining the parallel rise in childhood asthma and atopy.
- **Contradictory biomarker & birth-cohort evidence:** Objective fatty-acid biomarker studies challenged a simple LA-causes-asthma causal arrow. In a UK case-control study measuring fasting erythrocyte membrane fatty acids (*n* = 89 cases, 89 matched controls), Broadfield and colleagues {{< cite "broadfield2004" >}} found that higher **erythrocyte membrane LA** was associated with a *lower* odds of asthma (OR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.21–0.95), directly contradicting predictions from the dietary-LA hypothesis. In the large prospective Swedish **BAMSE** birth cohort, Magnusson and colleagues {{< cite "magnusson2018" >}} measured plasma phospholipid PUFAs at age 8 and tracked allergic disease to age 16: higher plasma n-3 PUFAs *and* higher plasma arachidonic acid were associated with *reduced* risk of subsequent asthma and rhinitis, while plasma **LA** showed no positive association with incident asthma.
- **Modifier of severity in vulnerable children:** By contrast, in an observational study of predominantly low-income urban children with *established* asthma (*n* = 135), Brigham and colleagues {{< cite "brigham2019" >}} found that higher dietary omega-6 intake was associated with worse asthma severity, higher circulating neutrophil percentages, and an amplified symptom response to indoor PM₂.₅ air pollution, whereas higher omega-3 intake blunted the PM₂.₅ effect.

**Evidence boundaries:** In human airways, omega-6 eicosanoids simultaneously drive leukotriene bronchoconstriction and EP4-mediated `PGE₂` bronchodilation. Prospective biomarker cohorts do *not* confirm that higher LA status causes incident asthma in the general population (`broadfield2004`, `magnusson2018`), whereas dietary n-6/n-3 balance may still act as an effect modifier of symptom severity and pollution sensitivity in children with existing inflammatory asthma (`brigham2019`).

#### Epithelial structure and the intestinal barrier

**Direct evidence in specific tissues and animal models.** Christon and colleagues {{< cite "christon1991" >}} found altered intestinal villi, crypts, epithelial ultrastructure, and brush-border fatty-acid composition in rats fed a low-linoleate diet. This extends the question beyond skin to the gut.

Stroud and colleagues {{< cite "stroud2009" >}} found skin and intestinal ulceration in mice unable to convert LA into its longer-chain products; dietary arachidonic acid prevented those lesions. The parent fatty acid and the downstream products therefore need to be distinguished. Neither experiment directly establishes a single requirement governing differentiation and proliferation in **all** epithelial cells, or quantifies intestinal permeability in humans.

#### An example involving saturated phospholipids

**A specific experimental connection, not yet a general rule.** Nakamura, Kawamoto, and Akino {{< cite "nakamura1980" >}} studied lung phosphatidylcholine in EFA-deficient rats. They found reduced palmitate in this lipid, fewer dipalmitoyl molecules, altered incorporation of labeled palmitate, and altered alveolar phosphatidylcholine turnover. Dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine is an important component of lung surfactant.

This provides a concrete example of EFA status affecting the processing and distribution of a saturated fatty acid in a functional membrane lipid. It does not demonstrate that all saturated-fat digestion, oxidation, or metabolism requires EFAs in the same way. The original note is broader than the experiment.

### 8. Bone, Skeletal Muscle, Cartilage, and Connective Tissue

#### Bone remodeling: osteoblast/osteoclast PGE₂ signaling and human fracture cohorts

**Direct animal bone biochemistry; human observational associations depend on n-6:n-3 balance and confounders.** Bone remodeling depends on local prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂), which stimulates osteoblastic bone formation at physiological concentrations but drives osteoclastogenesis and resorption when chronically elevated. Watkins and colleagues {{< cite "watkins2000" >}} varied dietary n-6:n-3 PUFA ratios in growing rats and demonstrated that altering dietary lipid balance shifted bone-compartment fatty acid composition, reduced ex vivo bone PGE₂ production at lower n-6:n-3 ratios, and improved markers of bone formation.

Human cohort studies highlight the need for caution when moving from rodent bone ratios to human fractures. In the Women's Health Initiative, Orchard and colleagues {{< cite "orchard2013" >}} measured red-blood-cell fatty acids in postmenopausal women and found that higher RBC n-3 PUFAs were associated with lower hip fracture risk, whereas a higher RBC **n-6:n-3 ratio** nearly doubled hip fracture risk (HR = 1.96, highest vs. lowest tertile)—notably without changing bone mineral density (BMD). In contrast, in the Framingham Osteoporosis Study, Farina and colleagues {{< cite "farina2012" >}} measured plasma phosphatidylcholine fatty acids and found that initial inverse associations between plasma linoleic acid and femoral-neck BMD or hip fracture risk were largely attenuated after adjusting for body mass index (BMI). These findings suggest that any skeletal risk associated with omega-6 status in humans is likely tied to the overall n-6:n-3 balance and body-composition context rather than an isolated toxic effect of dietary LA on bone mineralization.

#### Skeletal muscle phospholipid composition, insulin sensitivity, and lean-tissue partitioning

**Direct human biopsy and randomized feeding trials, complementing cell-culture hypertrophy work (`markworth2013`).** In a landmark human biopsy study, Borkman and colleagues {{< cite "borkman1993" >}} analyzed skeletal-muscle phospholipids from coronary-surgery patients and from healthy men undergoing euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamps; higher proportions of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids in muscle membranes correlated positively with whole-body insulin sensitivity, whereas saturated-fat enrichment correlated with insulin resistance.

Controlled human dietary interventions support a causal benefit of replacing saturated fat with LA-rich PUFA. In a randomized crossover trial, Summers and colleagues {{< cite "summers2002" >}} substituted dietary saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat and observed improved whole-body insulin sensitivity and reduced abdominal subcutaneous/visceral fat distribution without major body-weight differences. In the 7-week, double-blind, randomized **LIPOGAIN** overfeeding trial (+750 kcal/day in muffins), Rosqvist and colleagues {{< cite "rosqvist2014" >}} compared palmitic-acid-rich palm oil against linoleic-acid-rich sunflower oil in healthy adults: at identical ~3% total weight gain, saturated-fat overfeeding doubled visceral fat accumulation and markedly increased liver fat, whereas LA-rich PUFA overfeeding produced nearly a threefold greater gain in **lean tissue mass** and avoided hepatic fat accumulation.

These trials provide strong human evidence that dietary LA-rich PUFA behaves distinctly from saturated fat in muscle-lipid partitioning and insulin action during both isocaloric substitution and overfeeding. They do not, however, validate the older note's claim that LA is directly required for distinct myofibrillar vs. sarcoplasmic hypertrophy {{< cite "markworth2013" >}}, nor do they prove that chronic high-LA overfeeding is benign over multi-year timescales.

#### Articular cartilage, osteoarthritis, ligament fibroblasts, and cutaneous ECM repair

**Animal injury models and in vitro explant/fibroblast data; human clinical translation remains unproven.** Beyond the developmental Mead-acid patterns observed in immature cartilage {{< cite "cleland1995" >}}, experimental joint models indicate that high dietary omega-6 loads can worsen inflammatory cartilage breakdown. Wu and colleagues {{< cite "wu2015" >}} fed weight-matched mice diets high in saturated fat, n-6 PUFA, or n-3 PUFA prior to surgical joint destabilization and ear-punch wounding: diets rich in saturated fat or n-6 PUFA accelerated post-traumatic osteoarthritis severity and slowed ear-wound healing compared with n-3–supplemented diets, independent of body weight. Consistent with this in vitro, Bastiaansen-Jenniskens and colleagues {{< cite "bastiaansen2013" >}} reported that under inflammatory stimulation in cartilage explants, monounsaturated and saturated fatty acids reduced cartilage matrix destruction, whereas linoleic acid exerted a pro-inflammatory effect.

Similarly, in connective-tissue fibroblasts, Hankenson and colleagues {{< cite "hankenson2000" >}} found that treating medial collateral ligament fibroblasts with arachidonic acid decreased collagen synthesis, whereas n-3 EPA markedly upregulated collagen production and monolayer wound migration via altered PGE₂ and IL-6 signaling. In cutaneous wound healing—where frank EFAD severely impairs burn contraction and closure {{< cite "hulsey1980" >}}—topical fatty-acid comparisons in EFA-replete mice by Cardoso and colleagues {{< cite "cardoso2004" >}} showed that oleic acid (n-9) produced the fastest wound closure, linoleic acid (n-6) yielded a mild closure improvement alongside a 48-hour nitric-oxide peak, and α-linolenic acid (n-3) delayed closure while driving dense early extracellular-matrix deposition. Taken together, connective-tissue repair depends heavily on whether the baseline state is *deficiency* (where lacking EFAs cripples repair) versus *replete or high-fat injury* (where excess n-6/AA signaling can promote catabolism in cartilage and ligament fibroblasts relative to n-3 or n-9 lipids).

#### Skeletal muscle: satellite-cell regeneration (`PGE2`/EP4), myoblast LA dose-response, exercise phospholipid remodeling, & `12,13-DiHOME`

**Direct mouse stem-cell genetics, myoblast cell biology, and human exercise biopsy/metabolomics studies (deepening `markworth2013`, `borkman1993`, `stanford2018`).**
- **Satellite cell proliferation & regeneration (`COX-2`/`PGE2`/`EP4`):** Beyond in vitro C2C12 hypertrophy {{< cite "markworth2013" >}}, in vivo mouse genetics prove that AA-derived prostaglandins are indispensable for skeletal muscle repair. Bondesen and colleagues {{< cite "bondesen2004" >}} showed that genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of **COX-2** markedly impaired myoblast proliferation, myofiber growth, and early skeletal muscle regeneration after injury. Ho and colleagues {{< cite "ho2017" >}} pinpointed the stem-cell mechanism: acute injury triggers a transient **PGE₂** surge that signals directly through the **EP4** receptor on muscle stem cells (satellite cells), activating cAMP/Nurr1 signaling to drive rapid MuSC proliferation, accelerate regeneration, and restore muscle force—explaining why chronic high-dose NSAIDs can blunt muscle repair.
- **Direct LA dose-response in cultured myoblasts:** Exposing skeletal myoblasts directly to unesterified LA yields concentration-dependent effects. Hurley and colleagues {{< cite "hurley2006" >}} reported in L6 skeletal myoblasts that low concentrations of LA (and *cis*-9, *trans*-11 CLA) stimulated myogenic differentiation, whereas higher concentrations (and *trans*-10, *cis*-12 CLA) inhibited it. Similarly, Lee and colleagues {{< cite "lee2009" >}} showed in C2C12 myoblasts that LA modulated proliferation and differentiation via ERK1/2 and JNK phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner.
- **Exercise-induced skeletal muscle phospholipid remodeling:** Human muscle membrane lipids do not merely passively reflect dietary fat (`borkman1993`); physical activity actively remodels them. Andersson and colleagues {{< cite "andersson2000" >}} compared trained and untrained young men and found distinct skeletal-muscle phospholipid fatty-acid profiles (including higher unsaturation and altered n-6/n-3 partitioning in trained muscle). Crucially, using a within-subject **one-legged endurance training** design on a controlled diet, Helge and colleagues {{< cite "helge2001" >}} proved that exercise training *itself*—independent of diet—remodels human vastus lateralis phospholipid fatty-acid composition (increasing oleic acid 18:1 and DHA 22:6 while shifting linoleic acid 18:2 proportions alongside higher oxidative capacity).
- **`12,13-DiHOME` exercise signaling in skeletal muscle (building on `stanford2018`):** Stanford and colleagues {{< cite "stanford2018" >}} established that acute endurance exercise in both humans and mice triggers brown adipose tissue release of the LA-derived CYP/sEH oxylipin **12,13-DiHOME**, which acts directly on skeletal muscle to stimulate fatty-acid uptake and mitochondrial oxidation via FATP1/CD36 translocation. Two key human studies add essential metabolic context: Nieman and colleagues {{< cite "nieman2019" >}} showed in human cyclists completing a 75-km bout that prolonged intense exercise robustly elevates CYP-generated LA oxylipins (including DiHOMEs), and that **acute carbohydrate ingestion** during exercise sharply attenuates this post-exercise oxylipin surge—linking LA-oxylipin mobilization directly to lipolytic fuel demand. In a large human cross-sectional cohort (*n* > 2,200), Vasan and colleagues {{< cite "vasan2019" >}} further found that higher resting plasma **12,13-DiHOME** was inversely associated with BMI, fat mass, fasting triglycerides, and insulin resistance.

**Evidence boundaries:** In vivo stem-cell studies (`bondesen2004`, `ho2017`) prove that acute local `PGE₂`/EP4 signaling is required for optimal muscle regeneration, and human biopsy/exercise trials (`helge2001`, `stanford2018`, `nieman2019`) show that skeletal muscle dynamically remodels its LA/PUFA phospholipids and responds to LA-derived `12,13-DiHOME` during endurance fuel demand. None of these findings prove that high-LA diets accelerate human muscle hypertrophy or athletic performance beyond normal dietary sufficiency, and in vitro myoblast responses (`hurley2006`, `lee2009`) reflect unesterified free-fatty-acid exposure rather than whole-food nutrition.

#### Tendon stem-cell degeneration (`PGE2`) and osteoblast/osteoclast mechanisms in bone

**Direct animal knockout, stem-cell, and human mesenchymal stromal cell studies (deepening `watkins2000`, `orchard2013`, `farina2012`, `hankenson2000`).**
- **Tendon overuse and non-tenocyte differentiation:** Complementing ligament fibroblast collagen suppression by AA {{< cite "hankenson2000" >}}, Zhang and Wang {{< cite "zhang2010" >}} demonstrated a cellular mechanism linking repetitive mechanical overuse to degenerative tendinopathy: cyclic stretching of tendons markedly elevated local **PGE₂** production, and elevated `PGE₂` pushed tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSCs) away from tenocyte differentiation toward **non-tenocyte lineages** (adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteocytes)—explaining the lipid accumulation, proteoglycan buildup, and calcific metaplasia characteristic of chronic tendinopathy. (Lower, physiological `PGE₂` levels in acute tendon injury play a normal inflammatory-initiation role, underscoring a dose/duration threshold.)
- **The EP4 paradox in bone (osteoclast resorption vs. osteoblast formation):** Two landmark Japanese genetic studies resolved the biphasic bone effects of omega-6 `PGE₂` noted in Pass 1 (`watkins2000`):
1. **Resorption (`EP4` → RANKL → osteoclasts):** Suzawa and colleagues {{< cite "suzawa2000" >}} used selective EP1–EP4 agonists and *Ep4*-knockout mice to prove that `PGE₂` stimulates **osteoclastic bone resorption** predominantly through the **EP4** receptor (with a minor EP2 contribution) via cAMP-dependent induction of RANKL (osteoclast differentiation factor) in osteoblastic stromal cells.
2. **Formation (`EP4` → osteoblasts):** Using the very same receptor family, Yoshida and colleagues {{< cite "yoshida2002" >}} proved that selective **EP4 receptor agonist** administration stimulated *de novo* **osteoblastic bone formation** and reversed bone loss in ovariectomized and immobilized rodents—an anabolic effect abolished in *Ep4*-knockout mice.
- **Human mesenchymal stem cell lineage commitment:** At the precursor-cell level, Casado-Díaz and colleagues {{< cite "casadodiaz2013" >}} exposed human bone-marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) to omega-6 arachidonic acid versus omega-3 EPA/DHA: AA suppressed osteoblastogenesis and promoted **adipogenesis** (via PPARγ and down-regulated osteogenic transcription), whereas EPA and DHA did not impair osteoblast differentiation—providing a direct cellular mechanism for marrow adiposity and bone fragility under skewed n-6:n-3 signaling.

**Evidence boundaries:** Genetic EP4 studies (`suzawa2000`, `yoshida2002`) prove that AA-derived `PGE₂` is an intrinsic bidirectional coupling factor of bone remodeling rather than a one-way bone toxin: timing, local concentration, and pulsatile vs. chronic exposure determine whether EP4 drives net anabolism or resorption. Likewise, tendon stem-cell (`zhang2010`) and hMSC (`casadodiaz2013`) experiments reveal real cellular vulnerability to sustained excess `PGE₂`/AA signaling, while human fracture cohorts (`orchard2013`, `farina2012`) confirm that real-world skeletal risk depends on the overall n-6:n-3 ratio and body-composition context rather than isolated LA intake.

#### Muscle growth signaling

**Cell-culture evidence; necessity in humans is not established.** Markworth and Cameron-Smith {{< cite "markworth2013" >}}, added arachidonic acid to cultured mouse muscle cells and observed larger myotubes and increased protein accumulation. Blocking cyclooxygenase-2 blunted the growth response, implicating downstream prostaglandin production.

This makes fatty-acid-derived signaling relevant to muscle growth. However, adding AA to cells is not the same experiment as demonstrating that dietary LA and ALA are required for a particular form of training-induced hypertrophy. The study did not separately establish myofibrillar and sarcoplasmic hypertrophy in humans. That specific claim remains unverified here.

#### Mead acid—established synthesis, unverified depletion story

**The biochemical observation is supported; the proposed joint mechanism is not.** Ichi and colleagues {{< cite "ichi2014" >}} identified enzymes and pathways involved in making Mead acid from oleic acid, explaining its appearance during EFA deficiency. This is evidence of synthesis, not evidence that tissues must be stripped of pre-existing Mead acid.

Cleland and colleagues {{< cite "cleland1995" >}} found distinctive fatty-acid patterns in human and sheep cartilage, including more Mead acid in fetal than mature cartilage. They did not measure cartilage as the largest whole-body reservoir or demonstrate its mobilization after oleic-acid stores were exhausted. Neither paper links that proposed sequence to joint calcification or degeneration. Those parts of the note remain speculative.

## Part III: Receptors, Signaling, Oxidation Chemistry, Genetics, Immunity, and Disease

### 9. Direct Free Fatty Acid Receptors, Lipid Chaperones, Nuclear Sensors, and ER Stress

#### The Cold- and Exercise-Induced Lipokine 12,13-DiHOME vs. EpOME/DiHOME Toxicity

**Strong mechanistic evidence in mice and consistent human biomarker responses; not a justification for indiscriminate dietary LA loading.** Cytochrome P450 epoxygenases convert LA into 9,10-EpOME and 12,13-EpOME, which soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH) hydrolyzes into their corresponding diols, 9,10-DiHOME and 12,13-DiHOME. Lynes and colleagues {{< cite "lynes2017" >}} showed that acute cold exposure elevates circulating **12,13-DiHOME** in both humans and mice; secreted by brown adipose tissue (BAT), 12,13-DiHOME acts autocrinely and paracrinely to promote translocation of fatty-acid transporters (FATP1 and CD36) and increase fatty-acid uptake into BAT for thermogenesis, lowering serum triglycerides in mice.

Stanford and colleagues {{< cite "stanford2018" >}} extended this finding to physical activity: a single bout of moderate exercise increased circulating 12,13-DiHOME across diverse human cohorts (men and women, young and old, active and sedentary). In mice, surgical removal of BAT abolished the exercise-induced rise, and exogenous 12,13-DiHOME acutely stimulated fatty-acid uptake and oxidation specifically in skeletal muscle without altering glucose uptake. Pinckard and colleagues {{< cite "pinckard2021" >}} further reported an endocrine role for BAT-derived 12,13-DiHOME in enhancing cardiomyocyte respiration and calcium cycling via neuronal nitric oxide synthase (NOS1), improving cardiac contractility in mouse models.

These physiological findings sit directly alongside an older toxicology literature on the same enzymatic branch. Moghaddam and colleagues {{< cite "moghaddam1997" >}} demonstrated that **leukotoxin** (9,10-EpOME, generated by neutrophils during severe burn injury and acute respiratory distress) is actually a protoxin: cytotoxicity to pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells required its conversion by epoxide hydrolase into **leukotoxin diol** (9,10-DiHOME). Together, these studies show that CYP/sEH metabolites of LA are neither uniformly toxic nor uniformly beneficial. Context, regioisomer, concentration, and tissue source separate a regulated BAT-derived metabolic signal from an acute-injury toxin. None of these experiments establishes that raising dietary LA in humans increases physiological 12,13-DiHOME signaling or reproduces the benefits of cold exposure or exercise.

#### HODEs, KODEs (Oxo-ODEs), and Nuclear Receptors (PPARγ and PPARα)

**Direct biochemical and structural evidence; downstream physiological meaning depends on tissue and cell state.** Forman, Chen, and Evans {{< cite "forman1997" >}} established that unoxidized polyunsaturated fatty acids, including LA, can bind and activate **PPARα** and **PPARδ** directly, linking dietary fatty-acid supply to transcriptional programs governing fatty-acid oxidation. For **PPARγ**, however, oxidation of LA sharply increases signaling potency. Nagy and colleagues {{< cite "nagy1998" >}} demonstrated that oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) regulates macrophage gene expression—including induction of the scavenger receptor CD36—through ligand activation of PPARγ, and identified **9-HODE** and **13-HODE** as major endogenous PPARγ ligands within oxLDL.

Structural biology later clarified why keto/oxo derivatives of LA (**9-oxo-ODE / 9-KODE** and **13-oxo-ODE / 13-KODE**) are especially potent. Itoh and colleagues {{< cite "itoh2008" >}} solved crystal structures of the PPARγ ligand-binding domain showing that its large pocket can simultaneously accommodate two fatty-acid molecules and that electrophilic conjugated oxo-fatty acids form a **covalent bond** with a specific cysteine residue inside the binding pocket, stabilizing the receptor and driving robust transcriptional activation.

What these papers establish is that HODEs and KODEs are bona fide endogenous transcriptional ligands, not merely inert debris of lipid peroxidation. What they do *not* establish is a single valence for PPARγ activation: in atherosclerotic plaques, oxLDL-derived HODEs can amplify macrophage lipid uptake and foam-cell formation {{< cite "nagy1998" >}}, whereas in other contexts enzymatic 12/15-lipoxygenase production of 13-HODE participates in regulated cytokine signaling {{< cite "huang1999" >}} (see Section 4.1).

#### OXLAMs, TRPV1, and Nociception (Inflammatory & Thermal Pain)

**Direct mechanistic evidence in rodents and supportive human RCT data; does not prove dietary LA alone causes chronic pain.** Patwardhan and colleagues {{< cite "patwardhan2009" >}} demonstrated that oxidized linoleic acid metabolites (specifically 9-HODE and 13-HODE) generated in the spinal cord activate the capsaicin-sensitive ion channel **TRPV1**, contributing to central sensitization and inflammatory mechanical/thermal hyperalgesia in rodents. In a companion mechanistic study, Patwardhan and colleagues {{< cite "patwardhan2010" >}} showed that noxious heat itself generates OXLAMs in peripheral skin, which then gate TRPV1 to trigger nociception; blocking OXLAM formation or neutralizing 9- and 13-HODE attenuated heat-evoked TRPV1 responses and pain behavior in rodents.

Crucially, this pathway has been tested in human dietary intervention trials. Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2012" >}} showed that lowering dietary LA for 12 weeks significantly reduced circulating 9- and 13-HODE and 9- and 13-oxo-ODE in human plasma. In a 16-week randomized controlled trial of 182 adults with frequent migraine, Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2021" >}} found that increasing dietary EPA/DHA reduced headache hours and days compared with a control diet, and that **combining high omega-3 with lowered dietary LA** ($\le 1.8\%$ of energy) produced greater improvements in headache frequency and quality-of-life impact than raising omega-3 on a typical LA background (~7% of energy). This provides unusually strong translational continuity from molecular ion-channel biology to a human clinical endpoint. It does not establish that dietary LA reduction alone (without concurrent omega-3 elevation) produces the same clinical effect, or that the migraine findings generalize to non-headache pain syndromes.

#### GPR132 (G2A) as an Oxidative Lipid Sensor

**Clear receptor identification in cell systems; disease causation in humans is not established.** Obinata and colleagues {{< cite "obinata2005" >}} identified **9-HODE**—along with several related oxidized derivatives of LA and AA—as high-affinity endogenous agonists of the previously orphan G-protein-coupled receptor **G2A (GPR132)**. In receptor-expressing cells, 9-HODE triggered intracellular calcium mobilization, inositol phosphate accumulation, cAMP inhibition, and MAPK activation. Because GPR132 is heavily expressed in macrophages, lymphocytes, and keratinocytes, and 9-HODE accumulates in oxidative tissue environments such as atherosclerotic lesions and UV-stressed skin, G2A has been proposed as a surface sensor translating extracellular lipid oxidation into chemotactic and stress responses. However, G2A also displays proton-sensing and broader oxidized-lipid responsiveness, and receptor pharmacology in transfected cell lines does not by itself establish that dietary LA intake controls human GPR132 signaling in vivo.

#### NRF2/ARE Adaptive Defense Beyond EKODE

**Well-supported biochemical mechanism in cell models; does not make dietary LA an antioxidant.** Building on the existing note citing Wang and colleagues {{< cite "wang2009" >}} (which showed that the LA oxidation product EKODE, but not unoxidized LA itself, activates NRF2-dependent antioxidant response element [ARE] transcription in neurons), the broader redox-signaling literature explains the underlying chemical mechanism. Levonen and colleagues {{< cite "levonen2004" >}} demonstrated that electrophilic lipid oxidation products bearing $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated carbonyl moieties covalently modify nucleophilic cysteine residues on regulatory proteins—most notably **Keap1**—releasing **NRF2** to upregulate glutathione synthesis and phase-II cytoprotective enzymes. Consistent with this electrophilic mechanism, Ko and colleagues {{< cite "ko2022" >}} reported that **13-KODE** (13-oxo-ODE) activated **NRF2/HO-1** signaling while suppressing NF-κB and MAPK activation in LPS-stimulated murine macrophages.

This clarifies a recurring conceptual confusion: low-level generation of electrophilic OXLAMs can function as an adaptive feedback alarm that switches on endogenous antioxidant defenses (such as heme oxygenase-1 and glutathione synthesis), whereas excessive or unbuffered lipid peroxidation overwhelms those defenses and causes covalent protein damage (compare {{< cite "carlson2016" >}}). Cell-culture exposure to micromolar keto-linoleates does not show that increasing dietary LA strengthens antioxidant capacity in humans.

#### `FFAR1` (GPR40) and `FFAR4` (GPR120): β-Cell Insulin Amplification, Gut Incretins, Biased Signaling, and Adipose/Macrophage Tone

**Definitive receptor-pharmacology and mouse-genetic evidence; acute insulinotropic sensing does not equate to long-term metabolic protection from chronic free-fatty-acid excess.** Itoh and colleagues {{< cite "itoh2003" >}} deorphanized **GPR40 (`FFAR1`)**, showing it is abundantly expressed in pancreatic β-cells and activated by medium- and long-chain free fatty acids—including **linoleic acid**, oleic acid, and palmitic acid—to amplify **glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS)** via G$_{q/11}$/Ca$^{2+}$ signaling. Edfalk, Steneberg, and Edlund {{< cite "edfalk2008" >}} demonstrated that `FFAR1` is also expressed in gastrointestinal enteroendocrine **L-cells** and **K-cells**, where genetic deletion of *Gpr40* impaired fatty-acid-stimulated secretion of the incretin hormones **GLP-1** and **GIP**. Thus, `FFAR1` coordinates an acute lipid-triggered enteroinsular axis at both the gut and the islet. What these studies do *not* establish is that chronic elevation of circulating LA improves β-cell health; in fact, prolonged free fatty acid exposure engages intracellular lipotoxicity and glucolipotoxicity pathways (Section 2.1), and pharmacological `FFAR1` agonists have faced clinical safety hurdles (e.g., hepatotoxicity) unrelated to dietary LA.

**Strong mechanistic and structural biology evidence, alongside human loss-of-function genetics; ω-3 PUFAs and LA do not signal identically through `FFAR4`.** Hirasawa and colleagues {{< cite "hirasawa2005" >}} identified **GPR120 (`FFAR4`)** in intestinal enteroendocrine cells as a receptor for unsaturated long-chain free fatty acids (including α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid) that stimulates **GLP-1** secretion and inhibits apoptosis in enteroendocrine cell lines. In adipose tissue and macrophages, Oh and colleagues {{< cite "oh2010" >}} showed that activation of `FFAR4` recruits **$\beta$-arrestin-2**, sequestering **TAB1** and blocking **TAK1**-mediated NF-κB and JNK inflammatory cascades while enhancing GLUT4 translocation and insulin sensitivity. Notably, Oh et al. characterized `FFAR4` primarily as an **ω-3 fatty acid receptor** (potently activated by DHA and EPA), noting weaker or distinct anti-inflammatory efficacy for ω-6 fatty acids compared with long-chain ω-3 PUFAs.

Human genetics confirms that `FFAR4` loss impairs systemic energy balance: Ichimura and colleagues {{< cite "ichimura2012" >}} showed that *Gpr120*-deficient mice fed a high-fat diet develop obesity, hepatic steatosis, and insulin resistance, and identified a deleterious human loss-of-function variant (**p.R270H**) that impairs `FFAR4` signaling and increases human obesity risk in European cohorts. Explaining *why* different unsaturated fatty acids elicit divergent cellular outcomes at the exact same receptor, Mao and colleagues {{< cite "mao2023" >}} solved cryo-EM structures of `FFAR4` bound to multiple unsaturated fatty acids—including **linoleic acid (LA)**, oleic acid, and ω-3 EPA/DHA—and demonstrated that the number and positions of *cis* double bonds dictate specific aromatic π–π interactions inside the ligand-binding pocket, producing **biased signaling** across distinct G-protein subtypes (G$_q$, G$_i$, G$_s$) versus $\beta$-arrestin recruitment. Consequently, while LA clearly activates `FFAR4` (for example in gut incretin and lingual fat-sensing pathways), claiming that LA reproduces the full ω-3 macrophage anti-inflammatory phenotype of `FFAR4` overstates the receptor pharmacology {{< cite "oh2010" >}}, {{< cite "mao2023" >}}.

#### Lingual Fat-Taste Perception: `CD36` and `GPR40`/`GPR120` Detection of Linoleic Acid on the Tongue

**Strong causal evidence in rodents and supportive psychophysical/genetic evidence in humans; does not prove that oral LA perception alone explains human obesity.** Dietary fat perception was historically attributed to texture (viscosity) and olfaction. Laugerette and colleagues {{< cite "laugerette2005" >}} overturned this view by showing that the fatty-acid translocase **CD36** is specifically localized to the apical membrane of circumvallate taste-bud cells on the rodent tongue. Applying free **linoleic acid** (a long-chain unsaturated fatty acid liberated rapidly from dietary triglycerides by lingual lipase) directly to the tongue triggered rapid **cephalic-phase pancreatobiliary secretions** (even with esophageal ligation preventing swallowing) and strong spontaneous preference; targeted deletion of *Cd36* abolished both the LA preference and the digestive reflex.

Cartoni and colleagues {{< cite "cartoni2010" >}} established that G-protein-coupled lipid sensors operate in parallel on the tongue: **GPR120 (`FFAR4`)** (expressed largely in Type II taste cells) and **GPR40 (`FFAR1`)** (largely Type I cells) mediate gustatory nerve responses and behavioral preference for long-chain fatty acids including **linoleic acid** and oleic acid, with knockout of either receptor blunting fat preference and chorda tympani / glossopharyngeal nerve firing. Translating this biology to humans, Pepino and colleagues {{< cite "pepino2012" >}} demonstrated in obese adults that a common promoter polymorphism reducing **`CD36` expression (`rs1761667`)** lowers oral detection sensitivity to oleic acid and triolein, and that inhibiting lingual lipase reduces sensitivity to triglycerides—confirming that free fatty acid release and `CD36`-mediated chemoreception contribute to human oral fat detection. What these studies do *not* prove is that LA is uniquely addictive compared with oleic acid or other long-chain unsaturated fatty acids, or that lowering oral sensitivity causally drives weight gain rather than vice versa.

#### Intracellular Lipid Chaperones: `FABP1`, `FABP4`, and `FABP5` Directing LA and Oxylipins to Nuclear and Inflammatory Targets

**Strong biochemical, structural, and mouse-genetic evidence; human dietary-LA specificity varies sharply by isoform and tissue.** Because free long-chain fatty acids have nanomolar aqueous solubility and detergent toxicity, intracellular trafficking depends on cytosolic **fatty acid-binding proteins (FABPs)**:
- **`FABP1` (L-FABP, liver and intestine):** Unlike most FABPs that bind a single ligand, `FABP1` possesses an unusually large cavity capable of binding two long-chain fatty acids (including LA) simultaneously as well as acyl-CoAs and oxidized lipids. Wolfrum and colleagues {{< cite "wolfrum2001" >}} demonstrated that `FABP1` physically interacts with **PPARα** and **PPARγ** and translocates fatty-acid ligands into the nucleus in direct proportion to `FABP1` expression, coupling hepatic free-fatty-acid influx to PPARα-driven β-oxidation genes.
- **`FABP4` (`aP2`, adipocytes and macrophages):** Hotamisligil and colleagues {{< cite "hotamisligil1996" >}} showed that deleting *Fabp4* in mice uncouples dietary obesity from insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and TNF-α upregulation. Tan and colleagues {{< cite "tan2002" >}} further showed that `FABP4` selectively cooperates with nuclear **PPARγ**, relocating to the nucleus upon ligand binding to channel fatty acids/oxylipins directly to PPARγ. In macrophages and adipocytes, `FABP4` integrates free-fatty-acid availability with ER stress and pro-inflammatory signaling rather than acting specifically as an LA-only sensor.
- **`FABP5` (`E-FABP`, epidermis, macrophages, and epithelial tumors):** Tan and colleagues {{< cite "tan2002" >}} established that `FABP5` selectively partners with nuclear **PPARβ/δ**. Solving the structural basis of this selectivity, Armstrong and colleagues {{< cite "armstrong2014" >}} showed that *cis*-polyunsaturated fatty acids—specifically **linoleic acid** and arachidonic acid—trigger an allosteric conformational shift between the ligand-sensing $\beta2$ loop and a tertiary **nuclear localization signal (NLS)** in the $\alpha$-helical cap of `FABP5`, driving nuclear import and **PPARβ/δ** transactivation; saturated fatty acids fail to stabilize this loop and act as non-activating/inhibitory ligands. Physiologically, `FABP5` participates in epidermal barrier maturation: Owada and colleagues {{< cite "owada2002" >}} showed that *Fabp5*-deficient mice exhibit altered water permeability barrier recovery after tape stripping (complementing LA's structural esterification into acylceramides; compare {{< cite "zheng2011" >}}). Yet in immune cells and cancer, `FABP5` can amplify pathology: Zhang and colleagues {{< cite "zhang2015" >}} found that macrophage `FABP5` drives high-fat-diet-induced skin inflammation via lipid-droplet/IL-1β signaling, and Koundouros and colleagues {{< cite "koundouros2025" >}} demonstrated that LA binding to `FABP5` directly activates **mTORC1** in triple-negative breast cancer (detailed in Sections 2.2 and 4.3).

Together, these papers prove that intracellular trafficking proteins determine *which* signaling receptor sees LA. They do *not* prove that dietary LA universally activates harmful `FABP5` pathways in healthy tissues where `FABP5` expression is low or balanced by competing chaperones.

#### Direct Nuclear-Receptor Modulation: `LXR` Antagonism by PUFAs and `HNF-4α` Endogenous Linoleic Acid Binding

**Definitive molecular and cellular biochemistry; explains part of PUFA-driven suppression of hepatic lipogenesis.** Building on the finding that dietary PUFAs suppress hepatic `SREBP-1` expression ({{< cite "xu1999" >}}, already in `new_efa_references.yaml`), Ou and colleagues {{< cite "ou2001" >}} demonstrated in cultured hepatocytes and cell-free binding assays that unsaturated fatty acids—including **linoleic acid**, arachidonic acid, EPA, and DHA (with potency rising with unsaturation, whereas saturated fatty acids were inactive)—act as **competitive antagonists of Liver X Receptors (`LXRα` and `LXRβ`)**. By displacing endogenous oxysterol agonists from the LXR ligand-binding domain, PUFAs inhibit LXR-driven transcription of the **`SREBP-1c`** gene, suppressing hepatic fatty-acid and triglyceride synthesis. This complements `Fads`/`Elovl5` feedback regulation (Pass 1, Section 2.1) and provides a direct mechanistic explanation for why substituting LA-rich PUFAs for saturated fat reduces hepatic fat accumulation in human overfeeding trials. It does *not* show that LA is more potent than AA, EPA, or DHA at antagonizing LXR (in fact, C20–C22 PUFAs are stronger LXR antagonists on a molar basis).

**Direct affinity-isolation mass spectrometry proof in native mammalian liver; transcriptional modulation by acute LA binding remains subtle and context-dependent.** Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (**`HNF-4α`**) is a master regulator of liver, pancreas, and intestinal differentiation whose mutations cause **MODY1** (maturity-onset diabetes of the young 1) and whose ligand pocket was long thought to be constitutively locked by bacterial fatty acids in crystal structures. Using unbiased affinity isolation coupled to GC-MS (AIMS) directly from mammalian cells and mouse livers, Yuan and colleagues {{< cite "yuan2009" >}} proved that native mammalian `HNF-4α` is selectively and reversibly occupied *in vivo* by **linoleic acid (18:2n-6)**. Crucially, hepatic `HNF-4α` occupancy by LA was high in **fed mice** and fell dramatically during **fasting**, and MODY1 disease mutants exhibited impaired LA binding. Yet Yuan et al. carefully bounded their conclusion: acute addition or depletion of LA in human colon carcinoma cells produced **minimal global transcriptional changes** across `HNF-4α` target genes, indicating that LA serves primarily as a **reversible structural cofactor** stabilizing `HNF-4α` conformation rather than a classical high-gain on/off transcriptional switch. Whether chronic dietary LA excess or depletion alters human `HNF-4α` signaling in vivo remains unresolved.

#### Saturated vs. Unsaturated Fatty Acids at the ER Bilayer: Transmembrane UPR Activation (`IRE1α`/`PERK`), Lipid-Droplet Buffering, and `LPCAT3` Remodeling

**Foundational mechanistic evidence across mammalian cell lines, reconstituted liposomes, and mouse livers; does not mean high-dose LA is non-toxic in every cell type.**
- **Triglyceride droplet buffering (`DGAT` pathway):** Listenberger and colleagues {{< cite "listenberger2003" >}} established a paradigm shift in lipotoxicity: excess **palmitate** is poorly incorporated into triglycerides and rapidly triggers **lipoapoptosis**, whereas co-supplementation with **unsaturated fatty acids** (demonstrated primarily with oleate, and mechanistically shared by other *cis*-unsaturated fatty acids) rescues cells by channeling palmitate into inert **triglyceride-rich lipid droplets**. Conversely, blocking triglyceride synthesis (`Dgat1` deficiency) renders even unsaturated fatty acids lipotoxic—proving that neutral-lipid storage is a protective sink rather than the cause of acute cell death.
- **Direct ER stress in hepatocytes and palmitate–linoleate interaction:** Wei and colleagues {{< cite "wei2006" >}} showed in H4IIE liver cells that saturated fatty acids (palmitate, stearate) activate the **unfolded protein response (UPR)**—phosphorylating eIF2α, splicing *XBP1*, and inducing *GRP78* and pro-apoptotic *CHOP*—independently of ceramide synthesis. Examining palmitic and **linoleic acid** side by side and in combination in H4IIE hepatoma cells, Zhang and colleagues {{< cite "zhang2012" >}} observed an important nuance: while co-treating cells with linoleic acid attenuated palmitate-induced lipoapoptosis, **high-dose linoleic acid applied alone** also elevated intracellular Ca$^{2+}$ flux, ER-stress markers (*CHOP*, *GRP78*), and apoptosis—illustrating that LA's rescue of saturated lipotoxicity is concentration- and ratio-dependent rather than infinitely benign.
- **Direct transmembrane lipid-saturation sensing (`IRE1α` and `PERK`):** Two landmark 2013 studies proved that the ER senses bilayer stiffness directly rather than only luminal unfolded proteins. Volmer, van der Ploeg, and Ron {{< cite "volmer2013" >}} deleted the luminal unfolded-protein-sensing domains of **IRE1α** and **PERK** and showed that both truncated transducers still activated robustly in response to membrane lipid saturation (in cells and in reconstituted liposomes), strictly requiring their **transmembrane helices**. Independently, Kitai and colleagues {{< cite "kitai2013" >}} demonstrated that increasing ER phospholipid saturation (via palmitate loading or stearoyl-CoA desaturase inhibition) activates **IRE1α** *without* forming the high-order luminal clusters typical of proteotoxic stress, and that supplying unsaturated fatty acids restores bilayer fluidity and silences IRE1α activation.
- **Enzymatic PUFA incorporation into ER phosphatidylcholine (`LPCAT3`):** Connecting nuclear receptors directly to ER biophysics, Rong and colleagues {{< cite "rong2013" >}} demonstrated that LXR induces **lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (`LPCAT3`)**, the enzyme that preferentially esterifies **polyunsaturated fatty acids—specifically linoleate (18:2n-6) and arachidonate (20:4n-6)**—into the *sn*-2 position of ER phosphatidylcholine. Expressing `LPCAT3` prevented saturated-fat-induced ER stress and JNK/c-Src inflammatory activation in cultured cells and obese mouse livers, whereas hepatic *Lpcat3* knockdown stiffened ER membranes and exacerbated ER stress and inflammation.

**Evidence boundary:** These papers prove that ER membranes require an adequate fraction of kinked unsaturated acyl chains (including LA and AA via `LPCAT3`) to prevent biophysical stiffening, transmembrane `IRE1α`/`PERK` activation, and lipoapoptosis, and that unsaturated fatty acids detoxify saturated fatty acid overload by enabling triglyceride droplet sequestration. They do *not* establish that increasing dietary LA above adequate human intakes (~2–5% of energy) provides additional ER-stress protection in free-living humans, nor do they negate oxidative toxicity when unbuffered PUFAs undergo peroxidation (compare Section 3 and {{< cite "carlson2016" >}}).

#### Autophagy and `mTORC1`: Divergent Regulation of Nutrient Sensing and Self-Digestion by Linoleic Acid

- **Unsaturated fatty acids—including LA—can stimulate protective non-canonical autophagy *or* activate growth-promoting `mTORC1`, depending on cell type and `FABP5` expression**

**Strong mechanistic evidence in hepatocytes, epithelial cells, and *C. elegans*; context determines whether LA triggers autophagy or activates `mTORC1`.**
- **Hepatocyte autophagy vs. apoptosis:** Mei and colleagues {{< cite "mei2011" >}} demonstrated in HepG2 cells and primary mouse hepatocytes that saturated fatty acids (palmitate) suppress autophagic flux (via caspase-mediated cleavage of Beclin 1) and drive apoptosis, whereas unsaturated fatty acids promote lipid-droplet formation and **stimulate autophagy**, protecting hepatocytes from lipotoxicity.
- **Golgi-dependent non-canonical autophagy:** Niso-Santano and colleagues {{< cite "nisosantano2015" >}} revealed a conserved mechanistic bifurcation across yeast, nematodes, mice, and human cells: whereas palmitate activates canonical autophagy requiring `AMPK`, `PKR`, `JNK1`, and the `BECN1`/`PIK3C3` lipid-kinase complex, *cis*-unsaturated fatty acids (demonstrated prominently with oleate and shared across unsaturated fatty acids including linoleate) trigger a **non-canonical autophagic pathway** that bypasses `BECN1`/`PIK3C3` but strictly depends on an intact **Golgi apparatus** and LC3 lipidation machinery.
- **ω-6 PUFA–driven autophagy in organismal aging models:** O'Rourke and colleagues {{< cite "orourke2013" >}} showed in *Caenorhabditis elegans* and human epithelial cells that fasting-induced **ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids** (specifically γ-linolenic acid and arachidonic acid downstream of LA, as well as LA/ω-6 supplementation) activate autophagy and extend lifespan in *C. elegans*. This demonstrates conserved cross-talk between ω-6 PUFA mobilization and autophagic clearance, though nematode lifespan extension cannot be extrapolated to human dietary LA intake.
- **Direct LA sensing by `FABP5`–`mTORC1`:** In sharp contrast to autophagy induction (which is canonically opposed by `mTORC1`), Koundouros and colleagues {{< cite "koundouros2025" >}} discovered a direct lipid-sensing pathway in which **linoleic acid** binds **`FABP5`**, prompting `FABP5` to bind **Raptor** (the defining scaffold of **`mTORC1`**), enhance functional `mTORC1` assembly and substrate recruitment (S6K1/4E-BP1 phosphorylation), and drive cell proliferation specifically in **`FABP5`-high triple-negative breast cancer** cells and mouse xenografts (while receptor-positive breast cancer cells with low `FABP5` did not exhibit this LA-driven `mTORC1` surge).

**Conflict & synthesis:** Taken together, these four studies refute any simplistic claim that LA uniformly "activates" or "inhibits" `mTORC1` or autophagy. In hepatocytes and metabolically stressed cells, unsaturated fatty acids support non-canonical autophagy and lipid-droplet homeostasis {{< cite "mei2011" >}}, {{< cite "nisosantano2015" >}}, whereas in `FABP5`-overexpressing tumor cells, LA directly engages Raptor to hyperactivate `mTORC1` {{< cite "koundouros2025" >}}. Cellular `FABP5` abundance—not LA concentration alone—acts as the molecular switch between these fates.

### 10. Oxidized Linoleic Acid Metabolites (OXLAMs), Lipokines, and Oxidation Chemistry

#### Systematic Pathway Map: Enzymatic Oxygenases vs. Non-Enzymatic Free-Radical Autoxidation & Singlet Oxygen

**Definitive mechanistic enzymology and physical organic chemistry.** Four major enzymatic routes and two non-enzymatic routes oxidize LA in mammalian tissues:

Brash {{< cite "brash1999" >}} synthesized the catalytic architecture of lipoxygenases: non-heme iron dioxygenases that bind PUFAs in a precise head-to-tail orientation, abstract a pro-chiral hydrogen from the bis-allylic methylene, and insert $\text{O}_2$ antarafacially to yield single-enantiomer *(Z,E)* hydroperoxides (such as **13(*S*)-HpODE** by `15-LOX-1`, or **9(*R*)-HpODE** by epidermal `12R-LOX` {{< cite "zheng2011" >}}). Laneuville and colleagues {{< cite "laneuville1995" >}} mapped the fatty-acid substrate specificities of human `COX-1` and `COX-2`, demonstrating that `COX-2`'s wider active-site channel accommodates C18 fatty acids including **linoleic acid** and α-linolenic acid more readily than `COX-1`, generating stereochemically enriched **9(*R*)-HODE** and **13(*S*)-HODE** alongside classic AA-derived prostaglandins. Morisseau and Hammock {{< cite "morisseau2013" >}} reviewed the `CYP2C`/`CYP2J` epoxygenase and **soluble epoxide hydrolase (`sEH`)** branch: unlike arachidonate-derived EETs (which are vasodilatory/anti-inflammatory and inactivated by `sEH`), linoleate-derived **EpOMEs** are converted by `sEH` into **DiHOMEs** whose biological valence flips sharply by isomer, source, and concentration (compare `lynes2017`, `moghaddam1997`, `levan2019`, and `mcreynolds2021`).

#### Reactive Carbonyls and Structural Boundaries: `4-HNE`, `MDA`, `Acrolein`, vs. Arachidonate-Derived Isolevuglandins

**Definitive mechanistic organic chemistry; prevents common over-attribution of all lipid aldehydes solely to LA.** Yin, Xu, and Porter {{< cite "yin2011" >}} reviewed the free-radical autoxidation kinetics of PUFAs: relative oxidizability rises steeply with the number of bis-allylic methylene groups (LA has **1**, ALA has **2**, AA has **3**, DHA has **5**), meaning LA oxidizes *slower* per molecule than AA or DHA—yet because LA is by far the most abundant PUFA in human plasma lipoproteins (especially LDL cholesteryl esters) and adipose tissue, LA provides the largest quantitative substrate pool for systemic lipid peroxidation.

Secondary decomposition of LA hydroperoxides generates distinct electrophilic carbonyls—with strict structural boundaries:
- **4-Hydroxynonenal (`4-HNE`):** Esterbauer, Schaur, and Zollner {{< cite "esterbauer1991" >}} established `4-HNE` as the signature cytotoxic $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated aldehyde of **ω-6 PUFA** peroxidation (formed directly from both **linoleic acid** and arachidonic acid via C-9/C-13 hydroperoxide $\beta$-scission). `4-HNE` readily forms Michael adducts with cysteine, histidine, and lysine residues (including inhibiting mitochondrial cytochrome *c* oxidase, {{< cite "chen1998" >}}, already in `data/references.yaml`), while at low sub-micromolar concentrations triggering adaptive signaling such as Keap1–NRF2 (compare {{< cite "niki2009" >}}).
- **Malondialdehyde (`MDA`) and `Acrolein`:** As Esterbauer et al. {{< cite "esterbauer1991" >}} documented, canonical `MDA` formation via bicyclic endoperoxide cleavage requires PUFAs with **$\ge 3$ double bonds** (such as AA, ALA, EPA, DHA); LA (2 double bonds) yields far less `MDA` per mole than AA or DHA, though minor secondary pathways still contribute. Similarly, Uchida and colleagues {{< cite "uchida1998" >}} proved that **acrolein**—the strongest electrophile of the $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated aldehyde series—is generated during metal-catalyzed peroxidation of PUFAs (including LA, AA, and LDL lipids) and covalently modifies lysine residues (forming $N^\varepsilon$-(3-formyl-3,4-dehydropiperidino)lysine, FDP-lysine) in human oxidized LDL and atherosclerotic lesions.
- **Isolevuglandins (`isoLGs` / isoketals) require arachidonic acid, *not* unelongated LA:** Brame and colleagues {{< cite "brame1999" >}} identified **isolevuglandins** (extremely reactive $\gamma$-ketoaldehydes that adduct protein lysines orders of magnitude faster than `4-HNE`) as spontaneous rearrangement products of bicyclic endoperoxide intermediates (`H₂-isoprostanes`) formed exclusively from free-radical peroxidation of **arachidonic acid (20:4n-6)** (or analogous neuroketals from DHA)—a bicyclic ring closure that is chemically impossible from a two-double-bond fatty acid like C18:2 LA without prior elongation/desaturation to AA. Distinguishing `4-HNE` (directly from LA + AA) from `isoLGs` and canonical `MDA` (predominantly from AA/LC-PUFAs) prevents frequent chemical inaccuracies in the nutrition literature.

#### Analytical Chemistry: How Chiral-Phase and Regioisomeric LC-MS/MS Separate Regulated Signaling from Oxidative Damage

**Established analytical biochemistry with direct validation in human atherosclerotic plaques and metabolic cohorts.** As Niki {{< cite "niki2009" >}} and Yin, Xu, and Porter {{< cite "yin2011" >}} emphasize, quantifying total 9- or 13-HODE by achiral LC-MS/MS conflates regulated enzymatic synthesis with uncontrolled oxidative injury (and even with *ex vivo* autoxidation during sample handling if antioxidants like BHT and triphenylphosphine are omitted). Modern lipidomics resolves the source through three chemical signatures:
1. **Enantiomeric excess (*S* vs. *R*) via chiral-phase HPLC/LC-MS/MS:** Enzymatic `15-LOX-1` produces >95% **13(*S*)-HODE**, whereas free-radical autoxidation yields a **50:50 racemic mixture** of (*R*) and (*S*) enantiomers at both C-9 and C-13. Applying chiral HPLC to rabbit lesions, Kühn and colleagues {{< cite "kuhn1994" >}} demonstrated an informative temporal shift: **early atherosclerotic lesions** contained stereospecific enzymatic **13(*S*)-HODE** (from macrophage 15-LOX), whereas **advanced lesions** shifted toward racemic mixtures dominated by non-enzymatic free-radical autoxidation. Folcik and colleagues {{< cite "folcik1995" >}} confirmed this directly in **human atherosclerotic plaques**, detecting significant stereospecific excess of *S*-enantiomer HODEs in cholesteryl esters alongside substantial racemic non-enzymatic oxidation—proving that both pathways coexist in human vascular disease.
2. **Geometric isomerism (*(Z,E)* vs. *(E,E)*):** Enzymatic LOX and COX pathways generate exclusively ***cis,trans* (*(Z,E)*)** conjugated dienes. Free-radical peroxidation (when hydrogen-atom donors like $\alpha$-tocopherol are depleted, permitting peroxyl-radical $\beta$-fragmentation and equilibration) generates thermodynamic ***trans,trans* (*(E,E)*)** isomers—making the ratio of **9- and 13-(*E,E*)-HODE** to *(Z,E)*-HODE a sensitive readout of free-radical stress and antioxidant depletion {{< cite "yin2011" >}}, {{< cite "niki2009" >}}.
3. **Non-radical regioisomers (10- and 12-HODE):** Neither mammalian lipoxygenases nor free-radical H-atom abstraction at C-11 can place a hydroxyl group at C-10 or C-12. Umeno, Yoshida, and colleagues {{< cite "umeno2013" >}} demonstrated that **10-(*Z,E*)-HODE** and **12-(*Z,E*)-HODE** are formed exclusively via **singlet oxygen ($^1\text{O}_2$)** addition to linoleate, and showed that plasma 10- and 12-HODE rise early in human impaired glucose tolerance / pre-diabetes—allowing investigators to separate singlet-oxygen injury from both free-radical autoxidation and enzymatic LOX/COX signaling.

**Evidence boundary:** While chiral and geometric HODE profiling decisively separates enzymatic signaling from oxidative damage *inside a tissue or plasma sample*, finding elevated racemic HODEs or `4-HNE` in human disease proves increased oxidative stress or impaired antioxidant defense—not by itself that dietary LA intake was the root cause.

#### Oxidized linoleate as a signal

**A specific mechanistic basis for the idea, with a crucial distinction.** Wang and colleagues {{< cite "wang2009" >}} found that EKODE, a particular oxidized metabolite of LA, activated an antioxidant-response pathway in neuroblastoma cells and cerebrocortical neurons. The response required NRF2 and PI3-kinase and increased expression of protective genes. LA itself did not activate the response in that screen.

This supports the narrower idea that a product of lipid oxidation can help signal an adaptive defense. It does not make LA itself a conventional antioxidant, show that every oxidation product is beneficial, or establish that increasing dietary LA produces the same response in a person.

### 11. Desaturase/Elongase Cascade, Micronutrient Cofactors, Human FADS Evolution, and Disease Associations

#### Genetic Dissection of the LA → GLA → DGLA → AA Cascade (`FADS2`, `ELOVL5`, `FADS1`)

**Strong molecular and genetic evidence in mice; human deficiency states are rarer and mostly partial.** Cho, Nakamura, and Clarke {{< cite "cho1999" >}} cloned mammalian $\Delta6$-desaturase (**`FADS2`**), confirmed its ability to desaturate both LA (to GLA) and ALA (to stearidonic acid), and demonstrated tight nutritional feedback: feeding mice PUFA-rich corn oil suppressed hepatic $\Delta6$-desaturase activity by ~70% and mRNA by ~50% relative to triolein, while showing unexpectedly high `FADS2` expression in brain.

Building beyond `Fads2`-null mice ({{< cite "stroud2009" >}}, already cited in the notes for dermal/intestinal ulceration rescued by AA), genetic knockouts of the next two enzymes show that every step in the cascade carries distinct physiological consequences. Moon, Hammer, and Horton {{< cite "moon2009" >}} deleted **`Elovl5`** in mice, sharply lowering hepatic AA and DHA while accumulating C18 substrates; loss of LC-PUFA feedback inhibition constitutively activated **SREBP-1c** and drove hepatic steatosis, which was reversed by dietary AA or DHA. Fan and colleagues {{< cite "fan2012" >}} characterized **`Fads1`** ($\Delta5$-desaturase) knockout mice: despite ample dietary LA and markedly elevated DGLA, these animals suffered severe tissue AA depletion, poor viability without exogenous AA, and heightened vulnerability to intestinal mucosal injury and inflammation.

Together with `stroud2009` and `ichi2014`, these models prove that dietary LA cannot substitute for AA when conversion machinery is absent, and that downstream LC-PUFAs exercise direct transcriptional feedback over both desaturation and de novo lipogenesis. They do not imply that typical human variation in `FADS` expression causes frank AA deficiency in mixed diets.

#### Competition Between LA and ALA for $\Delta6$-Desaturase, and Why Raising LA Rarely Raises Tissue AA in Humans

**Direct animal evidence and controlled human stable-isotope trials; absolute intakes matter more than a simple ratio.** Mohrhauer and Holman {{< cite "mohrhauer1963" >}} established in rats that dietary ALA and LA competitively inhibit each other's desaturation and elongation along the shared $\Delta6$-desaturase pathway, altering liver and tissue PUFA profiles. In healthy young men given deuterium-labeled tracers, Emken, Adlof, and Gulley {{< cite "emken1994" >}} showed that doubling dietary LA from 15 g/day to 30 g/day reduced the conversion of labeled ALA (and LA itself) into longer-chain products by **40% to 54%**.

However, Goyens and colleagues {{< cite "goyens2006" >}} used [U-$^{13}$C]ALA compartmental modeling in humans to test whether the *ratio* of LA to ALA or their *absolute amounts* governs conversion. Lowering absolute dietary LA increased the fractional conversion of ALA to EPA, while raising absolute ALA increased the total mass converted; changing the ratio without controlling absolute intakes failed to predict conversion accurately. Equally important for the clinical debate is the systematic review of 36 human dietary trials by Rett and Whelan {{< cite "rett2011" >}}: across adults consuming Western-type diets, **increasing dietary LA (up to sixfold) did not reliably increase plasma or erythrocyte arachidonic acid**, nor did decreasing LA by up to 90% consistently reduce tissue AA, likely because $\Delta6$/$\Delta5$-desaturase activity is already feedback-regulated and near saturation for LA-to-AA flux at typical modern intakes (consistent with {{< cite "cho1999" >}} and {{< cite "burdge2002" >}}).

These findings narrow two common overstatements at once: high absolute LA intake genuinely suppresses endogenous conversion of plant ALA to EPA/DHA in humans, *without* automatically flooding human tissues with arachidonic acid.

#### Human Evolutionary Genetics of the `FADS` Cluster

**High-confidence human genomic evidence; evolutionary selection does not dictate a single optimal modern intake.** Modern humans do not share one uniform capacity to convert LA into AA and ALA into EPA/DHA. Ameur and colleagues {{< cite "ameur2012" >}} identified two major common human haplotypes spanning the **`FADS1`/`FADS2`** cluster: an ancestral lower-conversion haplotype (haplotype A) and a human-specific derived haplotype (haplotype D) that arose after the split from Neanderthals, carries strong signatures of positive selection in Africa, and markedly increases endogenous LC-PUFA synthesis from plant precursors (LA and ALA).

Subsequent studies revealed that selection operated in *both directions* depending on ancestral food webs:
- **Arctic marine adaptation (down-regulating synthesis):** Fumagalli and colleagues {{< cite "fumagalli2015" >}} found that the strongest genome-wide signature of natural selection in **Greenlandic Inuit** lies directly at the `FADS1`/`FADS2`/`FADS3` locus. Adapted to a marine-mammal diet already rich in preformed EPA, DHA, and AA, the selected Inuit alleles *reduce* endogenous desaturase conversion and exert large pleiotropic effects on lipid profiles, height, and body weight.
- **Agricultural transition in Eurasia (up-regulating synthesis):** Using genome-wide ancient DNA from 230 West Eurasians (6500–1000 BCE), Mathieson and colleagues {{< cite "mathieson2015" >}} directly tracked positive selection at the `FADS1`/`FADS2` locus across time, identifying it as one of the strongest selective sweeps accompanying the transition toward agrarian, plant-rich diets lower in preformed animal LC-PUFAs.
- **Traditional vegetarian adaptation in South Asia:** Kothapalli and colleagues {{< cite "kothapalli2016" >}} identified positive selection on a 22-base-pair regulatory insertion–deletion polymorphism (**`rs66698963`**) in intron 1 of `FADS2` in populations with long-standing plant-based diets (such as South Asians), where the insertion allele enhances apparent endogenous synthesis of arachidonic acid from linoleic acid.

These studies establish that human LA-to-AA conversion capacity is genetically polymorphic and evolutionarily tuned to ancestral preformed LC-PUFA availability. They raise—but do *not* by themselves settle—the clinical "mismatch" hypothesis that high-conversion `FADS` haplotypes (common in African and South Asian ancestries) interact adversely with high modern dietary LA intakes to increase inflammatory or cardiometabolic risk.

#### Interaction with vitamin B6

**Evidence of interaction, not demonstrated reciprocal sparing.** Maranesi and colleagues {{< cite "maranesi1993" >}} studied rats receiving little dietary EFA with or without vitamin B6 deficiency. B6 deficiency changed kidney phospholipid composition and increased renal prostaglandin E2 under those conditions.

This is relevant because it shows that vitamin status can change the handling of fatty acids and their signaling products. It does not demonstrate that LA or ALA reduces a measured B-vitamin requirement, that B vitamins reduce EFA requirements, or that the interaction extends to every B vitamin. “Sparing” remains a stronger proposition than this experiment tested.

#### Supply and conversion are separate problems

**Direct proof of principle in genetically modified mice.** In Stroud and colleagues {{< cite "stroud2009" >}}, mice without delta-6 desaturase developed disease despite ample dietary LA; AA prevented the skin and intestinal lesions. In the same type of model, Roqueta-Rivera and colleagues {{< cite "roquetarivera2010" >}} found that DHA restored male fertility more effectively than AA.

These experiments show why adding the parent fatty acid may fail when a required conversion step is missing, and why the appropriate downstream product can sometimes bypass that block. They do not establish that every disease-associated change in a desaturase or elongase creates the same bottleneck. They also argue against treating impaired conversion as necessarily irreversible or untreatable.

#### A map of disease associations, not one deficiency syndrome

**Several associations are documented, but the findings are not interchangeable.** “Derangement” can mean altered blood concentrations, different tissue composition, changed enzyme activity, or oxidation products. Those measurements do not all establish dietary EFA deficiency. The papers below provide specific entry points into this long list.

**Diabetes and malnutrition.** Holman and colleagues {{< cite "holman1983" >}} found higher LA but lower AA in several tissues of streptozotocin-diabetic rats, suggesting impaired conversion. Koletzko and colleagues {{< cite "koletzko1986" >}} found depleted polyunsaturated fatty acids in children with marasmus or kwashiorkor, without a significant difference between those two groups. These are different nutritional and metabolic situations.

**Vitamin and mineral status.** Balevska and colleagues {{< cite "balevska1985" >}} found altered liver-membrane fatty acids in copper-deficient rats. Buttriss and Diplock {{< cite "buttriss1988" >}} found that vitamin E deficiency reduced membrane PUFA, combined vitamin E/selenium deficiency increased its proportion, and selenium deficiency alone had no consistent effect. “Deficiency” therefore did not produce one uniform fatty-acid pattern.

**Cystic fibrosis and multiple sclerosis.** Freedman and colleagues {{< cite "freedman2004" >}} found altered AA/DHA ratios in CFTR-expressing tissue from people with cystic fibrosis. Holman, Johnson, and Kokmen {{< cite "holman1989" >}} reported altered plasma profiles in 14 people with multiple sclerosis. Neither observation establishes that insufficient dietary EFA caused the disease. The MS study cannot support the much broader phrase “most chronic and acute neurological diseases.”

**Lung injury and toxic liver injury.** Quinlan, Evans, and Gutteridge {{< cite "quinlan1994" >}}, observed linked declines in plasma linoleate and protein thiols in nine of eleven ARDS patients, consistent with oxidative damage. Carreau and colleagues {{< cite "carreau1980" >}}, measured reduced hepatic desaturase activity after carbon-tetrachloride poisoning in rats. These concern injury-associated changes, not proof that either illness begins with dietary deficiency.

**LEC rats, Wilson's disease, and liver cancer.** Shibata and colleagues {{< cite "shibata1999" >}} found that safflower- or perilla-oil-supplemented diets delayed hepatitis in LEC rats; that dietary intervention is not itself a diagnosis of EFAD. Azbukina and colleagues {{< cite "azbukina2020" >}} identified altered plasma lipid/oxylipin profiles in people with Wilson's disease. Qiu and colleagues {{< cite "qiu2015" >}} found altered plasma and tissue fatty-acid profiles in hepatocellular carcinoma. The human studies are associations, not demonstrations of a shared cause.

**Cardiac death and blood cells.** Luostarinen, Boberg, and Saldeen {{< cite "luostarinen1993" >}} found higher LA but lower AA and several other PUFAs in coronary arteries after sudden cardiac death; the cases also had more atherosclerosis. Rachmilewitz, Shohet, and Lubin {{< cite "rachmilewitz1976" >}}, documented altered red-cell lipids in beta-thalassemia major. Mackay and colleagues {{< cite "mackay2012" >}} found lower erythrocyte long-chain PUFAs in mothers and offspring in preeclampsia and growth-restricted pregnancies. This last result addresses a defined pregnancy context, not the undefined category “abnormal umbilical cord blood cells.”

**Boundary of this annotation.** These examples support investigating fatty-acid metabolism across diseases. They do not establish the entire original list as a single syndrome, independently verify its alloxan-specific claim, or show that EFA supplementation prevents or treats each condition.

#### Vitamin E can affect enzymes and protect against oxidation

**An enzyme effect is documented; the proposed exclusion of antioxidant action is contradicted.** Despret and colleagues {{< cite "despret1992" >}}, increased vitamin E in rat microsomal preparations and observed increased delta-6 desaturase activity in brain preparations but reduced activity in liver preparations. This supports a tissue-dependent interaction, not a universal alternative explanation for vitamin E's function.

Carlson and colleagues {{< cite "carlson2016" >}} found that vitamin E rescued mice lacking the lipid-peroxide-detoxifying enzyme GPX4 in hepatocytes; protection was lost when vitamin E was withdrawn. Their results support protection against damaging lipid peroxidation. The possibility of enzyme regulation does not displace this antioxidant role. The original note's either/or framing should not be read as an established conclusion.

### 12. Eicosanoids, Resolution, Endocannabinoids, Immunity, Cancer, and Clinical Conflicts

#### Prostaglandins and Leukotrienes: Initiation and Host Defense

**Foundational, definitive biochemical evidence.** Bergström, Danielsson, and Samuelsson {{< cite "bergstrom1964" >}} demonstrated the enzymatic conversion of arachidonic acid to **prostaglandin E$_2$ (PGE$_2$)**, providing the first direct biochemical bridge between essential fatty acids and potent, locally acting hormone-like lipid mediators. Samuelsson {{< cite "samuelsson1983" >}} synthesized the discovery and biology of **leukotrienes** synthesized from AA via 5-lipoxygenase: **LTB$_4$** acts as a powerful neutrophil chemoattractant and activator (compare {{< cite "chouinard2013" >}}), while the cysteinyl leukotrienes (**LTC$_4$, LTD$_4$, LTE$_4$**, previously known as slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis) drive bronchoconstriction and vascular permeability.

While excessive prostaglandin and leukotriene production drives pain, asthma, and tissue injury, completely blocking or depleting the AA pathway impairs gastric mucosal protection, renal hemodynamics, vascular homeostasis, and pathogen clearance (compare {{< cite "stroud2009" >}}, {{< cite "fan2012" >}}, {{< cite "chouinard2013" >}}). Labeling AA-derived eicosanoids strictly "bad" confuses physiological host-defense and barrier signaling with chronic dysregulation.

#### Lipoxins and Active Resolution of Inflammation — Plus the Modern Analytical Debate

- **Omega-6 arachidonic acid is required to *resolve* acute inflammation, not merely start it**

**Strong mechanistic evidence for lipid-mediator class switching; human quantification of endogenous SPMs is actively contested.** Serhan, Hamberg, and Samuelsson {{< cite "serhan1984" >}} discovered **lipoxins** (**LXA$_4$** and **LXB$_4$**), trihydroxytetraene metabolites formed directly from **arachidonic acid** through transcellular interactions between 5-lipoxygenase and 15-lipoxygenase in human leukocytes. Levy and colleagues {{< cite "levy2001" >}} then uncovered a critical biological turn: during acute inflammation, the classic "pro-inflammatory" omega-6 mediator **PGE$_2$** actually reprograms human neutrophils to induce 15-lipoxygenase and switch their synthesis *away* from neutrophil-recruiting LTB$_4$ and *toward* **LXA$_4$**. This **lipid mediator class switch** halts further neutrophil infiltration and signals the active resolution phase of inflammation. In other words, the omega-6 pathway encodes both the accelerator and the brake of acute inflammatory responses.

An evidence-bounded note must also record a significant contemporary methodological dispute. Schebb and colleagues {{< cite "schebb2022" >}} published a comprehensive critical evaluation of the **specialized pro-resolving mediator (SPM)** literature (encompassing both AA-derived lipoxins and n-3-derived resolvins/protectins), noting that many proposed SPMs occur at extremely low or undetectable concentrations in human plasma and leukocyte exudates under rigorous LC-MS/MS standards, and that several putative GPCR signaling assignments remain imperfectly replicated. This critique does not overturn the discovery of lipoxins or the reality of eicosanoid class switching in inflammatory exudates {{< cite "levy2001" >}}, but it cautions against treating circulating SPM assays as settled clinical biomarkers of dietary omega-6 or omega-3 adequacy.

#### Endocannabinoids (AEA, 2-AG) and *N*-Linoleoylethanolamine (LEA): Appetite, Satiety, and Energy Homeostasis

**Clear mechanistic evidence in rodents; human translation for LA-driven hyperphagia remains unestablished.** Devane and colleagues {{< cite "devane1992" >}} isolated **anandamide** (*N*-arachidonoylethanolamine, **AEA**) from brain, and Mechoulam and colleagues {{< cite "mechoulam1995" >}} identified **2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG)** in canine gut—establishing that both canonical endogenous agonists of CB1/CB2 cannabinoid receptors are synthesized directly from **arachidonic acid**-containing membrane phospholipids. Testing the dietary implication in mice, Alvheim and colleagues {{< cite "alvheim2012" >}} raised dietary LA from 1% to 8% of energy and observed elevated tissue AA phospholipids, a roughly **threefold increase** in liver and brain 2-AG and AEA, and increased food intake, feed efficiency, and adiposity; adding EPA and DHA normalized AA phospholipids, lowered endocannabinoid tone, and prevented obesity.

Yet linoleic acid also gives rise directly (without conversion to AA) to ***N*-linoleoylethanolamine (LEA)**, a member of the non-cannabinoid *N*-acylethanolamine (NAE) family alongside oleoylethanolamide (OEA). Artmann and colleagues {{< cite "artmann2008" >}} showed in rats that diets enriched in linoleic acid directly increase LEA concentrations in the small intestine (jejunum), liver, and brain. Unlike AEA and 2-AG, LEA does not strongly stimulate CB1-driven appetite; instead, Syed and colleagues {{< cite "syed2012" >}} demonstrated that LEA is a potent endogenous agonist of the intestinal metabolic receptor **GPR119** (which regulates GLP-1 incretin secretion and glucose homeostasis), with intestinal LEA rising sharply upon refeeding in lean mice. Diep and colleagues {{< cite "diep2011" >}} further showed that small-intestinal anorectic NAEs—OEA, PEA, and LEA—participate in gut fat-sensing satiety pathways (via PPARα and GPR119) that become blunted during prolonged high-fat feeding, potentially promoting overeating.

Taken together, LA participates in both orexigenic (AA → AEA/2-AG → CB1) and anorectic/incretin (LA → LEA → GPR119/PPARα) signaling arms. Crucially, the dramatic 1%-to-8% LA-driven surge in tissue AA and endocannabinoids seen in mice {{< cite "alvheim2012" >}} contrasts with human trials showing that varying LA across typical Western intakes rarely elevates tissue AA {{< cite "rett2011" >}}—underscoring that rodent endocannabinoid obesity models cannot be extrapolated uncritically to human diets.

#### Macrophages, T Cells, and B Cells: Context-Dependent Immunity and Membrane Biophysics

**High-quality mechanistic studies in mice and human cells; not a direct readout of dietary LA intake in healthy humans.**
- **Macrophage polarization and resolution:** Huang and colleagues {{< cite "huang1999" >}} showed that the Th2 cytokine **IL-4** coordinately induces **12/15-lipoxygenase** and PPARγ in monocytes and macrophages, generating endogenous **13-HODE** (from LA) and 15-HETE (from AA) to drive PPARγ-dependent transcriptional programs. In a complementary study of inflammatory resolution, Oishi and colleagues {{< cite "oishi2017" >}} demonstrated that during the late phase of macrophage TLR4 activation, **SREBP1** reprograms cellular fatty-acid metabolism to synthesize anti-inflammatory unsaturated fatty acids required to actively shut down pro-inflammatory gene expression.
- **Selective CD4$^+$ T-cell toxicity vs. CD8$^+$ T-cell potentiation:** Ma and colleagues {{< cite "ma2016" >}} reported that in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), accumulated **linoleic acid** in the liver preferentially induced mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) and apoptosis in **intrahepatic CD4$^+$ T cells** (which carry greater mitochondrial mass than CD8$^+$ T cells), impairing antitumor immune surveillance and accelerating hepatocarcinogenesis in mice. In striking contrast, Nava Lauson and colleagues {{< cite "navalauson2023" >}} demonstrated that **linoleic acid** acts as a positive metabolic regulator of **CD8$^+$ T cells**: LA enhanced endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondria contacts (MERCs), boosted calcium signaling and mitochondrial energetics, prevented exhaustion, and potentiated antitumor immunity in both mouse models and human T cells ex vivo.
- **Membrane lipid rafts in T and B lymphocytes:** Beyond specific ligands or ROS, PUFA acyl chains physically remodel immune-cell signaling domains. Stulnig and colleagues {{< cite "stulnig2001" >}} showed in T cells that incorporating long-chain PUFAs into membrane rafts physically displaces acylated signaling proteins (Lck and LAT) from raft microdomains. Rockett and colleagues {{< cite "rockett2012" >}} similarly demonstrated in murine **B cells** that altering dietary n-3 versus n-6 PUFA composition changes B-cell lipid-raft size, membrane order, and downstream functional activation.

These studies dismantle any blanket statement that LA is universally "pro-inflammatory" or "immunosuppressive." Even within T lymphocytes, the exact same fatty acid triggered mitochondrial oxidative death in NAFLD-exposed CD4$^+$ cells {{< cite "ma2016" >}} while enhancing ER–mitochondrial coupling and effector fitness in antitumor CD8$^+$ cells {{< cite "navalauson2023" >}}. Neither study tested ordinary dietary LA variation in healthy humans.

#### Direct Antimicrobial Activity of Linoleic Acid

**Direct microbiological evidence in vitro; physiological relevance depends on local free-fatty-acid concentrations (e.g., skin surface, abscesses).** The existing notes cite Beavers and colleagues {{< cite "beavers2019" >}} (AA killing *Staphylococcus aureus* via lipid peroxidation) and Chouinard and colleagues {{< cite "chouinard2013" >}} (AA and 2-AG stimulating neutrophil LTB$_4$ and antimicrobial-peptide release). Linoleic acid itself also exhibits direct antimicrobial activity via non-peroxidative targets. In a classic systematic screen of 30 fatty acids and derivatives, Kabara and colleagues {{< cite "kabara1972" >}} showed that unsaturation strongly enhanced antibacterial potency against Gram-positive organisms, with **linoleic acid (C18:2)** markedly more inhibitory than oleic (C18:1) or saturated C18 fatty acids. Zheng and colleagues {{< cite "zheng2005" >}} later identified a specific molecular target: LA and related long-chain unsaturated fatty acids directly inhibit bacterial **FabI** (enoyl-acyl carrier protein reductase), an essential enzyme of bacterial type II fatty-acid synthesis, blocking *S. aureus* growth in a manner reversible by exogenous fatty acids.

This adds a third concrete antimicrobial mechanism alongside peroxidation {{< cite "beavers2019" >}} and neutrophil activation {{< cite "chouinard2013" >}}. Because these are primarily in vitro microbiological assays, they support a role for free LA in local barrier defense (such as sebum or cutaneous surfaces) rather than proving that eating more LA reduces systemic bacterial infections.

#### The Human Clinical Conflict: Inflammatory Markers, OXLAMs, Historical High-LA Trials, and Prospective Biomarkers

**Substantial human evidence on both sides, with distinct methodological limitations for every design.**
1. **Short-to-medium-term RCTs of standard inflammatory biomarkers:** Johnson and Fritsche {{< cite "johnson2012" >}} systematically reviewed 15 randomized controlled trials in healthy non-infant populations and found virtually no evidence that increasing dietary LA raises systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF-α, IL-6, fibrinogen, PAI-1, or soluble adhesion molecules). A larger meta-analysis of 30 RCTs (1,377 participants) by Su and colleagues {{< cite "su2017" >}} likewise found no overall effect of higher LA intake on blood cytokines, acute-phase proteins, or adhesion molecules—though subgroup/meta-regression analysis flagged a possible rise in CRP specifically among participants given very large increases in dietary LA. These trials directly undercut the claim that ordinary dietary LA predictably raises classic circulating inflammatory markers, but most were relatively short (weeks to a few months) and did not measure tissue or plasma **OXLAMs**.
2. **Human OXLAM-lowering and clinical pain RCTs:** Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2012" >}} demonstrated in humans that lowering dietary LA for 12 weeks markedly decreased circulating bioactive OXLAMs (9- and 13-HODE, 9- and 13-oxo-ODE), and their 2021 migraine RCT {{< cite "ramsden2021" >}} showed clinical headache benefits when elevated EPA/DHA was paired with sharp LA restriction ($\le 1.8\%$ energy). This suggests that classic systemic markers like CRP and IL-6 may miss biologically active, LA-sensitive oxylipin pathways.
3. **Re-analyses of two mid-century high-LA replacement RCTs:** Ramsden and colleagues recovered previously unpublished data from two landmark randomized trials that replaced saturated fat with LA-rich vegetable oils. In the **Sydney Diet Heart Study** (secondary prevention in men; safflower oil and safflower margarine), Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2013" >}} found that despite lowering serum cholesterol, the high-LA intervention group had **higher** all-cause, cardiovascular, and coronary heart disease mortality. In the **Minnesota Coronary Experiment** (institutionalized adults; corn oil), Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2016" >}} found a ~14% drop in serum cholesterol with **no mortality benefit**, and an unexpected association between greater cholesterol reduction and higher mortality in participants aged $\ge 65$. Both re-analyses raise serious questions about substituting high doses of refined LA-rich oils (often >12–15% energy) for saturated fat, and the authors proposed OXLAM accumulation as a plausible mechanism. Yet both historical trials carry well-documented limitations: the Sydney intervention margarine likely contained **industrial trans fats**; the Minnesota trial had high participant turnover, short median follow-up (~1 year for many participants), and an institutionalized population not representative of free-living adults.
4. **Prospective observational biomarker cohorts:** On the opposite side of the ledger, Marklund and colleagues {{< cite "marklund2019" >}} pooled individual-level data from **30 prospective cohort studies** across 13 countries (68,659 participants) measuring objective circulating and adipose tissue omega-6 biomarkers. Higher *in vivo* **linoleic acid** levels were significantly associated with **lower** risks of total cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular mortality, and ischemic stroke, while **arachidonic acid** biomarkers were neutral or inversely associated with risk. As observational epidemiology, these findings cannot rule out residual confounding, healthy-user bias, or reverse causality (for example, metabolic disease or oxidative stress lowering plasma LA independently of intake; compare {{< cite "quinlan1994" >}} and {{< cite "holman1983" >}}).

**Boundary of the current evidence:** Taken together, the human data do **not** support the claim that dietary LA at typical intakes reliably elevates tissue AA {{< cite "rett2011" >}} or classic blood inflammatory markers like CRP and IL-6 {{< cite "johnson2012" >}}, {{< cite "su2017" >}}, nor do they align with a simple linear harm model across observational cohorts {{< cite "marklund2019" >}}. At the same time, they do **not** establish that very high intakes of LA-rich oils are biologically inert: high absolute LA measurably competes with ALA conversion {{< cite "emken1994" >}}, {{< cite "goyens2006" >}}, raises circulating OXLAMs {{< cite "ramsden2012" >}}, may blunt omega-3 analgesia in migraine {{< cite "ramsden2021" >}}, and failed to reduce mortality in two recovered mid-century replacement trials {{< cite "ramsden2013" >}}, {{< cite "ramsden2016" >}}. Resolving that tension requires distinguishing dose, background omega-3 intake, genetic `FADS` haplotype, lipid oxidation state, and clinical endpoint rather than treating "linoleic acid" as a single undifferentiated variable.

#### Mast Cells, Allergic Inflammation, and Helper/Regulatory T-Cell Polarization (`PGD₂`, `PGE₂`, and Gut-Microbial `12,13-DiHOME`)

**Definitive mouse-genetic and human-cell pharmacology; establishes AA-derived eicosanoids as effector mediators of allergy, not that dietary LA causes human asthma.** Activated mast cells rapidly mobilize membrane arachidonic acid (derived ultimately from dietary LA or preformed AA) to synthesize cysteinyl leukotrienes (`LTC₄`/`LTD₄`, {{< cite "samuelsson1983" >}}) and **prostaglandin D$_2$ (`PGD₂`)**. Matsuoka and colleagues {{< cite "matsuoka2000" >}} proved causality in allergic asthma by deleting the `PGD₂` receptor (**DP / DP1**) in mice: *DP*-null mice failed to develop ovalbumin-induced Th2 cytokine accumulation, eosinophil infiltration, or airway hyperreactivity. Hirai and colleagues {{< cite "hirai2001" >}} identified the second `PGD₂` receptor, **CRTH2 (DP2)**, demonstrating that `PGD₂` acts via CRTH2 to selectively induce chemotaxis of human **Th2 lymphocytes, eosinophils, and basophils**.

**Strong mechanistic evidence in primary human T cells and mouse autoimmunity models; illustrates why AA-derived `PGE₂` defies a simple "pro-inflammatory" label.** Two landmark 2009 studies overturned the older belief that `PGE₂` is purely Th1-suppressive/immunosuppressive. Boniface and colleagues {{< cite "boniface2009" >}} showed that `PGE₂` signals through **EP2 and EP4** receptors and cyclic AMP to directly promote human and murine **Th17 cell** differentiation (upregulating *IL23R* and *IL1R1* while suppressing IL-10). Concurrently, Yao and colleagues {{< cite "yao2009" >}} demonstrated *in vivo* that **`PGE₂`–EP4** signaling promotes both Th1 differentiation and **IL-23–driven Th17 expansion**, aggravating experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and contact hypersensitivity in mice. Yet in a different microenvironment (especially tumors and mucosal surfaces), Baratelli and colleagues {{< cite "baratelli2005" >}} showed that `PGE₂` upregulates **FOXP3** expression and enhances the suppressive function of human CD4$^+$CD25$^+$ **regulatory T cells (Tregs)** while converting CD4$^+$CD25$^-$ T cells into suppressive cells—a mechanism widely exploited by `COX-2`-expressing tumors to evade immunity.

**High-impact translational evidence combining human birth cohorts, metagenomics, and mouse causality; contrasts sharply with BAT-derived 12,13-DiHOME in adult metabolism.** Directly expanding the Pass 1 narrative on **12,13-DiHOME** (an adaptive brown-fat lipokine during cold and exercise, {{< cite "lynes2017" >}}, {{< cite "stanford2018" >}}), Fujimura and colleagues {{< cite "fujimura2016" >}} profiled neonatal gut microbiota and fecal metabolomes in human birth cohorts and discovered that infants at highest risk of multi-sensitized **atopy and childhood asthma** harbored elevated fecal **12,13-DiHOME**, which reduced human CD4$^+$CD25$^+$FOXP3$^+$ **Treg** proportions and skewed T cells toward IL-4/Th2 responses *ex vivo*. Tracing the origin of this linoleate diol, Levan and colleagues {{< cite "levan2019" >}} identified **bacterial epoxide hydrolase (`EH`) genes** in the neonatal gut microbiome (e.g., *Enterococcus faecalis*, *Bifidobacterium bifidum*) that directly convert LA epoxide into **12,13-DiHOME**; administering 12,13-DiHOME or EH-expressing bacteria to mice altered dendritic-cell PPARγ signaling, reduced pulmonary Tregs, and aggravated allergic airway inflammation.

**Evidence boundary:** While these studies establish a compelling causal chain from **microbial conversion of LA to 12,13-DiHOME $\rightarrow$ Treg suppression $\rightarrow$ allergic airway susceptibility**, they show that *microbial enzyme abundance* (neonatal dysbiosis), rather than maternal or infant dietary LA intake per se, differentiated high-risk from low-risk infants.

#### Sepsis, ARDS, and Critical Illness: Eicosanoid/SPM Trajectories, Plasma Linoleate Diols, and Parenteral Lipid Emulsions

**Direct human ICU observational cohorts and randomized parenteral-nutrition trials; intravenous bolus emulsions in critical illness cannot be conflated with oral dietary LA in healthy adults.**
- **Temporal eicosanoid and pro-resolving mediator signatures in sepsis:** Dalli and colleagues {{< cite "dalli2017" >}} profiled plasma lipid mediators longitudinally in critically ill human **sepsis** patients and reported that non-survivors exhibited markedly elevated pro-inflammatory eicosanoids (`PGF₂α`, `TXB₂`, `LTB₄`) alongside altered trajectories of proposed specialized pro-resolving mediators (`RvE1`, `RvD5`, `17R-PD1`), with distinct profiles associating with **ARDS** development and mortality. (As noted in Pass 1 Section 3.2, analytical quantification of low-abundance circulating SPMs remains methodologically debated {{< cite "schebb2022" >}}, whereas elevations in classic COX/LOX eicosanoids and linoleate diols are robust across laboratories.)
- **Plasma linoleate diols (`9,10-` and `12,13-DiHOME`) in severe pulmonary injury and ARDS:** Extending classic leukotoxin-diol lung toxicity ({{< cite "moghaddam1997" >}}, {{< cite "zheng2001" >}}) and plasma LA oxidative depletion in ARDS ({{< cite "quinlan1994" >}}), McReynolds and colleagues {{< cite "mcreynolds2021" >}} quantified oxylipins in hospitalized patients with severe **COVID-19** and pulmonary involvement and found striking elevations of circulating linoleate diols (**9,10-DiHOME** and **12,13-DiHOME**), supporting CYP/sEH-mediated linoleate diol accumulation as a biomarker and putative mediator of acute hyperinflammatory lung injury.
- **Parenteral soybean-oil (LA-rich) vs. fish-oil lipid emulsions in human sepsis:** In an open-label randomized clinical study of ICU patients with sepsis, Mayer and colleagues {{< cite "mayer2003" >}} compared continuous parenteral infusion of a conventional **n-6-rich soybean-oil emulsion** (~50–55% LA) against an **n-3-rich fish-oil emulsion**. Patients infused with soybean-oil emulsion maintained high free n-6 fatty acids and showed *increased* ex vivo endotoxin-stimulated monocyte TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 production, whereas fish-oil infusion rapidly shifted plasma/leukocyte lipids toward EPA/DHA and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine release by ~30%.

**Evidence boundary:** Direct intravenous infusion of pure soybean-oil triglyceride emulsions during hypermetabolic sepsis bypasses intestinal chylomicron processing and delivers massive free LA loads directly into inflamed vascular beds. Thus, Mayer et al. {{< cite "mayer2003" >}} and McReynolds et al. {{< cite "mcreynolds2021" >}} provide strong clinical grounds for caution with pure high-LA intravenous emulsions in ICU sepsis/ARDS, without proving that oral dietary LA causes sepsis or worsens baseline inflammation in healthy people.

#### Cancer Biology Dual-Edge: Tumor-Promoting Pathways (`COX-2`/`PGE₂`, `13-HODE`, `FABP5`–LA–`mTORC1`) vs. Tumor-Suppressive Mechanisms (CD8⁺ T Cells, `13(S)-HODE` Apoptosis, Ferroptosis) and Human Epidemiology

**Strong causal evidence in rodent tumor models and human tumor-cell/xenograft systems; subtype- and model-specific rather than universal across human cancers.**
- **Classic rodent mammary-tumor threshold:** Ip, Carter, and Ip {{< cite "ip1985" >}} demonstrated in DMBA-treated rats that mammary tumor yield rose steeply as dietary linoleate increased from 0.5% to ~4.4% by weight and then **plateaued** above ~4%—establishing that LA can act as a rate-limiting promoter in carcinogen-induced rodent mammary models up to a saturation threshold (rather than linearly ad infinitum).
- **`COX-2` / `PGE₂` axis in colorectal and epithelial carcinogenesis:** Oshima and colleagues {{< cite "oshima1996" >}} provided definitive genetic proof that the AA-to-`PGE₂` enzyme **`COX-2` (`Ptgs2`)** drives early intestinal tumorigenesis: deleting *Ptgs2* or administering a selective COX-2 inhibitor dramatically reduced intestinal polyp number and size in *Apc*$^{\Delta716}$ knockout mice. Downstream `PGE₂` promotes tumor angiogenesis, epithelial proliferation, and Treg-mediated immune evasion (compare {{< cite "baratelli2005" >}}).
- **Tissue-divergent `15-LOX-1` / `13-HODE` signaling (prostate vs. colon):** Even the direct enzymatic LA metabolite **13(*S*)-HODE** exhibits opposite valences across organs. In human **prostate adenocarcinoma**, Kelavkar and colleagues {{< cite "kelavkar2000" >}} found that `15-LOX-1` (which converts LA to 13-HODE) is *upregulated* concordantly with mutant p53 and correlates positively with Gleason grade and proliferative signaling. In stark contrast, in human **colorectal cancer**, Shureiqi and colleagues {{< cite "shureiqi2003" >}} showed that `15-LOX-1` is *lost* during carcinogenesis and that restoring **`15-LOX-1` / 13(*S*)-HODE** binds and **down-regulates PPARδ**, inducing apoptosis in colorectal cancer cells (mediating part of NSAID chemoprevention).
- **Direct LA–`FABP5`–`mTORC1` axis in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC):** Koundouros and colleagues {{< cite "koundouros2025" >}} uncovered a direct, non-oxidative tumor-promoting mechanism for dietary LA: LA binds **`FABP5`**, which directly engages **Raptor** to assemble and hyperactivate **`mTORC1`**, accelerating proliferation in **triple-negative breast cancer** cells and mouse models fed a high-LA diet (safflower oil). Crucially, `FABP5` levels were selectively elevated in tumors and serum from **TNBC** patients compared with hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer patients—and receptor-positive breast cancer lines lacking high `FABP5` were *insensitive* to LA-driven `mTORC1` activation. This landmark study provides a rigorous mechanistic hypothesis for precision nutritional oncology in `FABP5`-high TNBC, while *not* yet proving via human clinical RCTs that dietary LA restriction improves TNBC survival.

**High-quality mechanistic and mouse-tumor evidence operating directly against tumor growth.**
- **CD8⁺ T-cell antitumor fitness:** As covered in Pass 1 Section 4.1, Nava Lauson and colleagues {{< cite "navalauson2023" >}} proved that **linoleic acid** enhances ER–mitochondria contacts, Ca$^{2+}$ signaling, and mitochondrial bioenergetics in **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells**, preventing T-cell exhaustion and markedly improving adoptive T-cell tumor clearance in mice and human T cells *ex vivo*.
- **Ferroptosis sensitization via membrane PUFA enrichment:** Cancer cells with high oxidative metabolism are vulnerable to **ferroptosis**—iron-dependent lethal peroxidation of membrane phospholipids. Doll and colleagues {{< cite "doll2017" >}} identified **`ACSL4`** as the master genetic determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity because it selectively esterifies **ω-6 PUFAs** (arachidonic and adrenic acids, elongated from LA) into membrane phosphatidylethanolamines; deleting *Acsl4* conferred strong ferroptosis resistance. Testing dietary PUFAs directly in acidic tumor microenvironments, Dierge and colleagues {{< cite "dierge2021" >}} showed that **both ω-3 and ω-6 PUFAs** (including LA, AA, EPA, and DHA) accumulate in acid-adapted cancer cells, exceed lipid-droplet buffering capacity, and trigger **ferroptosis-mediated tumor suppression** in proportion to double-bond number (with n-3 LC-PUFAs more potent than LA, and cytotoxicity strongly amplified by DGAT inhibitors). Conversely, Ubellacker and colleagues {{< cite "ubellacker2020" >}} demonstrated that metastasizing melanoma cells survive transit through the **lymphatic system** specifically because lymph is enriched in **monounsaturated oleic acid**, which displaces peroxidation-prone PUFAs from cell membranes and shields tumor cells from ferroptosis—confirming *in vivo* that membrane PUFA incorporation acts as an endogenous brake on metastatic survival.

**Large-scale human prospective evidence shows no population-wide signal of LA-driven carcinogenesis, while lacking subtype-stratified clinical trials (such as `FABP5`-high TNBC).** Against the alarming rodent mammary models {{< cite "ip1985" >}}, Zock and Katan {{< cite "zock1998" >}} conducted a classic meta-analysis of human case-control and prospective cohort studies and found **no significant increase in breast (RR 1.05), colorectal (RR 0.92), or prostate (RR 0.83) cancer risk** with higher dietary LA intake. Updating this evidence base across 38 prospective cohort studies (>811,000 participants), Li and colleagues {{< cite "li2020" >}} found that higher **dietary LA intake** was associated with modestly **lower total mortality (RR 0.87), cardiovascular mortality (RR 0.87), and cancer mortality (RR 0.89)**, and objective **blood/adipose LA biomarkers** showed concordant inverse associations with cancer and total mortality (paralleling the cardiovascular biomarker pooling of {{< cite "marklund2019" >}}).

**Boundary of the current evidence:** Human prospective cohorts effectively rule out a large, uniform pan-cancer hazard from typical dietary LA intakes in the general population {{< cite "zock1998" >}}, {{< cite "li2020" >}}. However, observational epidemiology averages across all tumor genotypes and suffers from residual confounding; it cannot exclude clinically meaningful **molecular-subtype interactions**—such as `FABP5`-high triple-negative breast cancer {{< cite "koundouros2025" >}} or NAFLD-associated hepatocellular carcinoma with intrahepatic CD4$^+$ T-cell loss {{< cite "ma2016" >}}—nor can preclinical tumor-promotion studies override the simultaneous existence of LA-supported antitumor CD8$^+$ T-cell fitness {{< cite "navalauson2023" >}}, colorectal `13(S)-HODE` apoptosis {{< cite "shureiqi2003" >}}, and PUFA-mediated ferroptosis {{< cite "doll2017" >}}, {{< cite "dierge2021" >}}.

#### Antimicrobial lipids use more than one mechanism

**Mechanistic support for particular fatty acids and pathogens.** Beavers and colleagues {{< cite "beavers2019" >}} showed that arachidonic acid killed *Staphylococcus aureus* through lipid-peroxidation products that damaged bacterial molecules. Antioxidants reduced this toxicity. This is direct support for an antimicrobial role of lipid oxidation in that system.

Chouinard and colleagues {{< cite "chouinard2013" >}} found that AA and 2-arachidonoylglycerol stimulated human neutrophils to release factors active against bacteria and two viruses. That response involved leukotriene B4 signaling and antimicrobial peptides; it was not a demonstration that peroxides killed the viruses. Together these papers support lipid-dependent host defense, but not the claim that every effect belongs directly to LA/ALA or that immune-cell “hoarding” describes a single established mechanism.

## Part IV: Brain, Endocrinology, Comparative Adaptation, and Life Cycle

### 13. Brain, Glia, Sleep, Fever, Nociception, and Behavior

#### The Brain Linoleic Acid Paradox: Rapid Entry, Low Concentration (<2%)

**Direct tracer evidence in adult rats explains the paradox of low brain LA.** Unlike adipose tissue, heart, or liver, the mammalian brain keeps linoleic acid below roughly 2% of total fatty acids even on high-LA diets, while maintaining high concentrations of arachidonic acid (AA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). DeMar and colleagues {{< cite "demar2006" >}} infused radiolabeled `[¹⁴C]`linoleic acid intravenously into unanesthetized adult rats and found that although LA crosses the blood-brain barrier readily, approximately 86% of the radioactivity entering the brain was rapidly catabolized into β-oxidation products within 5 minutes, while less than 1% was elongated to AA or stably esterified into brain phospholipids.

As synthesized by Taha {{< cite "taha2020" >}}, the adult brain therefore treats incoming unesterified LA primarily as a substrate for rapid oxidation (or local conversion into oxidized signaling lipids) rather than a structural membrane building block, relying instead on hepatic conversion and circulating AA/DHA to maintain neural membrane composition. This demonstrates active metabolic exclusion of LA from mature brain phospholipids. It does not establish that dietary LA is inert in the central nervous system, nor does it rule out indirect brain effects mediated through plasma AA, competitive inhibition of omega-3 synthesis, or locally generated oxidized metabolites.

#### Myelin, Membrane Composition, & Neurodevelopmental Buffering

**Classic human compositional data and animal dose-response experiments show strong neural buffering.** Svennerholm {{< cite "svennerholm1968" >}} mapped phosphoglyceride fatty acids across human cerebral gray and white matter from early fetal life through old age, showing pronounced regional and developmental specificity: neural membranes are enriched in AA and DHA rather than parent LA, with distinct developmental shifts in n-6 and n-3 proportions during active myelination and maturation.

In young rats fed graded levels of dietary LA alongside adequate ALA, Bourre and colleagues {{< cite "bourre1990" >}} showed that brain, myelin, synaptosome, and retina fatty-acid profiles remained tightly regulated across wide dietary LA ranges, reaching saturation at a much lower minimal dietary LA threshold than peripheral organs such as liver. Together, these findings support the view that the developing nervous system defends its myelin and synaptic lipid architecture against moderate fluctuations in dietary LA. They do not establish that the brain is immune to extreme maternal LA excess or severe omega-3 depletion, especially during perinatal windows when membrane accretion is fastest.

#### Oxidized Linoleic Acid Metabolites (OXLAMs) in Peripheral & Central Nociception

**Strong mechanistic evidence in rodents; promising but bounded clinical translation in humans.** Patwardhan and colleagues {{< cite "patwardhan2009" >}} demonstrated that depolarization of the rodent spinal cord triggers release of oxidized linoleic acid metabolites—specifically 9- and 13-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (9- and 13-HODE)—which potently activate central TRPV1 channels and mediate inflammatory mechanical allodynia; intrathecal antibodies neutralizing 9- and 13-HODE blocked CFA-induced allodynia. In a companion study, Patwardhan and colleagues {{< cite "patwardhan2010" >}} showed that noxious heat applied to peripheral skin rapidly generates 9- and 13-HODE and their downstream ketones (9- and 13-oxoODE), acting as endogenous TRPV1 agonists that drive thermal nociception.

Testing whether diet can shift pain-related lipid mediators clinically, Ramsden and colleagues {{< cite "ramsden2021" >}} conducted a 16-week, three-arm randomized controlled trial in 182 adults with frequent migraine comparing usual U.S. intake (~7% energy LA, <150 mg/day EPA+DHA), a high-omega-3 diet (1.5 g/day EPA+DHA with ~7% LA; H3), and a combined high-omega-3/low-LA diet (1.5 g/day EPA+DHA with ≤1.8% LA; H3-L6). Both intervention arms significantly raised circulating antinociceptive 17-HDHA and reduced diary-recorded total headache hours per day (by ~1.3 hours in H3 and ~1.7 hours in H3-L6 vs. control), with H3-L6 producing greater reductions in total headache days per month than H3 alone. However, the primary clinical quality-of-life endpoint (HIT-6 score) did not reach statistical significance compared with control, and because the trial did not include a low-LA/low-omega-3 arm, it cannot isolate dietary LA reduction independently of increased EPA and DHA. Rodent TRPV1 activation by OXLAMs is well established; generalizing from a targeted migraine intervention to chronic pain syndromes broadly remains an active clinical question.

#### Neuroinflammation & Synaptic Plasticity

**Mechanistic animal evidence extending beyond omega-3 deficiency (`lafourcade2011`).** While `lafourcade2011` linked lifelong omega-3 deficiency to impaired endocannabinoid-mediated synaptic plasticity, more recent rodent work examines how LA intake and LA oxidation products themselves affect brain reactivity. Taha and colleagues {{< cite "taha2017" >}} fed rats either an adequate-LA (~5.2% energy) or low-LA (~0.4% energy) diet for 15 weeks before intracerebroventricular lipopolysaccharide (LPS) infusion; dietary LA lowering attenuated the LPS-induced rise in brain COX-2 activity and prostaglandin E₂, indicating that dietary LA supply can modulate neuroinflammatory AA turnover under an acute immune challenge.

Directly testing acute synaptic function, Hennebelle and colleagues {{< cite "hennebelle2017" >}} showed that CO₂-induced acute brain ischemia in rats causes marked accumulation of OXLAMs across brain regions. In rat hippocampal slices, applying native parent LA had no effect on paired-pulse facilitation, whereas applying 13-HODE increased somatic paired-pulse facilitation by roughly 80%. This reinforces the pattern already noted for `wang2009` (EKODE and NRF2): intact LA and its enzymatically or oxidatively generated metabolites behave as distinct biological entities. These rodent experiments show that OXLAMs are neuroactive during ischemia and that severe dietary LA restriction dampens LPS-evoked prostaglandin synthesis, but they do not establish that ordinary human LA intakes cause chronic neuroinflammation or neurodegeneration.

#### Blood-Brain Barrier Transport: `Mfsd2a` for LPC-DHA vs. Passive Diffusion, FATPs, & CD36 for Unesterified LA

**Mechanistic animal and human genetic evidence resolving how BBB transport complements rapid LA catabolism (`demar2006`, `taha2020`).** A central puzzle in brain lipid biology is how neural membranes accumulate high concentrations of DHA while keeping parent LA below ~2% of total fatty acids. Two distinct transport regimes operate across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). For unesterified (non-esterified) fatty acids—including circulating free LA, oleic acid, and AA—Hamilton and Brunaldi {{< cite "hamilton2007" >}} outlined a biophysical flip-flop diffusion model in which protonated free fatty acids rapidly cross endothelial plasma membranes passively and are metabolically trapped intracellularly by acyl-CoA synthetases. In human brain microvessel endothelial cells, Mitchell and colleagues {{< cite "mitchell2011" >}} showed that membrane-associated proteins—principally FATP-1, FATP-4, and fatty-acid translocase/CD36—are abundantly expressed and facilitate transcellular permeation of unesterified fatty acids; siRNA knockdown of these proteins reduced fatty acid transport across human BBB monolayers.

In stark contrast, Nguyen and colleagues {{< cite "nguyen2014" >}} discovered that the endothelial orphan symporter **Mfsd2a** is the primary active transporter delivering DHA into the brain—not as a free fatty acid, but specifically in the form of **lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC-DHA)** via a sodium-dependent mechanism. Mice lacking `Mfsd2a` showed markedly depleted brain DHA, hippocampal and cerebellar neuronal loss, cognitive and anxiety deficits, and microcephaly. Confirming human relevance, Guemez-Gamboa and colleagues {{< cite "guemezgamboa2015" >}} identified homozygous inactivating mutations in human `MFSD2A` that abolish LPC-lipid transport and cause an autosomal recessive lethal primary microcephaly syndrome with severe hypomyelination and neurodevelopmental failure.

**What this proves—and does not prove.** Together with `demar2006`, these findings prove that low brain LA is *not* due to an inability of LA to cross the BBB: unesterified LA readily traverses brain endothelium via passive diffusion and FATP/CD36-assisted uptake, but once inside the parenchyma it is preferentially shunted into β-oxidation rather than phospholipid esterification, whereas DHA is actively imported as LPC-DHA via `Mfsd2a` for structural incorporation. These transport data do *not* establish that dietary LA competes directly with `Mfsd2a`-mediated LPC-DHA uptake at physiological human intakes, nor do they show that raising plasma unesterified LA forces structural LA accumulation in healthy mature brain membranes.

#### Astrocytes & Microglia: Glial β-Oxidation, Local Ketogenesis, Lipotoxicity Buffering, & Microglial Polarization

**Cell-type metabolic division of labor in the central nervous system.** Neurons have very limited intrinsic capacity for mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation and are highly susceptible to oxidative stress from excess free fatty acids. In classic primary-culture comparisons of developing brain cells, Edmond and colleagues {{< cite "edmond1987" >}} demonstrated that while neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes all oxidize glucose and ketone bodies, **only astrocytes** efficiently oxidize long-chain free fatty acids. Moore and colleagues {{< cite "moore1991" >}} similarly showed in primary cultures that astrocytes—not neurons—possess the desaturase/elongase machinery to convert 18-carbon precursors (LA and ALA) into AA and DHA and export them to neighboring neurons. (Crucially, however, intact adult rodent tracer studies such as `demar2006` show that *in vivo*, hepatic conversion supplies nearly all brain AA and DHA, whereas incoming LA in mature brain is overwhelmingly oxidized rather than elongated—highlighting an important boundary between developing primary astrocyte cultures and the intact adult brain.)

Extending astrocytic fatty acid oxidation to brain energy metabolism, Guzmán and Blázquez {{< cite "guzman2001" >}} proposed an **astrocyte-neuron ketone body shuttle**, and later synthesized {{< cite "guzman2004" >}} how astrocytic β-oxidation and local ketogenesis serve a dual neuroprotective role: scavenging excess unesterified fatty acids (preventing lipotoxic ceramide accumulation) while exporting ketone bodies as an alternative neuronal fuel during glucose scarcity. Providing direct mechanistic resolution in active neural circuits, Ioannou and colleagues {{< cite "ioannou2019" >}} demonstrated that hyperactive neurons generate excess peroxidized fatty acids that are packaged into ApoE-containing particles, transferred to astrocytes, sequestered into astrocytic **lipid droplets**, and safely disposed of via astrocytic mitochondrial β-oxidation—protecting neurons from activity-induced lipotoxicity.

**Microglial phenotype and the n-6 vs. n-3 distinction.** Microglia—the brain's resident innate immune cells—also remodel their membrane phospholipids and eicosanoid/docosanoid signaling in response to dietary PUFA supply. Madore and colleagues {{< cite "madore2014" >}} showed in mice that perinatal dietary omega-3 deficiency (a high-LA, n-3-deficient diet) shifts developing microglia toward a primed, pro-inflammatory phenotype with altered motility, elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression by postnatal day 21, and disrupted synaptic-plasticity gene expression. Reviewing the broader neuroimmunology literature, Layé and colleagues {{< cite "laye2018" >}} detailed how omega-3 PUFAs and their lipid mediators promote resolving, anti-inflammatory microglial states and dampen LPS- or injury-induced neuroinflammation, whereas AA-derived eicosanoids typically amplify acute inflammatory signaling.

**Boundary of the evidence.** Glial biology proves that astrocytes are the primary cellular site buffering and β-oxidizing free fatty acids in the CNS, and that perinatal n-3 depletion alters microglial priming in rodents. It does *not* prove that dietary LA at adequate n-3 intakes independently triggers chronic microglial activation in humans, nor can primary astrocyte culture conversion rates (`moore1991`) be extrapolated to imply substantial adult human brain LA → AA synthesis *in vivo*.

#### Sleep, Circadian Rhythm, & Fever: Hypothalamic PGE₂ (`EP3`) and Meningeal PGD₂ (`DP1`)

**Direct genetic proof in mice that AA-derived prostaglandins govern thermoregulation and physiological sleep.** Framing the omega-6/arachidonic acid cascade exclusively as a pathological inflammatory pathway misses two foundational homeostatic functions in the central nervous system: fever generation and sleep-wake regulation.

In a landmark knockout study, Ushikubi and colleagues {{< cite "ushikubi1998" >}} disrupted each of the four prostaglandin E₂ receptor subtypes (`EP1`–`EP4`) in mice and showed that only mice lacking **`EP3`** failed to mount a febrile response to peripheral lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or interleukin-1β, establishing PGE₂–EP3 signaling as the essential final mediator of fever. Pinpointing the exact neural locus, Lazarus and colleagues {{< cite "lazarus2007" >}} selectively deleted `EP3` receptors in the median preoptic nucleus (MnPO) of the hypothalamus in mice and abolished LPS- and PGE₂-induced fever, confirming that hypothalamic PGE₂ acts directly on preoptic EP3 neurons to disinhibit sympathetic thermogenesis.

Separately, the Hayaishi/Urade group established **prostaglandin D₂ (PGD₂)**—synthesized from AA by lipocalin-type prostaglandin D synthase in the leptomeninges and choroid plexus—as one of the most potent endogenous sleep-inducing signals in mammals. Mizoguchi and colleagues {{< cite "mizoguchi2001" >}} localized PGD₂ (`DP1`) receptors predominantly to arachnoid trabecular cells in the ventral basal forebrain and demonstrated that `DP1`-knockout mice fail to increase non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep in response to PGD₂ infusion. As synthesized by Urade and Hayaishi {{< cite "urade2011" >}}, PGD₂ binding to meningeal DP1 receptors stimulates extracellular adenosine release, activating A₂A receptors to excite the sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) and inhibit the wake-promoting tuberomammillary nucleus—operating in dynamic opposition to wake-promoting PGE₂ pathways.

**Boundary of the evidence.** These experiments definitively prove that endogenous AA-derived prostaglandins (`PGE₂` and `PGD₂`) are essential physiological regulators of mammalian fever and sleep-wake homeostasis. They do *not* imply that increasing dietary LA above baseline sufficiency raises body temperature, induces somnolence, or disrupts human circadian rhythms; tissue AA supply for basal COX signaling is tightly buffered across ordinary human dietary LA ranges.

#### Mood, Depression, Impulsivity, & Aggression: Separating LA-Specific Data from Omega-3 Deficiency Data

**A field frequently conflating high dietary LA with low dietary EPA/DHA.** Claims linking dietary linoleic acid to depression, impulsivity, and violence rest on three very different tiers of human evidence that must be kept strictly separate.

1. **Ecological and prospective observational data on LA and mood/violence.** Hibbeln, Nieminen, and Lands {{< cite "hibbeln2004" >}} correlated national apparent LA consumption (from FAO food balance sheets) with homicide mortality across five Western countries from 1961 to 2000, reporting a strong positive ecological correlation. However, ecological time-series analyses cannot isolate dietary LA from concurrent societal shifts (demographics, urbanization, lead exposure, substance use, or economic change) and cannot establish causality. At the individual level, Wolfe and colleagues {{< cite "wolfe2009" >}} followed 4,856 U.S. adults (NHANES I) for ~10.6 years and found that higher baseline dietary LA intake was associated with a higher odds of severe depressed mood in **men** (adjusted OR 2.34 for highest vs. lowest tertile, *p*-trend = 0.001), whereas no such LA association appeared in women (where oleic acid was inversely associated). In 54,632 women from the Nurses' Health Study followed for 10 years, Lucas and colleagues {{< cite "lucas2011" >}} found no independent linear increase in clinical depression from LA alone, but observed an interaction: the inverse association between plant omega-3 (ALA) intake and clinical depression was stronger among women with lower LA intake.
2. **Randomized controlled trial evidence for depression.** In a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating omega-3 and total polyunsaturated fats for prevention of depression and anxiety symptoms (31 long-chain omega-3 trials, *n* = 41,470), Deane and colleagues {{< cite "deane2021" >}} concluded that increasing long-chain omega-3 intake probably has **little or no effect** on preventing depression symptoms (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92–1.10) or anxiety, and explicitly highlighted a striking evidence gap: virtually no randomized trials have isolated dietary omega-6/LA reduction as a standalone intervention for clinical depression.
3. **Randomized controlled trial evidence for aggression and antisocial behavior.** In a double-blind RCT of 231 young adult prisoners, Gesch and colleagues {{< cite "gesch2002" >}} found that daily supplementation with a combined multivitamin, mineral, and essential fatty acid capsule (containing both omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, including GLA and LA alongside EPA/DHA) reduced disciplinary offenses by 26.3% overall (and violent offenses by ~37% among completers)—though the multi-nutrient formulation cannot isolate fatty acids from micronutrients, nor did it restrict dietary LA. Focusing specifically on omega-3 interventions, a 2024 meta-analysis of 29 randomized controlled trials (35 independent samples, *n* = 3,918) by Raine and Brodrick {{< cite "raine2024" >}} found a modest, statistically significant reduction in both reactive and proactive aggression with omega-3 supplementation (overall effect size *g* = 0.22).

**Reconciling the evidence.** Human RCTs support a small beneficial effect of *omega-3 supplementation* on aggressive behavior (`raine2024`), show little or no preventive benefit of omega-3 supplementation on incident depression in general populations (`deane2021`), and provide **no direct randomized clinical proof** that lowering dietary LA independently prevents depression or reduces human violence. Observational signals (`wolfe2009`, `lucas2011`) and cross-national correlations (`hibbeln2004`) remain hypothesis-generating and heavily confounded by overall diet quality and omega-3 status.

#### Neural function and behavior

**A plausible mechanism in mice, not validation of the entire symptom list.** Lafourcade and colleagues {{< cite "lafourcade2011" >}} found that lifelong dietary omega-3 insufficiency disrupted endocannabinoid-mediated synaptic plasticity in the prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens of mice and was associated with altered emotional behavior.

This connects a defined nutritional manipulation to specific neural signaling and behavioral outcomes. It does not establish that EFA deficiency causes all the listed human psychiatric conditions, or that those conditions identify an EFA deficiency. Depression, aggression, attention changes, compulsive behavior, and eating disorders are distinct outcomes requiring their own evidence. The long list should be treated as a set of research questions rather than a validated deficiency syndrome.

### 14. Endocrinology, Insulin Sensitivity, Thyroid and Adrenal Axes, and Comparative Adaptation

#### Insulin Sensitivity & Type 2 Diabetes: Human Evidence vs. Rodent Obesogenic Models

**Prospective human cohorts and isocaloric feeding trials generally favor LA; several mouse high-fat models show metabolic harm—with a critical caveat on soybean oil.** In the largest pooled human biomarker analysis to date, Wu and colleagues {{< cite "wu2017" >}} harmonized individual-level data across 20 prospective cohorts (39,740 adults) and found that higher circulating or tissue LA proportions were associated with a 35% lower risk of incident type 2 diabetes per interquintile range (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.60–0.72), whereas AA showed no significant association (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.88–1.05). Consistent with this direction, a meta-analysis of 102 randomized controlled feeding trials by Imamura and colleagues {{< cite "imamura2016" >}} found that isocalorically replacing saturated fat or carbohydrate with polyunsaturated fat (predominantly LA in most trials) improved HbA1c and measures of glycemic control and insulin secretion. In a 10-week isocaloric RCT of 67 abdominally obese adults without weight loss, Bjermo and colleagues {{< cite "bjermo2012" >}} likewise found that an n-6 PUFA-rich vegetable-oil diet lowered intrahepatic fat and LDL cholesterol relative to a butter-rich saturated-fat diet without raising inflammatory or oxidative-stress markers.

**Contrasting rodent evidence and its boundaries.** In mice, however, Alvheim and colleagues {{< cite "alvheim2012" >}} reported that raising dietary LA from 1% to 8% of energy elevated hepatic AA and endocannabinoids (2-AG and anandamide) and increased adiposity and weight gain. Similarly, `deol2015` (already annotated in the notes) found greater obesity, fatty liver, and diabetes in C57BL/6 mice fed soybean oil than coconut oil. Yet a crucial follow-up by Deol and colleagues {{< cite "deol2020" >}} complicated a simple LA-toxicity explanation: both conventional high-LA soybean oil *and* genetically modified low-LA, high-oleic soybean oil (Plenish) strongly dysregulated hypothalamic gene expression (including oxytocin) and impaired glucose tolerance relative to coconut oil, indicating that LA itself was not the sole or primary driver of that hypothalamic phenotype.

**Reconciling the conflict.** Human observational biomarker cohorts cannot exclude confounding or metabolic reverse causation (insulin resistance itself alters Δ6 desaturase activity and LA partitioning), and human RCTs test short-to-medium-term isocaloric surrogate endpoints rather than lifelong ad libitum intake. Conversely, rodent models frequently use extreme high-fat purified diets (35–60% energy), specific inbred strains (C57BL/6), and whole-oil matrices whose non-LA components or background n-3 levels vary. Neither body of evidence justifies treating LA as universally protective or universally diabetogenic independent of energy balance, food matrix, omega-3 background, and species.

#### Adrenal Steroidogenesis & Cholesteryl Linoleate

**Well-established cholesterol delivery pathways; oxidized LA derivatives stimulate steroidogenesis in vitro.** Extending the tension noted between `bistrian1981` (human TPN case of probable glucocorticoid insufficiency) and `vahouny1979` (preserved corticosterone output in EFA-deficient rat adrenal cells), adrenocortical cells maintain large intracellular lipid droplets rich in polyunsaturated cholesteryl esters (including cholesteryl linoleate and arachidonate) that serve as rapid cholesterol reserves for steroidogenesis. Temel and colleagues {{< cite "temel1997" >}} established that scavenger receptor class B, type I (SR-BI) is the primary route delivering HDL cholesteryl esters to the steroidogenic pathway in mouse adrenocortical cells, while Kraemer {{< cite "kraemer2007" >}} reviewed how hormone-sensitive lipase mobilizes stored cholesteryl esters and highlighted an important species divergence: rodents rely predominantly on HDL/SR-BI uptake, whereas human adrenals draw substantially from both LDL and HDL pathways.

Separately, Goodfriend and colleagues {{< cite "goodfriend2002" >}} showed that an oxidized derivative of linoleic acid—12,13-epoxy-9-oxo-10(*trans*)-octadecenoic acid (EKODE, the same class of metabolite linked to NRF2 activation in `wang2009`)—stimulated aldosterone production in isolated rat zona glomerulosa cells and corticosterone production in zona fasciculata cells at 1–30 µM, while inhibiting steroidogenesis above 50 µM. This demonstrates a biphasic in vitro signaling effect of an oxidized LA derivative on adrenal cells; it does not establish that dietary LA drives human hyperaldosteronism or that dietary LA deficiency reliably causes adrenal failure in vivo.

#### Thyroid Hormone, Desaturases (`FADS1`/`FADS2`), & Membrane Remodeling

**Direct animal evidence that thyroid state governs LA desaturation and membrane architecture.** Classic biochemical studies established that thyroid status markedly alters the handling of linoleic acid and membrane phospholipids. Faas and Carter {{< cite "faas1982" >}} showed that experimental hypothyroidism in rats reduced hepatic microsomal Δ9 desaturation by 80% and Δ6 (linoleate) desaturation by 43%, causing LA (18:2n-6) to accumulate and arachidonate (20:4n-6) to fall in phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine—changes completely reversed by T3 treatment. Yet in experimental hyperthyroidism, Faas and Carter {{< cite "faas1981" >}} observed a more complex pattern: hepatic microsomal arachidonate proportions increased even though measured Δ6 desaturation of linoleate could be depressed depending on physiological state, reflecting coordinated systemic shifts in lipid turnover, oxidation, and redistribution rather than a single one-way enzymatic throttle.

Synthesizing decades of membrane biochemistry, Hoch {{< cite "hoch1988" >}} documented how thyroid hormones remodel mitochondrial and microsomal acyl-chain profiles (including linoleate-rich cardiolipin and phospholipid unsaturation) to match metabolic demand. Pehowich {{< cite "pehowich1999" >}} further demonstrated that thyroid status and dietary fatty-acid composition interact—via desaturase regulation and membrane unsaturation—to influence mitochondrial proton leak. Hulbert {{< cite "hulbert2000" >}} integrated these observations into the comparative "membrane pacemaker" framework connecting thyroid-regulated membrane polyunsaturation to cellular metabolic rate. Crucially, however, these findings show that *thyroid hormone regulates how membranes process and remodel LA and other PUFAs*; together with `rafael1988` (where extra dietary LA did not raise basal metabolic rate in rats), they do not establish that eating more or less LA independently controls human thyroid function or whole-body metabolic rate.

#### Hibernation & Torpor: Adaptive Physiology & the Peroxidation Tradeoff

**Strong comparative and mechanistic evidence across multiple hibernating mammals.** One of the clearest demonstrations that linoleic acid is not merely an accidental modern industrial artifact comes from mammalian hibernation physiology. Geiser and Kenagy {{< cite "geiser1987" >}} showed that chipmunks (*Eutamias amoenus*) fed a polyunsaturated, LA-rich diet entered deeper torpor with lower minimum body temperatures, lower torpor metabolic rates, and significantly longer torpor bouts than animals fed a saturated-fat diet. Florant and colleagues {{< cite "florant1993" >}} demonstrated that yellow-bellied marmots (*Marmota flaviventris*) placed on a low-EFA/low-LA diet showed impaired hibernation expression and altered white adipose fatty-acid composition, confirming a physiological requirement for dietary LA in heterothermic survival.

**Active diet selection and an upper bound set by lipid peroxidation.** Frank {{< cite "frank1994" >}} demonstrated that golden-mantled ground squirrels (*Spermophilus lateralis*) actively select diets enriched in linoleic acid prior to winter hibernation. Crucially, however, Frank and Storey {{< cite "frank1995" >}} showed that more LA is *not* monotonically better: hibernation performance peaked at an *intermediate optimal* depot LA concentration, while excessive PUFA loading impaired torpor and increased vulnerability to lipid peroxidation during periodic rewarming (inter-bout arousals), when oxygen consumption surges.

**The cardiac SERCA2a mechanism.** Ruf and Arnold {{< cite "ruf2008" >}} synthesized the hibernation literature to hypothesize that high membrane LA prevents fatal cold-induced cardiac arrhythmias by sustaining sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (**SERCA2a**) activity at near-freezing body temperatures. Giroud and colleagues {{< cite "giroud2013" >}} tested this directly in Syrian hamsters (*Mesocricetus auratus*): cardiac sarcoplasmic reticulum SERCA activity rose strongly with phospholipid LA proportion and declined with DHA proportion; hamsters entering deep torpor maintained high cardiac SR LA and low DHA and tolerated lower minimum body temperatures, whereas individuals that failed to enter torpor retained summer-like low-LA, high-DHA cardiac profiles.

**Boundary of the analogy.** Hibernation physiology proves that LA plays an indispensable biophysical role in cold-adapted cardiac calcium handling and illustrates a genuine evolutionary optimum bounded above by lipid peroxidation. It does *not* imply that non-hibernating, homeothermic humans enter "torpor" or lower their basal metabolic rate when consuming linoleic acid—extrapolating adaptive hibernator membrane specialization directly to human weight gain or metabolic slowing is an untested leap, not a finding of these experiments.

#### Pancreatic β-Cell Biology: Acute GPR40 (FFAR1) Insulin Potentiation vs. Chronic Glucolipotoxicity

**Mechanistic resolution aligning with human diabetes cohorts (`wu2017`) and feeding RCTs (`imamura2016`).** Free fatty acids play a well-defined biphasic role in pancreatic β-cell physiology: acutely they amplify glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS), whereas chronic elevation of specific lipid species alongside hyperglycemia ("glucolipotoxicity") impairs β-cell survival.

**Acute potentiation via GPR40 (FFAR1).** Itoh and colleagues {{< cite "itoh2003" >}} discovered that the orphan G-protein-coupled receptor **GPR40** (now **FFAR1**) is highly expressed in pancreatic β-cells and serves as a direct receptor for medium- and long-chain free fatty acids—including linoleic acid, oleic acid, and palmitic acid—amplifying GSIS via Gq/11–Ca²⁺ signaling. Addressing early debate over whether chronic GPR40 signaling mediated β-cell failure, Latour and colleagues {{< cite "latour2007" >}} studied `Gpr40-/-` mice and showed that GPR40 is required for roughly half of the *acute* fatty-acid potentiation of insulin secretion *in vivo*, yet `Gpr40-/-` and wild-type islets were equally susceptible to chronic fatty-acid-induced secretory impairment *in vitro*, demonstrating that GPR40 mediates acute physiological nutrient sensing rather than chronic lipotoxicity.

**The palmitate vs. linoleate contrast in β-cell lipotoxicity.** Chronic β-cell apoptosis under elevated free fatty acids is strongly acyl-chain-specific. Kharroubi and colleagues {{< cite "kharroubi2004" >}} established that free fatty acids kill β-cells via **endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress** rather than the NF-κB pathway used by inflammatory cytokines. Cunha and colleagues {{< cite "cunha2008" >}} dissected this lipotoxic ER stress further, demonstrating that the saturated fatty acid **palmitate** causes rapid ER Ca²⁺ depletion, severe activation of PERK/IRE1/ATF6 unfolded protein response branches, and marked β-cell apoptosis, whereas unsaturated fatty acids induce much milder ER stress and far less cell death. Directly characterizing polyunsaturated fatty acids in insulin-secreting BRIN-BD11 cells, Dhayal and Morgan {{< cite "dhayal2011" >}} showed that unsaturated species—including **linoleate (18:2n-6)** and other long-chain PUFAs—do not trigger palmitate-like lipotoxicity and instead exert **cytoprotective** effects against saturated-fat-induced apoptosis via a mechanism independent of β-oxidation or COX/LOX metabolite formation.

**Boundary of the evidence.** This β-cell biochemistry provides a plausible cellular mechanism consistent with human meta-analyses (`imamura2016`, `wu2017`) showing improved insulin secretion and lower type 2 diabetes risk when polyunsaturated fat replaces saturated fat. However, isolated islet and clonal β-cell experiments apply supra-physiological free fatty acid ratios over hours to days without systemic adipose-liver crosstalk, so they do not prove that high-dose LA supplementation preserves human β-cell mass over decades in obesity.

#### Adipokines (Adiponectin & Leptin) & the HPA Stress Axis

**Human trial evidence on adipokines.** Adipose tissue secretes key endocrine hormones—notably insulin-sensitizing **adiponectin** and satiety/energy-balance hormone **leptin**—whose responsiveness to dietary LA has been directly tested in human interventions. In a 16-week randomized, double-masked crossover trial of 35 obese postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes comparing 8 g/day of conjugated linoleic acid (CLA) against 8 g/day of LA-rich **safflower oil** (~78% LA), Norris and colleagues {{< cite "norris2009" >}} unexpectedly found that safflower oil significantly reduced trunk adipose mass, lowered fasting glucose, and **increased serum adiponectin** (*P* = 0.0051). A time-course follow-up from the same group by Asp and colleagues {{< cite "asp2011" >}} confirmed that daily safflower oil progressively elevated adiponectin and HDL-C while lowering HbA1c and C-reactive protein over 16 weeks. Because these trials enrolled a small, specific clinical group (postmenopausal women with T2D) and compared safflower oil against CLA (which can worsen insulin resistance) rather than an inert oil placebo, they demonstrate metabolic compatibility—and possible adiponectin elevation—of modest supplemental LA in insulin-resistant adults, but do not prove broad adiponectin raising in lean healthy populations.

For **leptin**, Kratz and colleagues {{< cite "kratz2002" >}} conducted a controlled dietary trial in 55 healthy non-obese men and women comparing high-monounsaturated olive oil, high-LA sunflower oil, and high-ALA rapeseed oil diets; **neither the LA-rich sunflower oil diet nor the olive oil diet altered serum leptin concentrations**, whereas the ALA-rich rapeseed oil diet produced sex-divergent shifts (lower leptin in women). By contrast, Reseland and colleagues {{< cite "reseland2001" >}} showed in humans, rats, and cultured adipocytes that marine **n-3 PUFAs** (EPA/DHA) reduce plasma leptin and directly downregulate *LEP* gene promoter activity via PPARγ/SREBP-1 pathways. Thus, dietary LA does not appear to suppress or dysregulate basal human serum leptin independently of body fat mass.

**The HPA stress axis and prostaglandin signaling.** Extending Pass 1's adrenal steroidogenesis review (`bistrian1981`, `vahouny1979`, `goodfriend2002`), the central hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) stress response during systemic inflammation relies critically on AA-derived **prostaglandin E₂**. Matsuoka and colleagues {{< cite "matsuoka2003" >}} demonstrated that mice deficient in either `EP1` or `EP3` prostaglandin E₂ receptors exhibit markedly blunted **adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)** release and reduced hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) c-Fos activation in response to bacterial endotoxin (LPS), proving that PGE₂ mediates cytokine-driven CRH/ACTH activation. Reviewing the broader stress neurobiology literature, Furuyashiki and Narumiya {{< cite "furuyashiki2011" >}} synthesized how PGE₂ acts through distinct EP receptor subtypes (`EP1`, `EP3`, `EP4`) to orchestrate both immune-stress HPA activation and psychological/social-defeat stress responses—including glucocorticoid surge, fever, and behavioral adaptation. This confirms an essential physiological role for the LA → AA → PGE₂ pathway in acute stress neuroendocrinology; it does *not* show that higher dietary LA intake chronically elevates human cortisol or triggers HPA axis hyperactivity.

#### Comparative & Ecological Physiology: Migratory Birds & Cold-Acclimated Ectotherms and Non-Hibernating Mammals

**Migratory birds: n-6 LA priming vs. marine n-3 "natural doping."** Beyond mammalian hibernators (`geiser1987`–`giroud2013`), comparative physiology reveals that migratory birds actively exploit dietary fatty acids to maximize sustained aerobic flight—using *different* PUFA classes depending on ecological niche. In red-eyed vireos (*Vireo olivaceus*), Pierce and colleagues {{< cite "pierce2005" >}} showed that dietary unsaturation and linoleic acid (18:2n-6) composition directly reshape adipose stores and alter basal and exercise peak metabolic rates. Testing muscle membrane function directly in migratory white-throated sparrows (*Zonotrichia albicollis*), Price and Guglielmo {{< cite "price2009" >}} compared a high-n-6 (LA-rich) diet against a high-n-3 diet: sparrows fed the **high-n-6 diet achieved a significantly higher peak metabolic rate (PMR)** despite no differences in oxidative enzyme activities or fatty-acid transporter mRNA expression, pointing to a direct biophysical or signaling advantage of n-6-enriched flight-muscle membranes in terrestrial passerines.

In contrast, Maillet and Weber {{< cite "maillet2006" >}} documented a marine-n-3 strategy in long-distance migrant semipalmated sandpipers (*Calidris pusilla*): during Bay of Fundy stopovers, sandpipers binge on mud shrimp (*Corophium volutator*) extraordinarily rich in EPA and DHA, incorporating n-3 LC-PUFAs into flight-muscle membranes and upregulating aerobic oxidative enzymes ("natural doping"). Synthesizing avian and vertebrate exercise physiology, Pierce and McWilliams {{< cite "pierce2014" >}} framed three non-exclusive mechanisms—the **fuel hypothesis** (faster mobilization/oxidation of unsaturated depot lipids), **membrane hypothesis** (optimal phospholipid unsaturation for transmembrane pumps and mitochondrial respiration), and **signal hypothesis** (PUFA activation of PPARs)—while emphasizing that optimal n-6 vs. n-3 balance is species- and diet-context-specific and carries an oxidative-damage tradeoff during intense aerobic flight.

**Cold-acclimated ectotherms and non-hibernating endotherms.** In ectotherms (fishes, amphibians, reptiles, invertebrates), Hazel {{< cite "hazel1995" >}} reviewed **homeoviscous adaptation** and dynamic phase behavior: cold acclimation systematically elevates membrane phospholipid unsaturation (incorporating both n-3 and n-6 PUFAs and adjusting headgroup ratios) to preserve fluidity, bilayer stability, and membrane-enzyme function at low temperatures. Remarkably, this seasonal membrane remodeling is not restricted to ectotherms or diet-driven hibernators. Arnold and colleagues {{< cite "arnold2011" >}} showed in free-living alpine marmots (*Marmota marmota*) that large seasonal surges in tissue phospholipid n-6 PUFA (supporting cold cardiac SERCA activity) occur **independently of dietary PUFA supply** and *precede* the winter drop in body temperature under endogenous circannual control. Extending this discovery to a large **non-hibernating mammal**, Gasch and colleagues {{< cite "gasch2024" >}} fed red deer (*Cervus elaphus*) diets supplemented with either LA-rich sunflower oil or ALA-rich linseed oil across seasons and found that dietary LA/ALA manipulation had little effect on skeletal-muscle phospholipid remodeling; instead, **photoperiod** drove seasonal membrane adjustments in which sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) activity scaled directly with SR phospholipid **n-6 PUFA** concentration to counteract cold-induced slowing in winter peripheral tissues.

**Boundary of the comparative evidence.** Together, avian migration, ectotherm thermal adaptation, and mammalian seasonal acclimatization demonstrate that vertebrates have repeatedly evolved specialized mechanisms to deploy linoleic acid and other PUFAs for aerobic capacity and cold-tolerant calcium pumping—often tightly regulated by endogenous circannual/photoperiodic programs rather than passive dietary mirroring. These adaptations underscore LA's deep evolutionary utility, while simultaneously cautioning against simplistic extrapolations from specialized migratory or seasonal wild species to sedentary, thermoneutral human metabolism.

#### Stress hormones—suggestive and contrary findings

**A human case supports the possibility; an animal experiment limits the generalization.** Bistrian and colleagues {{< cite "bistrian1981" >}} described probable glucocorticoid insufficiency and blood abnormalities during prolonged fat-free feeding, alongside biochemical EFA deficiency. The abnormalities responded to fat repletion. A single case suggests a connection but cannot establish a universal requirement or isolate every contributing factor.

In contrast, Vahouny, Hodges, and Treadwell {{< cite "vahouny1979" >}} found normal stimulated corticosterone output from adrenal cells of EFA-deficient rats despite major changes in stored fatty acids. The proposed chain from deficiency to failed stress response to degeneration should therefore be read as a hypothesis, not a consequence demonstrated across organisms and settings.

#### Sex differences are supported; proportionality to muscle is not

**Human evidence points to hormonal regulation.** Giltay and colleagues {{< cite "giltay2004" >}} found a higher DHA proportion in women than men eating controlled diets. In hormone-treatment groups, oral ethinyl estradiol increased DHA and testosterone reduced it; transdermal estradiol did not show the same increase as oral treatment.

These findings support sex- and hormone-associated differences in fatty-acid status. They do not establish that those differences are proportional to muscle mass, or that muscle mass explains the hormonal effects. The proportionality claim remains unverified rather than a finding that can be attributed to this study.

### 15. Reproduction, Placenta, Lactation, and Developmental Programming

#### Spermatogenesis & Testicular Very-Long-Chain PUFAs

**Genetic knockout evidence refining `stoffel2020` and `roquetarivera2010`.** Existing annotations (`stroud2009`, `roquetarivera2010`, `stoffel2020`) showed that mice lacking Δ6 desaturase (`Fads2-/-`) suffer male infertility that can be rescued by downstream DHA (or combined AA + DHA). Zadravec and colleagues {{< cite "zadravec2011" >}} revealed a deeper downstream specificity by ablating the elongase `Elovl2` in mice: `Elovl2-/-` males completely lacked testicular C24–C30 n-6 very-long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (specifically 28:5n-6 and 30:5n-6, derived along the LA → AA → 22:5n-6 elongation axis) and suffered complete spermatogenic arrest at primary spermatocytes, while heterozygotes showed haploinsufficiency and malformed sperm heads. Critically, dietary DHA *failed* to rescue fertility in `Elovl2-/-` males, demonstrating that elongated n-6 VLC-PUFAs play an irreplaceable structural role in murine germ-cell maturation when `Elovl2` is absent (whereas `Fads2-/-` mice retain `Elovl2` and can elongate supplied 20–22-carbon PUFAs).

As reviewed by Wathes, Abayasekara, and Aitken {{< cite "wathes2007" >}}, species differences are substantial: rodent testes are heavily enriched in LA-derived docosapentaenoic acid (22:5n-6) and n-6 VLC-PUFAs, whereas human and ruminant spermatozoa are predominantly enriched in DHA (22:6n-3). Thus, murine knockouts establish the necessity of PUFA elongation for spermatogenesis, but exact n-6 versus n-3 ratios cannot be transposed unchanged from mice to human male fertility.

#### Ovarian Follicle Signaling, Implantation, & Parturition

**Direct genetic proof in mice that omega-6 prostaglandin pathways govern female reproduction.** Discussions that frame LA-derived arachidonic acid solely as a driver of chronic pathology overlook its foundational role in mammalian reproduction. Lim and colleagues {{< cite "lim1997" >}} showed that mice lacking cyclooxygenase-2 (`COX-2-/-`) experience multiple female reproductive failures spanning ovulation, fertilization, implantation, and decidualization. In the same year, Sugimoto and colleagues {{< cite "sugimoto1997" >}} demonstrated that mice lacking the prostaglandin F₂α receptor (`FP-/-`) fail to undergo luteolysis and progesterone withdrawal at term and consequently fail to initiate parturition, a defect rescued by ovariectomy.

Wathes and colleagues {{< cite "wathes2007" >}} synthesized how dietary LA and ALA modulate follicular fluid composition, steroidogenesis, and uterine prostaglandin synthesis (PGE₂ and PGF₂α) across mammalian species. Together, these studies confirm that downstream omega-6 eicosanoids are essential physiological mediators of ovulation, implantation, and labor. They do not establish that increasing dietary LA above baseline physiological sufficiency improves human fertility or pregnancy outcomes.

#### Selective Placental Fatty Acid Transport ("Biomagnification")

**Direct ex vivo perfusion and in vivo human stable-isotope tracer evidence extending `mackay2012`.** Rather than passively mirroring maternal blood fat proportions, the human placenta selectively enriches long-chain PUFAs in the fetal circulation relative to maternal plasma—a process termed biomagnification. Using an ex vivo dually perfused human placental lobule model, Haggarty and colleagues {{< cite "haggarty1997" >}} demonstrated preferential maternal-to-fetal transfer favoring DHA and AA over parent LA. Duttaroy {{< cite "duttaroy2009" >}} reviewed the membrane transport machinery underlying this selectivity, including placental plasma membrane fatty-acid-binding protein (p-FABPpm), FAT/CD36, and fatty-acid transport proteins (FATP1 and FATP4).

Providing direct in vivo human confirmation, Gil-Sánchez and colleagues {{< cite "gilsanchez2010" >}} administered oral `[¹³C]`-labeled fatty acids to pregnant women 12 hours prior to cesarean delivery and measured isotopic enrichment in maternal plasma, placenta, and cord blood, demonstrating preferential placental uptake and maternal-to-fetal transfer of `[¹³C]`DHA relative to `[¹³C]`linoleic, oleic, and palmitic acids. This selective filtering protects fetal neural development from simple mirroring of maternal LA fluctuations, though as `mackay2012` illustrated in preeclampsia, maternal synthesis and placental transport can still be compromised in pregnancy complications.

#### Human Breast Milk Linoleic Acid Trends & Infant Growth / Neurodevelopment

**Deficiency threshold firmly established in human infants.** In a landmark clinical study of 428 infants fed milk mixtures varying in fat type and quantity, Hansen and colleagues {{< cite "hansen1963" >}} proved that linoleic acid is essential for human infant skin integrity and growth: infants receiving <0.1% of calories as LA developed dry, scaly skin and required higher caloric intake per unit of weight gain, whereas ~1% of calories prevented overt clinical deficiency and ~2–4% supported optimal growth and serum lipid profiles.

**Secular rise and global variation in human milk LA.** Compiling decades of human milk analyses, Ailhaud and colleagues {{< cite "ailhaud2006" >}} documented that the LA proportion of U.S. human breast milk rose roughly two- to three-fold from the 1940s–1950s (~6–7% of fatty acids) to the 1990s–2000s (~15–16%+) in parallel with industrial vegetable-oil consumption, while ALA changed little. Across nine countries, Yuhas, Pramuk, and Lien {{< cite "yuhas2006" >}} likewise documented substantial geographic differences in breast milk LA (reflecting maternal dietary oils) alongside even wider relative variation in breast milk DHA. While the historical rise in breast milk LA is well documented, Ailhaud and colleagues' stronger mechanistic hypothesis—that elevated early-life LA drives human childhood obesity via prostacyclin-stimulated adipogenesis—rests primarily on rodent and cell models rather than randomized human infant trials.

**Observational signals on early cognition—with clear causal limits.** In the French EDEN mother-child birth cohort, Bernard and colleagues {{< cite "bernard2015" >}} found that higher colostrum LA concentrations were negatively associated with child motor and cognitive scores at ages 2 and 3 years, independent of breastfeeding duration; infants receiving the highest-LA colostrum scored closer to never-breastfed peers. In a longer follow-up of 1,080 children from the same cohort at ages 5–6 years, Bernard and colleagues {{< cite "bernard2017" >}} confirmed that longer breastfeeding duration remained positively associated with overall IQ, whereas higher colostrum LA remained inversely associated with verbal IQ—most clearly when high LA coincided with low DHA. These cohort findings raise a concrete, testable concern about high maternal LA combined with low omega-3 status during infancy. However, as observational analyses using single colostrum samples, they cannot exclude residual socioeconomic, dietary, or genetic confounding, nor do they negate the broader developmental benefits of breastfeeding or establish an exact safe upper limit for breast milk LA.

#### Sperm Capacitation, Acrosome Reaction, & the PUFA Peroxidation Paradox

**Mechanistic reproduction biology extending `zadravec2011` and `wathes2007`.** Mature mammalian spermatozoa possess among the highest polyunsaturated phospholipid concentrations of any cell type—heavily enriched in DHA in humans and n-6 docosapentaenoic acid (22:5n-6) plus VLC-PUFAs in rodents (`wathes2007`, `zadravec2011`)—alongside minimal cytoplasm and sparse antioxidant defenses. This lipid architecture is essential for flagellar motility, plasma-membrane fluidity during **capacitation**, and the membrane-fusion events of the **acrosome reaction** required to penetrate the oocyte zona pellucida.

Early biochemical studies showed that regulated lipoxygenase oxidation of released PUFAs participates directly in acrosomal signaling: Lax and colleagues {{< cite "lax1990" >}} demonstrated in mammalian spermatozoa that pharmacological inhibition of lipoxygenase blocks the calcium-ionophore-induced acrosome reaction, whereas lipoxygenase products restore exocytosis, implicating endogenous PUFA oxygenation in physiological fertilization signaling. Yet this same PUFA-rich membrane creates a razor-thin oxidative margin. Koppers, Garg, and Aitken {{< cite "koppers2010" >}} demonstrated in defective human spermatozoa that unesterified polyunsaturated fatty acids (including **linoleic**, arachidonic, and docosahexaenoic acids) potently stimulate mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, initiating a self-propagating lipid peroxidation cascade that impairs motility and damages sperm DNA. Pinpointing a key enzymatic driver of this pathology, Walters and colleagues {{< cite "walters2018" >}} showed that **arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (`ALOX15`)** catalyzes PUFA oxidation in human spermatozoa to generate cytotoxic electrophilic aldehydes such as **4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)**; selective pharmacological inhibition of ALOX15 (PD146176) significantly reduced lipid peroxidation and 4-HNE adduct formation while preserving human sperm functional competence (including acrosome reaction and zona-binding capacity) under oxidative stress.

**Boundary of the evidence.** Sperm physiology highlights the classic PUFA double-edged sword: polyunsaturated membranes and regulated lipoxygenase/ROS signals are indispensable for capacitation and fertilization, while excess unesterified PUFAs or dysregulated `ALOX15`/mitochondrial ROS drive male subfertility via lipid peroxidation. These cellular studies do *not* prove that ordinary human dietary LA intakes impair male fertility in vivo, particularly given that human sperm membranes predominantly enrich DHA over LA (`wathes2007`).

#### Preeclampsia: Placental Prostacyclin/Thromboxane Imbalance, Lipid Peroxidation, & Low-Dose Aspirin

**Classic pathophysiology and definitive clinical trial confirmation extending `mackay2012`.** Preeclampsia—a hypertensive pregnancy disorder characterized by impaired spiral-artery remodeling, placental ischemia, and maternal endothelial dysfunction—illustrates how downstream arachidonic acid eicosanoids can shift from physiological homeostasis to acute vascular pathology. In a landmark study, Walsh {{< cite "walsh1985" >}} demonstrated that normal human placentas produce balanced amounts of vasodilatory, anti-aggregatory **prostacyclin (PGI₂)** and vasoconstrictive, pro-aggregatory **thromboxane A₂ (TxA₂)**, whereas placentas from preeclamptic pregnancies produce markedly excessive TxA₂ alongside deficient PGI₂—a roughly 7-fold shift in the TxA₂:PGI₂ ratio that drives placental vasoconstriction, platelet activation, and reduced uteroplacental perfusion. Hubel and colleagues {{< cite "hubel1989" >}} integrated this eicosanoid shift with oxidative stress, synthesizing evidence that excessive **lipid peroxidation** in preeclampsia selectively inhibits prostacyclin synthase while sparing or stimulating thromboxane synthesis and damaging vascular endothelium—a biochemical cascade that also aligns with `mackay2012`'s finding of compromised maternal LC-PUFA synthesis and fetal deficiency in preeclamptic pregnancies.

Providing definitive clinical proof that this AA-eicosanoid imbalance is causally modifiable in humans, the multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled **ASPRE trial** by Rolnik and colleagues {{< cite "rolnik2017" >}} randomized 1,776 women at high risk for preterm preeclampsia to low-dose aspirin (150 mg/day, started at 11–14 weeks' gestation) or placebo. Low-dose aspirin—which preferentially acetylates platelet COX-1 to suppress TxA₂ while largely sparing endothelial PGI₂ synthesis—reduced preterm preeclampsia from **4.3% to 1.6%** (adjusted OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20–0.74; *P* = 0.004).

**Boundary of the evidence.** Preeclampsia proves that endogenous COX-mediated AA metabolism (specifically the TxA₂:PGI₂ axis) and lipid peroxidation are central, clinically actionable drivers of human placental vascular disease. It does *not* establish that dietary linoleic acid intake causes preeclampsia or that lowering dietary LA replicates the targeted platelet-COX-1 pharmacology of low-dose aspirin.

#### Fetal & Neonatal Programming (DOHaD): Rodent Multigenerational High-LA Models vs. Human Longitudinal Cohorts & Maternal RCTs

**A classic Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) test case with sharp species, dose, and causal boundaries.** Building directly on `ailhaud2006` (which documented rising modern breast-milk LA and hypothesized prostacyclin-driven infant adipogenesis) and `bernard2015`/`bernard2017` (which linked high colostrum LA to modestly lower early-childhood cognitive scores in the French EDEN cohort), the adiposity-programming literature reveals an instructive divergence across three tiers of evidence:

1. **Rodent mechanistic and multigenerational models.** Massiera and colleagues {{< cite "massiera2003" >}} demonstrated in mice that feeding mothers a high-LA diet (versus a balanced LA+ALA diet) during gestation and lactation increased offspring body weight and epididymal fat-pad mass by weaning and into adulthood—an effect completely **abolished in mice lacking the prostacyclin receptor (`IP-/-`)**, proving that AA-derived prostacyclin signaling drives early preadipocyte differentiation and adipose hyperplasia in this murine model. Pushing this paradigm across generations, Massiera and colleagues {{< cite "massiera2010" >}} fed mice an isoenergetic "Western-like" high-LA/low-ALA diet (18.7% energy LA, 0.6% ALA; LA:ALA ratio ~28:1, later reaching 59:1 in related designs) over **four consecutive generations** and observed a progressive, transgenerational expansion of fat mass driven by combined adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia, accompanied by altered adipose gene expression (e.g., *Csf3*, *Apln*) and fasting insulin elevation without increased caloric intake.
2. **Human prospective pregnancy and birth cohorts.** Longitudinal human cohorts measuring maternal prenatal plasma PUFAs and child body composition show directionally intriguing—yet substantially smaller and more heterogeneous—associations:
     - In the U.S. **Project Viva** cohort (*n* = 1,250 mother-child pairs), Donahue and colleagues {{< cite "donahue2011" >}} found that higher maternal mid-pregnancy dietary and plasma n-3 LC-PUFA (EPA+DHA) status was associated with lower child adiposity (sum of subscapular and triceps skinfolds and obesity odds) at age 3 years, whereas a higher maternal and cord-blood **n-6:n-3 ratio** was associated with modestly higher skinfold thickness and obesity risk.
     - In the UK **Southampton Women's Survey** (*n* = 293 mother-child pairs assessed by DXA), Moon and colleagues {{< cite "moon2013" >}} reported that higher maternal late-pregnancy plasma **n-6 PUFA** (specifically LA and dihomo-γ-linolenic acid) positively predicted offspring fat mass at ages 4 and 6 years, whereas maternal n-3 PUFA showed no significant association with fat mass (though higher n-3 correlated with lean mass).
     - In the large Dutch **Generation R** cohort (*n* = 4,830 mother-child pairs), Vidakovic and colleagues {{< cite "vidakovic2016" >}} found that higher maternal second-trimester plasma **n-3 PUFA** concentrations were associated with lower childhood total-body fat percentage and android-to-gynoid fat ratio at median age 6 years, while higher maternal **n-6 PUFA** concentrations and higher n-6:n-3 ratios were associated with modestly higher total and abdominal (preperitoneal) fat measures.
3. **Human randomized controlled trials (the causal stress test).** When researchers tested the Ailhaud/Massiera hypothesis prospectively in human pregnancy and lactation RCTs, the adiposity-prevention signal did **not** replicate:
     - In the German **INFAT** open-label RCT (*n* = 208 pregnant women), Hauner and colleagues {{< cite "hauner2012" >}} explicitly intervened to **reduce the maternal n-6:n-3 LC-PUFA ratio** during pregnancy and lactation (combining 1.2 g/day EPA+DHA supplementation with dietary counseling to reduce arachidonic acid intake); despite shifting maternal and breast-milk fatty acid profiles significantly, the intervention had **no effect** on infant adipose tissue growth (sum of four skinfold thicknesses or ultrasound abdominal fat) through the first year of life.
     - In the long-term follow-up of the Australian double-blind **DOMInO** RCT (*n* = 1,531 children), Muhlhausler and colleagues {{< cite "muhlhausler2016" >}} found that maternal supplementation with 800 mg/day DHA during the second half of pregnancy (versus vegetable-oil placebo capsules) produced **no reduction** in child BMI z-scores or percentage body fat at ages 3 or 5 years, regardless of offspring *PPARγ* obesity-risk genotype.

**Reconciling the conflict.** Mouse models (`massiera2003`, `massiera2010`) use extreme dietary LA doses (~18–20% of energy) paired with severe ALA restriction and prolonged multigenerational exposure that rarely match human diets—and mice exhibit species-specific adipose developmental timing and high prostacyclin responsiveness. Human birth cohorts (`donahue2011`, `moon2013`, `vidakovic2016`) show modest, reproducible observational correlations linking higher prenatal n-6/n-3 ratios (or lower maternal n-3 status) to slightly higher childhood adiposity, yet observational pregnancy biomarkers remain susceptible to residual confounding by overall maternal dietary pattern, metabolic health, and socioeconomic status. Meanwhile, the null RCTs (`hauner2012`, `muhlhausler2016`) show that modifying maternal n-3 or AA intake during mid-to-late pregnancy does *not* measurably reduce early-childhood fat mass—though importantly, neither trial dramatically lowered maternal **parent LA** intake from modern Western levels (~6–8% energy) down to pre-industrial levels (~2–3% energy) starting prior to conception. Thus, while the murine prostacyclin-adipogenesis mechanism is real in rodents, causal proof that lowering maternal dietary LA prevents human childhood obesity does not currently exist.

#### Reproduction and membrane composition

**Strong experimental evidence in a defined mouse model.** Stoffel and colleagues {{< cite "stoffel2020" >}} studied male and female mice lacking FADS2, an enzyme needed to make longer-chain PUFAs from dietary precursors. The mice developed disrupted ovarian and testicular membrane organization and infertility. Sustained combined AA and DHA feeding restored the relevant structures and fertility, including in adult animals.

This supports an important role for PUFA composition in reproductive function and shows that precursor supply alone may be insufficient when conversion fails. It does not establish identical requirements in ordinary human diets, nor that supplying more LA or ALA treats every cause of infertility.
