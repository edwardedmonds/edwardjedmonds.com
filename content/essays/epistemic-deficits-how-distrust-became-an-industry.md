## Two Layers

The American economy runs on two layers, and the relationship between them explains a good deal about how distrust of expertise gets manufactured and what happens when the effort succeeds.

The first layer is built by experts. Engineers design bridges that hold, oncologists develop chemotherapy protocols that extend life, epidemiologists track disease vectors, and regulatory scientists test whether the food on your table will make you sick. This layer is boring, credentialed, and accountable, and it produces things that work. The second layer is built by people selling things that have not been shown to work: supplements that were never tested, wellness products that contradict the evidence, and health claims that would not survive peer review. It is exciting, populist, and largely unaccountable, and it produces revenue.

The two layers are not independent, because the second is parasitic on distrust of the first. That distrust has genuine cultural roots, but it has also been cultivated by identifiable actors, using documented strategies, for traceable financial and political gain. The historian Robert Proctor coined the term *agnotology* for the study of culturally produced ignorance, meaning ignorance that is made, maintained, and strategically deployed rather than simply left over from what science has not yet discovered {{< cite "proctor2008" >}}. This essay is an attempt to map that architecture, and I have tried to mark where the evidence is firm and where I am extrapolating.

## The Homegrown Vulnerability

Richard Hofstadter's *Anti-Intellectualism in American Life*, published in 1963 and awarded the 1964 Pulitzer Prize, traced suspicion of the life of the mind through the whole span of American history {{< cite "hofstadter1964" >}}. He defined it as a resentment of intellectual life and a disposition to minimize its value. This homegrown strain developed within American culture over centuries, through evangelical religion that prized spiritual experience over learning, a cult of the self-made man that set practical success against theory, populist politics that blurred expertise into elitism, and McCarthyism that cast intellectuals as subversive.

Hofstadter was making a structural claim, and that is easy to miss. "Some people just don't trust experts" is a cultural observation, whereas "powerful interests cultivate distrust of expertise because it serves them" is a claim about how a society's incentives are arranged. His material supported the second reading, in which an existing democratic instinct, that no person's judgment is inherently superior to another's, is repeatedly turned into a tool by those who benefit from the absence of informed scrutiny. The vulnerability is real, old, and not going away, but it does not on its own explain the present scale of anti-expert sentiment. Something amplified it.

The systems framing helps here, and it connects to a biological argument I have made elsewhere. In [Thyroid Hormone as the Guardian of Form]({{< relref "essays/thyroid-hormone-guardian-of-form" >}}), the central point is that living tissue is held far from equilibrium by continuous, energetically costly maintenance, and that physiological reactive oxygen species are a functional part of normal respiration until their production outpaces the capacity to repair what they damage. Skepticism in a democracy works in a similar way. Doubt about experts is a normal byproduct of open debate and accountability, and it is useful, because it catches real errors and resists technocratic overreach. The problem begins when doubt is produced faster than trust can be rebuilt, and the filter that distinguishes healthy skepticism from manufactured confusion stops working.

## The Tobacco Strategy

In December 1953, executives from six of the major American tobacco manufacturers, all of the large producers except Liggett & Myers, met at the Plaza Hotel in New York with John Hill, founder of the public relations firm Hill & Knowlton {{< cite "brandt2012" >}}. They had one purpose, which was to protect their product from mounting scientific evidence that it caused cancer. The strategy they settled on did not require proving the science wrong, which would have required actual counter-evidence. It required keeping the question looking open.

A 1969 internal memo from Brown & Williamson stated the approach plainly: *"Doubt is our product since it is the best means of competing with the 'body of fact' that exists in the mind of the general public"* {{< cite "brownwilliamson1969" >}}. It was a business model. The industry funded its own research, not to find the truth but to generate enough noise that the public could not tell signal from static, and it hired credentialed scientists to lend legitimacy to the campaign. For decades this delayed regulation, won lawsuits, and kept the public uncertain about a question that medicine had largely settled. In 2006, in *United States v. Philip Morris USA, Inc.*, Judge Gladys Kessler found the major cigarette manufacturers liable under the civil RICO statute for a decades-long conspiracy to defraud the American public about the health effects of smoking and the addictiveness of nicotine {{< cite "usvphilipmorris2006" >}}. The finding was civil, not criminal, but it established the central facts in court. By then the playbook had already been copied.

### The playbook migrates

Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway documented the migration in *Merchants of Doubt* {{< cite "oreskes2010" >}}. A small group of scientists who helped the tobacco industry contest the smoking-cancer link later applied similar methods to acid rain, the ozone hole, and climate change, with funding and institutional support from a network of corporations and conservative think tanks such as the George C. Marshall Institute, the Competitive Enterprise Institute, and the Heritage Foundation. Those were often the same people using the same methods, and the targets changed while the mechanism stayed constant:

| Stage | Method | Purpose |
|----|----|----|
| 1. Fund counter-research | Pay credentialed scientists to produce contrarian findings | Create the appearance of scientific disagreement |
| 2. Exploit media "balance" | Demand equal coverage for the minority position | Make settled science look like an open question |
| 3. Attack the experts | Question motives, funding, and integrity of mainstream science | Undermine the credibility of the source rather than the evidence |
| 4. Delay regulation | Argue that "more research is needed" before action | Protect revenue streams during the delay |

The sequence has components, an order of operations, and a specific output, which is public confusion sufficient to prevent collective action. That is the sense in which I use the word architecture throughout this essay.

## The Parallel Economy

The tobacco strategy was defensive, meant to shield an existing industry from regulation by attacking the scientific basis for it. A more ambitious product followed, an entire parallel market that profits from distrust of the evidence-based system.

The dietary supplement industry is the clearest case. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA), supplements reach the U.S. market largely free of pre-market safety and efficacy review {{< cite "dshea1994" >}}. The FDA does not approve them before sale, although it retains reactive authority to act against products after they are shown to be adulterated or unsafe. The U.S. market is now roughly \$65 to \$69 billion annually. The evidence of harm is not anecdotal. An analysis of FDA recalls found that 66.7% of recalled supplements still contained the banned pharmaceutical ingredient when sampled after the recall {{< cite "cohen2014" >}}. A review of FDA warnings identified 776 adulterated supplements, many containing undeclared drugs {{< cite "tucker2018" >}}, and national surveillance data estimate about 23,000 emergency department visits per year attributable to supplements {{< cite "geller2015" >}}.

An industry of this size depends on a particular set of beliefs: that regulators are compromised, that "natural" is safer than "tested," and that institutional science serves corporate interests rather than public health. The irony is that anti-expert sentiment was first manufactured by corporations to protect their profits from regulation, and the same sentiment now generates profit directly, by creating a market for products that would struggle under rigorous evaluation.

### The wellness economy

The supplement industry is one node in a larger structure. The Global Wellness Institute estimates the global wellness economy at \$6.3 trillion, of which about \$2.0 trillion is in the United States {{< cite "gwi2024" >}}. The global pharmaceutical market, by comparison, is about \$1.6 trillion {{< cite "iqvia2024" >}}, or roughly \$600 to \$670 billion net in the United States. The wellness figure is therefore roughly four times the pharmaceutical figure globally and two and a half to three times larger in the U.S.

That comparison needs a caveat, because the wellness number is not an estimate of the "anti-expert economy." The Global Wellness Institute's \$6.3 trillion spans eleven broad sectors, including personal care and beauty, healthy eating and nutrition, fitness and gyms, wellness tourism, spas, and wellness real estate. Most of that spending is not a rejection of medicine, and much of it is perfectly sensible. What the figure does show is the scale of a commercial ecosystem in which "wellness" is positioned as an alternative or counterweight to conventional medicine, and in which a portion of the revenue, particularly in supplements and alternative medicine, depends on distrust of the evidence-based system. I cannot say how large that portion is, and I would treat any single number claiming to measure it with suspicion.

### The political economy of ignorance

The economics do not stop at consumer products, because anti-expert sentiment also has political utility. Framing government action as "common sense" against "elite expertise" creates a permission structure for deregulation: if experts cannot be trusted, their recommendations on environmental standards, financial oversight, food safety, and public health carry little weight, and regulation becomes an imposition by a discredited class rather than a protection informed by evidence. Industries facing environmental rules benefit when climate scientists are discredited, financial firms benefit when economists calling for oversight are dismissed as elitist, and supplement makers benefit when the FDA is portrayed as captured or incompetent.

There is a structural irony here that I think matters. Much of the push against elites comes from wealthy, educated people within government and industry, and the loudest voices against "the establishment" are frequently themselves establishment figures who benefit financially from the distrust they promote. The form looks like a bottom-up revolt against credentialed authority, while the funding and strategy are often top-down.

## The Amplification Stack

### Foreign exploitation

Anyone wanting to weaken a rival cheaply would amplify its existing divisions instead of creating new ones. Distrust of expertise in the United States is exactly this kind of pre-existing vulnerability, and foreign state actors have exploited it. In September 2024 the U.S. Department of Justice seized 32 internet domains used by the Russian government in the "Doppelganger" operation, which used fabricated articles and spoofed news sites to influence American opinion {{< cite "doj2024" >}}. Russian, Chinese, and Iranian state-linked actors have used troll farms, bots, and increasingly AI-generated content around issues where expert consensus exists, such as public health, climate, and elections. These actors did not invent American anti-intellectualism. They found a system already primed by decades of domestic doubt-manufacturing and added accelerant.

### Algorithmic acceleration

Social media introduced a dynamic in which doubt could become self-sustaining. Content visibility is governed by engagement, and outrage, fear, and novelty generate engagement more reliably than settled consensus does. In a large study of Twitter cascades, false news spread farther, faster, and more broadly than true news, an effect driven by humans rather than bots and associated with the novelty of false claims {{< cite "vosoughi2018" >}}. Moral outrage in particular is amplified by platform design, because social rewards in the form of likes and shares teach users to express more of it {{< cite "brady2021" >}}. A careful explanation of vaccine safety will usually travel less far than a story about vaccine injury, and the algorithm has no agenda to explain that. Its optimization target is enough.

C. Thi Nguyen's distinction is useful for understanding what follows {{< cite "nguyen2020" >}}. An *epistemic bubble* is a passive filtering of information in which relevant voices are simply left out, and exposure to the missing voices can correct it. An *echo chamber* is a structure in which outside voices are actively discredited, so that trust in insiders is amplified and trust in everyone else is eroded, and mere exposure to contrary evidence does little because the source has been pre-labeled as untrustworthy. Algorithmic feeds can create bubbles on their own, but the tobacco-style attack on experts supplies the discrediting that turns a bubble into an echo chamber. That makes the combination of the two layers more durable than either alone.

Once these dynamics are running, they no longer need a sponsor. Doubt generates engagement, engagement generates advertising revenue, and revenue rewards more doubt-generating content. The tobacco executives of 1953 had to spend heavily to maintain their campaign, whereas the current version is partly funded by the platforms' own business models.

### AI-generated scale

Generative AI has lowered the cost of producing fake articles, fabricated quotes, and pseudo-scientific text to nearly nothing, at a quality sufficient to pass casual inspection. The World Economic Forum's *Global Risks Report 2025* ranked misinformation and disinformation as the number one global risk over a two-year horizon, for the second year running {{< cite "wef2025" >}}. The full stack looks like this:

| Layer | Mechanism | Era |
|----|----|----|
| Cultural substrate | Homegrown American anti-intellectualism | 1700s–present |
| Corporate manufacturing | Tobacco strategy, think tank networks | 1950s–present |
| Political weaponization | Populist framing, deregulation campaigns | 1980s–present |
| Foreign exploitation | State-sponsored disinformation operations | 2010s–present |
| Algorithmic acceleration | Engagement-optimized content distribution | 2010s–present |
| AI-generated scale | Cheap, high-volume synthetic content | 2020s–present |

Each layer builds on the ones beneath it. The cultural substrate makes the population receptive, corporate manufacturing creates the initial doubt, political actors convert it into policy, foreign actors widen the divisions, algorithms make the doubt self-sustaining, and AI lowers the cost of the whole operation.

## The Cascade and the Convergence of Incentives

The consequences move through the system in stages that will look familiar to anyone who studies complex systems. First, credibility erodes in the populations most predisposed by the cultural substrate, until distrust reaches critical mass. Second, the parallel economy expands into the gap, and every new participant acquires a financial stake in sustaining the doubt that feeds it. Third, regulatory capture becomes politically feasible, because agencies can be defunded, restructured, or placed under leaders hostile to their mission without much electoral cost. Fourth, institutional knowledge degrades as funding falls and scientists leave, and the resulting dysfunction becomes evidence for the original claim that the institutions do not work. Finally, a society with degraded institutions and widespread distrust is maximally vulnerable to manipulation, which degrades trust further, and the loop closes.

The Roman question *cui bono*, who benefits, is useful here, and the answer is that nearly everyone in the stack benefits for a different reason. Corporations facing regulation benefit from discredited regulators and scientists. Political actors seeking deregulation gain permission from the framing of expertise as elite opinion. The wellness and supplement markets earn revenue in proportion to distrust of conventional medicine. Foreign adversaries find a divided and distrustful population cheaper to manipulate than a cohesive one, and platforms profit from the engagement that controversy produces. These interests are independent and in many cases unaware of each other, and they require no coordinating conspiracy. They converge on doubt because it is the shared route to each actor's separate goal, and the sum of their efforts is larger than any one of them could achieve alone.

The convergence is the important point. If there were a central plot, exposing it would be enough to end it. Because the incentives are distributed, the architecture persists whether or not any single actor is removed, and it can continue to produce doubt without anyone intending the aggregate result.

## Architecture and Information Metabolism

The parallel with biology that I developed in [Thyroid Hormone as the Guardian of Form]({{< relref "essays/thyroid-hormone-guardian-of-form" >}}) is a useful analogy and I do not mean it as more than that. Just as cellular architecture determines what metabolism can accomplish, informational architecture determines a society's capacity to process evidence and act on it. A society with sound epistemic architecture, meaning credible institutions, independent scientific bodies, accountable media, and an educated public, can take in new information, evaluate it, and respond appropriately, the informational counterpart of efficient oxidative metabolism. A society with degraded architecture finds that evidence gets trapped in manufactured controversy and that signal becomes difficult to separate from noise. It falls back on faster, lower-yield, short-horizon processing, in the form of tribalism, anecdote, and gut feeling, much as cells under metabolic stress shift toward glycolysis in the Warburg pattern. Those fallback modes are not worthless, and they are sometimes the right response to an emergency, but they cannot sustain the work of a complex society for long.

The same logic applies to maintenance. Democratic debate, legitimate disagreement, and the exposure of real institutional failures produce doubt as a necessary byproduct, just as respiration produces reactive oxygen species, and in moderation that byproduct is functional. Keeping the architecture intact takes continuous investment in independent research, education, media accountability, and institutional independence. When doubt is produced faster than that maintenance capacity can absorb it, the architecture degrades, and the current situation looks like a system running a deficit. Part of that deficit is natural, because the cultural substrate is real, but a significant part is deliberate, funded, and traceable.

## What I Know, What I'm Guessing, What I Don't Know

### Well-grounded

- American anti-intellectualism has deep cultural roots, documented by Hofstadter and others since at least the 1950s
- The tobacco industry deliberately manufactured public doubt about the health effects of smoking for decades; in 2006 a federal court found the major manufacturers liable under civil RICO for a conspiracy to defraud the public
- The same strategy and, in some cases, the same individuals migrated from tobacco to acid rain, ozone, and climate; this is documented by Oreskes, Conway, and others
- The dietary supplement industry is largely free of pre-market safety and efficacy review under DSHEA (1994), and adulterated products and adverse events are well documented
- Foreign state actors have conducted documented disinformation campaigns targeting American public discourse, and the DOJ has acted against specific operations
- Engagement-driven platforms spread false and outrage-laden content faster than corrections, as shown in peer-reviewed studies

### Speculative but plausible

- Current anti-expert sentiment is a qualitative escalation and not just a cyclical recurrence, owing to the amplification stack
- The portion of the wellness economy that sells alternatives to evidence-based medicine is structurally dependent on manufactured doubt and not merely a beneficiary of it
- The convergence of corporate, political, foreign, and algorithmic interests is producing effects greater than any single actor could achieve alone
- The cascade from doubt to institutional degradation to greater vulnerability is a positive feedback loop that will keep accelerating without intervention

### Unknown

- Whether the current wave of anti-expert sentiment is reversible, or whether the epistemic architecture has been damaged past recovery
- The relative contribution of each layer of the stack, and which one does the most damage
- Whether there is a threshold of institutional trust below which the system cannot self-correct
- What interventions, if any, can rebuild epistemic architecture faster than it is degrading
- How much of the parallel economy would shrink if institutional trust were restored, and how much has become self-sustaining independent of the doubt that created it

## Why Think About This At All

If the evidence is incomplete in places and the dynamics are complex, it is fair to ask why build an elaborate structural analysis. My answer is that the framing matters. The dominant narratives treat anti-intellectualism either as a cultural failing of the uneducated, which is elitist and wrong, or as a legitimate populist revolt against a compromised establishment, which is understandable but incomplete. Both obscure the fact that identifiable actors have spent decades and enormous sums cultivating the doubt that now saturates public discourse, and treating anti-expert sentiment as a spontaneous cultural phenomenon misses the architecture.

The analysis does not offer a simple solution. Epistemic architecture, like biological architecture, degrades faster than it is rebuilt, the layers of the stack would each need separate attention, and the cultural substrate cannot and should not be eliminated, because democratic skepticism is a feature that has been exploited and not a defect to be corrected. Understanding the structure is still a prerequisite for responding to it, since manufactured doubt cannot be addressed by treating it as organic confusion.

If you find yourself wanting to believe this framework because it confirms your suspicion that "they" are manipulating "us," be suspicious of yourself. The architecture of doubt is real, but so is the architecture of legitimate institutional failure. Experts have been wrong, institutions have been captured, and the pharmaceutical industry has lied. The line between manufactured doubt and earned distrust is not always clean, and treating all skepticism as manufactured is itself an instance of the failure this essay describes.

If you find yourself dismissing this framework because it sounds like a conspiracy theory, be equally suspicious. The tobacco strategy is a matter of court record, the think tank funding networks are publicly documented, and the Justice Department has seized the domains of foreign disinformation operations. The question is not whether the architecture exists but how much of the present anti-expert sentiment it explains.

A society with genuine cultural vulnerabilities has had them deliberately amplified by actors who profit from the resulting confusion, and both the vulnerability and the amplification are real. The American economy runs on two layers not because Americans are uniquely gullible but because distrust of expertise is unusually profitable, and in a market economy profitability builds its own architecture.
