## Presence Is Not Adequacy

Public discussion of dietary linoleic acid (18:2n-6) has settled into a binary. On one side, it is treated as a cardiovascular remedy, a cholesterol-lowering fat whose rising intake over the twentieth century should be welcomed wherever it displaces saturated fat. On the other, it is described as an industrial artifact, an unstable molecule held responsible for metabolic slowing, chronic inflammation, and most of the diseases of modern life. Both stories are easy to remember, and both come apart when they are set beside the biological record.

The difficulty is not a shortage of evidence. Nearly a century of biochemistry, clinical nutrition, lipidomics, genetics, and comparative physiology has followed linoleic acid through almost every organ system in mammals. The difficulty is that nutritional adequacy is not a static property of a food list. It is a relationship among supply, mobilization, cellular machinery, physiological demand, and maintenance. A fatty acid sitting inside an adipocyte does not nourish a tissue if insulin-suppressed lipolysis keeps it in storage. An abundant dietary precursor cannot build a membrane or a signaling mediator if the desaturase, elongase, or remodeling enzymes downstream are impaired or saturated. And a molecule whose double bonds make it indispensable for mitochondrial cristae, epidermal water retention, and regulated cell signaling carries, through the same chemistry, a vulnerability to peroxidation when antioxidant defenses and cellular turnover fall behind.

Linoleic acid sits at the intersection of these constraints. It is a structural substrate, an energy source, a receptor ligand, and the entry point to a signaling network, and it cannot be reduced to any one of those roles. This essay is the conceptual foundation and narrative table of contents for a series on that biology. It draws on the companion [Research Notes](/essential-fatty-acids-research-notes/), a working ledger of more than 450 peer-reviewed papers spanning 1927 to 2025, to lay out what is firmly established, what remains contested, and why I think the useful questions about essential fatty acids are structural and adaptive rather than moral.

## The Experimental Origin of Essentiality (1927–1971)

Essential fatty acids were discovered through analytical rigor rather than dietary ideology. Through the 1910s and early 1920s, leading nutritionists concluded that warm-blooded animals did not require dietary fat so long as calories and fat-soluble vitamins were supplied, partly because crude cornstarch and unextracted casein carried enough residual lipid to mask a deficiency. While purifying diets for vitamin E research, Herbert Evans and George Burr replaced those crude ingredients with recrystallized sucrose and solvent-extracted casein {{< cite "evans1927" >}}. Rats on these rations stopped growing, developed scaly skin and irregular ovulation, and failed to respond to any known vitamin. Natural fats cured the syndrome, which narrowed the missing factor to the saponifiable lipid fraction {{< cite "evans1928" >}}.

George and Mildred Burr then defined the full syndrome on rigorously fat-free diets: growth arrest, scaly dermatitis, inflamed hind paws, tail necrosis, renal hemorrhage, reproductive failure, extraordinary water consumption, and early death {{< cite "burr1929" >}}. Saturated fats such as butter, coconut oil, and hydrogenated oils did not cure the animals, and neither did monounsaturated oleic acid, whereas small doses of linoleic acid restored growth, skin integrity, water balance, and reproduction {{< cite "burr1930" >}}. Mammals, it turned out, cannot synthesize the *cis*-9,*cis*-12 double-bond arrangement of linoleic acid *de novo*.

Translating that finding to humans took three decades. Arild Hansen and colleagues fed infants skim-milk formulas supplying less than 0.1% of energy as linoleic acid and documented dry, thickened, desquamating skin and poor caloric efficiency per unit of weight gain {{< cite "hansen1958" >}}. In a controlled evaluation of 428 infants across five milk formulations, roughly 1% of energy as linoleic acid prevented overt clinical deficiency, while 2–4% normalized both growth and serum fatty-acid profiles {{< cite "hansen1963" >}}. In the meantime, James Mead and William Slaton had identified the biochemical hallmark of deficiency: when linoleic acid is scarce, desaturases act on endogenous oleic acid to produce 5,8,11-eicosatrienoic acid, or Mead acid {{< cite "mead1956" >}}. Ralph Holman turned that observation into a quantitative index, the ratio of Mead acid (triene) to arachidonic acid (tetraene), and mapped dose-response curves across rodents, infants, and adults that placed the conserved mammalian floor near 1–2% of total energy {{< cite "holman1960" "holman1964" "holman1971" >}}.

Hugh Sinclair, working in parallel, pushed the concept from frank deficiency toward chronic disease. In a well-known *Lancet* letter he proposed that Western atherosclerosis reflected a "relative deficiency" of essential fatty acids, worsened by saturated fats and industrial hydrogenation {{< cite "sinclair1956" >}}. The letter was a generative hypothesis and not a clinical proof, but it sharpened attention on fat quality and cholesteryl-ester transport.

The clearest lesson about human adequacy came from adult hospital medicine. In 1971, Collins and colleagues reported the first well-documented adult case of linoleic acid deficiency, in a man maintained on fat-free intravenous nutrition for 100 days after bowel resection; scaly dermatitis and a rising Mead acid fraction resolved quickly once an intravenous soybean-oil emulsion was supplied {{< cite "collins1971" >}}. Controlled fat-free feeding trials soon showed that healthy men and surgical patients developed biochemical deficiency within one to four weeks on continuous glucose and amino-acid infusions, despite carrying kilograms of linoleic acid in their own adipose tissue {{< cite "wene1975" "goodgame1978" >}}. A complementary experiment explained why. When surgical patients received hypocaloric, glucose-free amino-acid infusions ("protein-sparing therapy"), insulin stayed low enough to permit adipose lipolysis, stored linoleic acid was mobilized, and biochemical deficiency did not appear {{< cite "stegink1977" >}}.

Possession is not availability.

## A Shifting Supply and the Problem of Measurement

If early clinical physiology established the floor of the linoleic acid requirement, twentieth-century agriculture moved the ceiling of exposure. Using USDA commodity disappearance records from 1909 to 1999, Blasbalg and colleagues estimated that apparent U.S. soybean oil consumption rose more than a thousandfold, lifting apparent linoleic acid availability from roughly 2.8% to 7.2% of total energy while α-linolenic acid rose far more modestly {{< cite "blasbalg2011" >}}. Because humans cannot make linoleic acid, that shift in supply wrote itself into human tissue. Compiling U.S. adult adipose biopsy studies from 1959 to 2008, Guyenet and Carlson documented a 136% secular increase in subcutaneous adipose linoleic acid, from an average of 9.1% to 21.5% of total fatty acids, tracking soybean oil disappearance closely {{< cite "guyenet2015" >}}.

Those numbers also expose a timescale problem. Radiocarbon dating of human adipocytes indicates that adipose triglycerides turn over roughly six times across an adipocyte's average ten-year lifespan {{< cite "arner2011" >}}. In a five-year institutional trial that switched older men to a high-linoleate vegetable-oil diet, Dayton and colleagues measured an adipose linoleic acid equilibration half-time of roughly 680 days, nearly two years {{< cite "dayton1966" >}}. Acute deficiency can appear and reverse within weeks, yet remodeling of whole-body depots unfolds over years. Short feeding trials and long-term tissue composition therefore measure different physiological regimes, and neither can stand in for the other.

Two historical confounders complicate the picture further. Much of the mid-century expansion in seed oils occurred alongside industrial partial hydrogenation, and partially hydrogenated oils introduced *trans* fatty acids that suppressed hepatic Δ6- and Δ5-desaturase activity in animal models {{< cite "hill1982" >}} and raised LDL-cholesterol while lowering HDL-cholesterol in humans {{< cite "mensink1990" >}}. Historical seed-oil trends and mid-century margarine trials therefore cannot be read as clean tests of native, unhydrogenated linoleic acid.

The food supply also did not freeze in 2008. After the 2006 U.S. *trans*-fat labeling mandate and the 2015–2018 regulatory phase-out of partially hydrogenated oils, manufacturers and commercial fryers moved quickly toward high-oleic soybean, sunflower, and canola oils engineered for frying stability without hydrogenation {{< cite "su2004" "gubaev2023" >}}. The shift has begun to alter livestock feed and pork fat composition as well {{< cite "gaffield2022" >}}. Nutrient databases, food-frequency questionnaires, and public commentary often trail industrial reformulation by years, so measuring what populations actually eat, and what sits in their tissues today, is harder than extending a twentieth-century trend line indefinitely upward.

## Mitochondrial Architecture, Energy, and Cell Fate

A common intuition treats polyunsaturated fats as inherently antimetabolic because their double bonds oxidize easily in a test tube. Mitochondrial biochemistry contradicts that assumption at the structural level.

The inner mitochondrial membrane is enriched in cardiolipin, a four-chained dimeric phospholipid whose conical geometry shapes the negative curvature of cristae and stabilizes respiratory chain supercomplexes {{< cite "schlame2017" >}}. In mature mammalian heart and skeletal muscle, post-synthetic remodeling fills cardiolipin overwhelmingly with linoleic acid, so that tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin (L4-CL) accounts for roughly 70–80% of the cardiac pool {{< cite "schlame2002" "schlame2017" >}}. That enrichment is governed by more than enzyme transcription. It also depends on the physical packing of curved membranes and on the fatty-acid composition of the surrounding cellular phospholipid pool, which means dietary linoleic acid availability can shape cardiolipin assembly directly {{< cite "schlame2012" "oemer2020" >}}.

Barth syndrome shows the cost of losing this architecture. Mutations in *TAFAZZIN*, the transacylase that remodels nascent cardiolipin into L4-CL, deplete cardiac tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin and cause cardiomyopathy, skeletal myopathy, and impaired oxidative metabolism {{< cite "schlame2002" >}}. In tafazzin-knockdown mouse hearts, the bioenergetic lesion extends beyond supercomplex geometry to impaired CoA-dependent oxidation of both pyruvate and palmitoylcarnitine before overt heart failure {{< cite "le2020" >}}.

Dietary linoleic acid is also not simply shelved in membranes and fat droplets. Human stable-isotope tracer studies show that it is oxidized more rapidly than long-chain saturated fats such as palmitate and stearate, though less rapidly than α-linolenic or oleic acid {{< cite "delany2000" >}}. Because its *cis* double bonds require auxiliary enzymes, including mitochondrial 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase (DECR1), complete breakdown is an active, regulated pathway, and *Decr1*-null mice accumulate partially degraded linoleate acylcarnitines and develop fasting hypoglycemia and cold intolerance {{< cite "miinalainen2009" >}}. A substantial share of the acetyl-CoA released by linoleic acid β-oxidation is also recycled into *de novo* synthesis of saturated fatty acids and cholesterol, especially during growth {{< cite "cunnane2003" >}}.

The lipid architecture that sustains oxidative phosphorylation also governs mitochondrial quality control and cell death. Under sub-lethal stress, intact cardiolipin flips to the outer mitochondrial membrane through phospholipid scramblase-3 and binds LC3 directly, acting as an "eat-me" signal that triggers selective mitophagy {{< cite "chu2013" >}}. Under deeper oxidative stress, cytochrome *c* bound to cardiolipin becomes a cardiolipin-specific peroxidase that oxidizes linoleoyl chains, releasing cytochrome *c* and initiating intrinsic apoptosis {{< cite "kagan2005" >}}. Beyond the mitochondrion, iron-dependent peroxidation of polyunsaturated membrane phospholipids drives ferroptosis. This regulated cell-death pathway is executed principally by arachidonic and adrenic acid phosphatidylethanolamines loaded through ACSL4 and LPCAT3 rather than by parent linoleic acid itself, and it is held in check by the selenoenzyme GPX4 working with vitamin E {{< cite "doll2017" "kagan2017" "carlson2016" >}}. Linoleic acid thus takes part in respiration, turnover, and programmed demise through separate molecular switches, and which one is thrown depends on the capacity for maintenance.

## Barriers, Organ Systems, and the Microbiome

The same interplay between structural necessity and signaling recurs across the major organ systems.

Linoleic acid's direct structural role is clearest in the epidermis. The heavy water consumption Burr and Burr noticed in fat-deficient rats reflected catastrophic transepidermal water loss through disrupted stratum corneum lipid lamellae {{< cite "elias1978" >}}. Restoring the barrier does not require converting linoleic acid into prostaglandins. It requires esterifying linoleate onto ultra-long-chain ω-hydroxyceramides to build acylceramides {{< cite "hansen1985" >}}. Genetic and biochemical work in mice and humans has since mapped the assembly line: ELOVL4 elongates the ultra-long-chain scaffold {{< cite "vasireddy2007" >}}, PNPLA1 transfers linoleic acid onto the ceramide {{< cite "ohno2017" >}}, 12R-LOX and eLOX3 stereospecifically oxidize the esterified linoleate {{< cite "zheng2011" >}}, and SDR9C7 converts that intermediate into a reactive epoxy-enone that covalently anchors the lipid envelope to corneocyte proteins {{< cite "takeichi2020" >}}. Loss-of-function mutations at any of these steps cause severe congenital ichthyosis in humans, which is direct genetic evidence that esterified linoleate is part of the human skin barrier.

In the kidney, linoleic acid acts mainly downstream, through arachidonate-derived prostaglandins. Essential fatty acid deficiency depletes renal medullary prostaglandin E₂, removing a physiological brake on vasopressin and shifting the kidney toward impaired natriuresis and maximal urine concentration {{< cite "hansen1981" >}}. The kidney also presents a paradox. In experimental glomerulonephritis in rats, essential fatty acid deficiency selectively depleted resident renal macrophages, blocked glomerular macrophage infiltration, and cut proteinuria by roughly 85% {{< cite "schreiner1989" >}}. The precursor pool needed for normal renal water handling and tubular integrity also supplies the chemoattractants that amplify acute inflammatory kidney injury.

In the gastrointestinal tract, the resident microbiome adds a third layer. Gut commensals such as *Lactobacillus plantarum* hydrate and saturate dietary linoleic acid, probably to neutralize the antibacterial toxicity of free polyunsaturated fatty acids, and in doing so generate 10-hydroxy-*cis*-12-octadecenoic acid (HYA) and conjugated linoleic acid isomers {{< cite "kishino2013" >}}. HYA is not inert waste. It signals through intestinal GPR40 and GPR120 to preserve epithelial tight junctions during colitis, stimulate GLP-1 secretion, and confer resistance to high-fat-diet obesity in mice {{< cite "miyamoto2015" "miyamoto2019" >}}. Yet in susceptible mice fed high-LA soybean oil, unconverted linoleic acid can instead expand adherent-invasive *Escherichia coli*, impair barrier function, and worsen colitis {{< cite "deol2023" >}}. The host never meets dietary linoleic acid alone; it meets whatever its microbial ecology has made of it.

In plasma lipoprotein transport, linoleic acid is the preferred acyl donor of lecithin:cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), which esterifies free cholesterol on HDL into hydrophobic cholesteryl linoleate, and this species makes up roughly half of circulating human cholesteryl esters {{< cite "glomset1968" "sgoutas1972" >}}. In hepatic clearance, dietary saturated fatty acids suppress hepatic LDL-receptor expression and plasma LDL clearance in animal models, whereas polyunsaturated fats preserve receptor-mediated uptake {{< cite "spady1985" >}}. That mechanism helps explain why replacing saturated fat with linoleic acid reliably lowers serum LDL-cholesterol in controlled human feeding trials {{< cite "mensink2003" >}}. It is a statement about LDL, and as the final section shows, what follows from it for mortality is a separate and unsettled matter.

## Sensors, Signals, and Human Evolution

For decades, linoleic acid was treated either as a passive building block or as a waiting room on the way to arachidonic acid. Receptor biology and lipidomics have overturned both views. Unoxidized linoleic acid is itself a signaling molecule in several tissues. On the tongue, apical CD36 and the G-protein-coupled receptors GPR40 (FFAR1) and GPR120 (FFAR4) detect free linoleic acid liberated by lingual lipase, triggering cephalic digestive reflexes and spontaneous fat preference {{< cite "laugerette2005" "cartoni2010" >}}. In pancreatic β-cells and intestinal enteroendocrine cells, GPR40 and GPR120 couple acute fatty-acid flux to glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and GLP-1 release, and cryo-EM structures show that double-bond geometry biases receptor signaling toward distinct downstream pathways {{< cite "itoh2003" "hirasawa2005" "mao2023" >}}. Inside the cell, fatty acid-binding proteins such as FABP5 undergo a polyunsaturate-specific conformational shift that directs linoleic acid to the nucleus {{< cite "armstrong2014" >}}. There, polyunsaturated fatty acids antagonize Liver X Receptors to suppress lipogenic *SREBP-1c* transcription {{< cite "ou2001" >}}, and linoleic acid serves as a reversible, feeding-state-dependent endogenous ligand of hepatic HNF-4α {{< cite "yuan2009" >}}.

Unsaturated fatty acids also solve a biophysical problem that saturated fats create in the endoplasmic reticulum. Excess palmitate stiffens the ER bilayer and directly activates the unfolded-protein-response sensors IRE1α and PERK, triggering lipoapoptosis {{< cite "volmer2013" >}}. Co-supplying unsaturated fatty acids rescues cells by channeling saturated acyl chains into neutral triglyceride droplets {{< cite "listenberger2003" >}}, and the remodeling enzyme LPCAT3 inserts linoleate and arachidonate into ER phosphatidylcholine to maintain bilayer fluidity and restrain inflammatory stress {{< cite "rong2013" >}}.

Once oxygenated, linoleic acid branches into a wider signaling web, the oxidized linoleic acid metabolites, or OXLAMs. Cold exposure and acute exercise prompt brown adipose tissue to secrete the cytochrome P450 and soluble epoxide hydrolase product 12,13-DiHOME, an adaptive lipokine that promotes fatty-acid uptake into brown fat and skeletal muscle in mice and humans {{< cite "lynes2017" "stanford2018" >}}. The same enzymatic branch generates 9,10-DiHOME ("leukotoxin-diol"), which causes acute pulmonary epithelial injury in severe burn and ARDS models {{< cite "moghaddam1997" >}}. Likewise, 9- and 13-HODE act as endogenous agonists of the pain receptor TRPV1 in rodents {{< cite "patwardhan2009" >}}, and in a randomized trial of adults with frequent migraine, combining higher marine omega-3 intake with a sharp reduction in dietary linoleic acid lowered headache frequency more than omega-3 enrichment alone {{< cite "ramsden2021" >}}.

Sorting these outcomes requires analytical care. Stereospecific lipoxygenases and cyclooxygenases imprint a single chirality and *cis,trans* geometry onto linoleate, whereas uncontrolled free-radical autoxidation yields racemic mixtures, thermodynamic *trans,trans* isomers, and secondary electrophiles such as 4-hydroxynonenal {{< cite "brash1999" "niki2009" "yin2011" >}}. Grouping regulated enzymatic mediators with the debris of free-radical peroxidation hides the very difference biology cares about most.

Downstream of linoleic acid, human populations do not share a uniform rate of conversion to arachidonic acid. The *FADS1*/*FADS2* desaturase cluster has been under repeated and opposing episodes of natural selection. A high-conversion derived haplotype swept to high frequency in Africa and later across agrarian Eurasia and South Asia, where plant-rich diets supplied precursors rather than preformed animal long-chain PUFAs {{< cite "ameur2012" "mathieson2015" "kothapalli2016" >}}. Greenlandic Inuit, by contrast, underwent strong selection for lower-conversion alleles suited to marine diets already rich in preformed EPA, DHA, and arachidonate {{< cite "fumagalli2015" >}}.

The omega-6 cascade does not act in a single inflammatory direction either. Arachidonic acid gives rise to the classical initiators of acute inflammation, prostaglandins and leukotrienes, but prostaglandin E₂ also reprograms neutrophils toward arachidonate-derived lipoxins and so helps initiate the resolution of inflammation {{< cite "serhan1984" "levy2001" >}}. In energy balance, arachidonate-derived endocannabinoids (anandamide and 2-AG) stimulate appetite through CB1, while linoleate-derived *N*-linoleoylethanolamine (LEA) activates intestinal GPR119 incretin and satiety pathways {{< cite "syed2012" >}}. In immunity and oncology the same duality appears at the level of single cell lineages. In murine fatty liver disease, accumulated linoleic acid drives mitochondrial ROS and selective apoptosis of intrahepatic CD4⁺ T cells, weakening antitumor surveillance {{< cite "ma2016" >}}, whereas in cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells linoleic acid strengthens ER–mitochondria contacts, boosts bioenergetics, and prevents exhaustion {{< cite "navalauson2023" >}}. Most recently, linoleic acid binding to FABP5 was shown to activate mTORC1 and drive proliferation specifically in FABP5-high triple-negative breast cancer models, leaving FABP5-low breast cancer cells unaffected {{< cite "koundouros2025" >}}. In each of these cases the cellular context, and not the molecule in isolation, determined the outcome.

## Whole-Organism Adaptation: Brain, Hormones, Torpor, and Life Cycle

Stepping back from single cells to the whole organism shows how tightly physiology buffers and repurposes linoleic acid across tissues, hormones, and life stages.

The mammalian brain presents an instructive paradox. Although linoleic acid is a major component of dietary polyunsaturated fat, it remains below roughly 2% of total brain fatty acids even on high-LA diets. Radiolabeled tracer studies in rats showed why: unesterified linoleic acid crosses the blood-brain barrier readily, but roughly 86% of what enters is rapidly channeled into β-oxidation within minutes rather than elongated or esterified into structural phospholipids {{< cite "demar2006" "taha2020" >}}. DHA, by contrast, is imported across brain endothelium as lysophosphatidylcholine through the dedicated symporter Mfsd2a {{< cite "nguyen2014" >}}. The central nervous system nonetheless depends on the downstream omega-6 cascade for core homeostasis: hypothalamic prostaglandin E₂ acting on EP3 receptors is the essential final mediator of fever {{< cite "ushikubi1998" >}}, and meningeal prostaglandin D₂ acting on DP1 receptors is a major physiological driver of non-REM sleep {{< cite "mizoguchi2001" >}}.

In systemic metabolism, human and rodent evidence diverge sharply. Pooled individual-level data from 20 prospective human cohorts, nearly 40,000 adults, associate higher circulating or tissue linoleic acid biomarkers with a 35% lower risk of incident type 2 diabetes {{< cite "wu2017" >}}, and meta-analyses of controlled feeding trials show improved glycemic control when polyunsaturated fat replaces saturated fat or carbohydrate {{< cite "imamura2016" >}}. In mice, raising dietary linoleic acid from 1% to 8% of energy elevates tissue arachidonate and endocannabinoids and promotes adiposity {{< cite "alvheim2012" >}}, and high-fat soybean-oil diets induce marked fatty liver and diabetes relative to coconut oil {{< cite "deol2015" >}}. A critical follow-up complicates even that result: genetically modified low-LA, high-oleic soybean oil still caused hypothalamic dysregulation and glucose intolerance, a warning against attributing whole-oil rodent phenotypes to linoleic acid alone {{< cite "deol2020" >}}.

Endocrine status governs how tissues handle linoleic acid as much as it is shaped by it. As explored in [Thyroid Hormone as the Guardian of Form]({{< relref "essays/thyroid-hormone-guardian-of-form" >}}), thyroid signaling coordinates metabolic throughput with membrane architecture. Experimental hypothyroidism in rats sharply suppresses hepatic Δ6-desaturase activity, so that linoleic acid accumulates and arachidonate falls in membrane phospholipids. These changes are fully reversed by T3, which broadly remodels mitochondrial and microsomal acyl chains to match energetic demand {{< cite "faas1982" "hoch1988" >}}.

Comparative physiology gives perhaps the clearest picture of linoleic acid's adaptive utility and of its upper bound. Mammalian hibernators such as chipmunks and ground squirrels require dietary linoleic acid to enter deep, prolonged torpor {{< cite "geiser1987" >}}, because linoleate-enriched sarcoplasmic reticulum membranes sustain cardiac SERCA2a calcium pumping at near-freezing body temperatures and prevent fatal cold-induced arrhythmias {{< cite "ruf2008" "giroud2013" >}}. More is not monotonically better, however. Hibernation performance peaks at an intermediate depot linoleic acid concentration, above which lipid peroxidation during periodic rewarming degrades torpor {{< cite "frank1995" >}}. Migratory songbirds likewise use linoleic acid to reshape depot fuel and raise aerobic peak metabolic rate {{< cite "pierce2005" >}}. These adaptations show a deep evolutionary utility, though they give no license for a leap from hibernating rodents to human weight gain.

Across reproduction and early life, omega-6 lipids are woven into every transition. Ovulation, implantation, and parturition depend on COX-2 and prostaglandin F₂α signaling {{< cite "lim1997" "sugimoto1997" >}}, and rodent spermatogenesis requires *Elovl2*-elongated very-long-chain n-6 PUFAs that DHA alone cannot replace {{< cite "zadravec2011" >}}. During human pregnancy the placenta does not passively mirror maternal blood lipids; stable-isotope tracing confirms active biomagnification favoring DHA and arachidonic acid over parent linoleic acid {{< cite "gilsanchez2010" >}}. After birth, human breast milk linoleic acid roughly doubled to tripled over the late twentieth century alongside industrial oil use {{< cite "ailhaud2006" >}}. Extreme multigenerational high-LA rodent diets promote offspring adiposity through prostacyclin signaling, yet randomized trials that lowered the maternal n-6:n-3 ratio in human pregnancy have not reproduced an anti-adiposity effect in infants {{< cite "hauner2012" >}}.

## Where the Evidence Conflicts, and Where the Series Begins

An honest synthesis of linoleic acid has to end by naming the unresolved conflicts rather than smoothing them over.

The sharpest conflict concerns human cardiovascular mortality. Re-analyses of previously unpublished data from two mid-century randomized trials, the Sydney Diet Heart Study and the Minnesota Coronary Experiment, showed that replacing saturated fat with high doses of linoleic-acid-rich safflower or corn oil and margarine lowered serum cholesterol by about 13–14% yet failed to reduce mortality, and in the Sydney trial increased all-cause and cardiovascular death {{< cite "ramsden2013" "ramsden2016" >}}. Both trials have real limitations, including probable *trans*-fat contamination in 1960s margarines and short institutional follow-up in Minnesota. Other evidence pulls the opposite way. A harmonized pooling of 30 prospective cohorts across 13 countries (68,659 participants) found that higher circulating and adipose linoleic acid biomarkers were associated with lower cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular mortality, and ischemic stroke {{< cite "marklund2019" >}}, and a Cochrane review of 19 randomized trials found little or no effect of increased omega-6 intake on all-cause mortality, alongside a possible modest reduction in myocardial infarction {{< cite "hooper2018" >}}.

A parallel tension separates inflammatory biomarkers from bioactive oxylipins. Systematic reviews of randomized human feeding trials find little evidence that raising dietary linoleic acid elevates standard systemic inflammatory markers such as CRP, TNF-α, or IL-6 {{< cite "johnson2012" "su2017" >}}, and across typical Western intakes, varying dietary linoleic acid rarely alters plasma or erythrocyte arachidonic acid {{< cite "rett2011" >}}. Yet lowering dietary linoleic acid in humans measurably lowers circulating oxidized linoleic acid metabolites {{< cite "ramsden2012" >}}, which suggests that conventional inflammatory panels and tissue arachidonate averages may miss localized oxylipin dynamics and the competition from omega-3 fats.

Every tier of evidence has a boundary. Cell-culture exposures to unesterified free fatty acids cannot predict whole-body dietary substitution. Inbred C57BL/6 mice on 60%-fat purified chows do not behave like free-living humans on mixed diets. Observational biomarker cohorts cannot eliminate metabolic reverse causation, and historical margarine trials cannot cleanly separate native linoleic acid from industrial hydrogenation. What survives across all of these limits is the organizing thesis of the series: biological outcomes of linoleic acid emerge from the matching of supply, mobilization, enzymatic capacity, omega-3 background, and oxidative maintenance, so that it behaves as neither panacea nor poison.

To see how we arrived at these questions, and how each piece of the architecture works, we have to go back to the beginning, when a purified diet without fat first showed that a lipid could be essential at all. The series begins with George and Mildred Burr.
