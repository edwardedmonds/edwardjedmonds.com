## The Thermodynamic Imperative

Conventional endocrinology describes thyroid function through the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis: a regulatory loop in which the hypothalamus, pituitary, and thyroid gland hold circulating hormone concentrations steady through feedback. Serum TSH, T4, and T3 are clinically useful readouts of that loop. They are not, however, the same thing as hormone action inside a tissue, and a measurement of metabolic rate does not by itself explain what the energy being produced is *for*.

Thyroid hormone is usually described as a regulator of metabolic rate. T3 raises energy expenditure, influences mitochondrial activity, and changes the expression of metabolic proteins, and these effects are well established. What interests me is what that additional metabolic activity accomplishes. A living cell must continually maintain chemical gradients, replace damaged components, regulate the composition of its membranes, and preserve the conditions its specialized function requires. All of this consumes energy, and all of it also depends on the integrity of the machinery that produces the energy.

The relationship is reciprocal.

Energy maintains architecture, and architecture makes energy production possible. Thyroid hormone participates in coordinating that loop, so the useful question is not only how much energy T3 causes a cell to expend, but whether the resulting activity can sustain the organization the cell depends on. This essay develops that question and tests it against the evidence, including the places where my own earlier framing went further than the evidence allows.

## The Concept of Form

By form I mean the structural and functional organization that distinguishes living tissue from a collection of its constituent molecules. A dead cell and a living cell may begin with nearly the same molecular inventory. What differs is the arrangement of those molecules and the continuous activity required to preserve it. Living organisms are thermodynamically open systems held far from equilibrium: the plasma membrane maintains an electrochemical separation between the cell and its surroundings, mitochondria maintain a proton gradient across their inner membranes, proteins are synthesized, folded, transported, and degraded, membrane phospholipids are remodeled, and calcium is partitioned among compartments.

None of these arrangements is permanent. A cell does not escape the second law of thermodynamics; it sustains local organization by taking in energy and exporting entropy to its environment. Maintenance of form is therefore a process rather than a state, and it has a continuous energetic cost.

Thyroid hormone acts on several of the processes involved. T3 regulates mitochondrial metabolism and the expression or activity of energy-consuming transport systems, including Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and, in relevant tissues, calcium-handling machinery such as SERCA. These pumps establish the ionic conditions cells need and contribute to the gradients that support membrane potentials. Organization reaches further than ion transport, though. A functioning tissue also needs appropriate membrane composition, extracellular matrix, protein turnover, intercellular communication, and developmental regulation, and thyroid hormone touches these through metabolic and transcriptional control. It is not the sole determinant of form, and more thyroid activity does not automatically yield better organization, because keeping a structure intact and speeding up its activity are different jobs.

## Thyroid Hormone and the Capacity for Metabolic Work

The familiar reading is that thyroid hormone sets the rate at which an organism runs. Recent reviews of thyroid hormone and mitochondrial regulation describe a richer picture {{< cite "sagliocchi2024" >}}: T3 influences mitochondrial biogenesis, dynamics, respiratory function, and substrate handling, and through them whole-body energy expenditure and thermogenesis. The effects vary among tissues, depending on receptor expression, local hormone availability, and the existing metabolic state. I find it more useful to say that thyroid hormone helps regulate both the capacity for metabolic work and the demand placed on it.

Increased capacity can support growth, tissue remodeling, and thermoregulation, but each of these has material requirements. A cell cannot maintain its membranes without the lipids needed to build them, replace damaged proteins without amino acids and a working biosynthetic system, or sustain oxidative phosphorylation without properly organized respiratory machinery. The same degree of thyroid stimulation will therefore be absorbed differently depending on nutritional status, oxygen availability, mitochondrial integrity, and the load the tissue already carries. A signal that raises metabolic activity guarantees nothing about whether the materials and machinery to sustain that activity are present.

## Membrane Architecture Is Part of Metabolism

Fatty acids make the point concrete. It is tempting to sort them by chemical stability, treating saturated fats as metabolically favorable and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) as inherently disruptive, because the double bonds that make PUFAs reactive in a test tube seem like an obvious liability. That view overlooks the structural requirements of oxidative metabolism itself. PUFAs are indispensable components of biological membranes; their double bonds shape molecular geometry, lipid packing, and the interactions between lipids and membrane proteins. Their effects depend on the particular fatty acid, its position, and the phospholipid carrying it, so neither saturation nor unsaturation alone determines whether a membrane will work.

Cardiolipin is the instructive example. It is a phospholipid concentrated in the inner mitochondrial membrane, and unlike conventional phospholipids with two fatty acid chains, it carries four. In the mature mammalian heart it is strongly enriched in linoleic acid, with tetralinoleoyl-cardiolipin as a predominant species, and ongoing remodeling of its acyl chains contributes to the organization of the respiratory machinery. Respiratory complexes operate inside this lipid environment: cardiolipin interacts with them directly, supports the architecture of mitochondrial cristae, and contributes to the stability of respiratory supercomplexes.

Barth syndrome shows what happens when remodeling fails. Mutations in tafazzin, an enzyme required for cardiolipin remodeling, alter inner-membrane lipid composition and produce cardiomyopathy, skeletal muscle weakness, and impaired energy metabolism. In a tafazzin-knockdown (*TazKD*) mouse model, cardiac mitochondria showed impaired CoA-dependent oxidative metabolism of both pyruvate and palmitoylcarnitine, and this appeared before overt heart failure {{< cite "le2020" >}}. The defect therefore extends beyond supercomplex instability to intermediary metabolism itself, which is a reminder that a lipid-architecture problem can surface in several parts of the oxidative machinery at once.

Linoleic acid is therefore not inherently antimetabolic. Incorporated into a specialized phospholipid, it is part of the architecture that lets respiration proceed. An organism needs both energy substrates and structural substrates, and fatty acids can serve either role depending on how they are handled.

## The Oxidation Problem

The structural importance of PUFAs sits uneasily beside their chemistry. The double bonds that give them their biological functions also make many polyunsaturated lipids more susceptible to oxidation, and that susceptibility is real. During lipid peroxidation, reactive intermediates propagate damage through membranes, and products such as 4-hydroxynonenal (HNE) can modify proteins and interfere with mitochondrial enzymes. Experimental work demonstrated that HNE forms covalent adducts with subunits of cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) and inhibits the enzyme, and also that conjugation of HNE with reduced glutathione (GSH) protected Complex IV from that inhibition {{< cite "chen1998" >}}. The second finding matters as much as the first: it shows the damage and its cellular defense in the same experiment.

It would be a mistake to conclude from this that the parent fatty acid is itself a respiratory inhibitor. A peroxidation product acting on an enzyme tells us about oxidative degradation, not about the function of an intact lipid. Nor should the enzyme-controlled β-oxidation of fatty acids for energy be equated with uncontrolled peroxidation, since the two are chemically and physiologically distinct processes.

The more interesting problem is maintenance. Mitochondrial membranes must preserve the lipid composition respiration requires while operating in an environment that generates reactive oxygen species. Cells meet this with antioxidant systems such as glutathione, phospholipid remodeling, lipid replacement, and mitochondrial quality control. Oxidation is also not uniformly pathological, since reactive species take part in normal cellular signaling; outcomes depend on how much is produced, where, what it reaches, and whether the resulting changes can be controlled or repaired. A functional membrane is therefore not one that avoids all chemical change, but one whose composition can be sustained despite it. For thyroid-dependent metabolism, this means that higher activity may increase the load on maintenance systems, while whether damage accumulates depends on the balance among injury, repair, replacement, and adaptation.

## The Oxygen Budget

Energy substrates place different demands on oxidative metabolism. Complete glucose oxidation yields more ATP per oxygen atom consumed than long-chain fatty acid oxidation: theoretical P/O ratios are roughly 2.58 for glucose and 2.33 for palmitate, an advantage for glucose of about 10–12% {{< cite "lopaschuk2010" >}}. That difference matters when oxygen delivery is limited or workload is high, as in ischemia or heart failure, where a tissue near its oxygen-delivery limit may benefit from shifting its substrate mix. It is easy to turn this into a claim that fatty acid oxidation is pathological, yet the healthy adult heart normally derives 60–90% of its ATP from fatty acid β-oxidation {{< cite "lopaschuk2010" >}}. The heart is also flexible, oxidizing fatty acids, glucose, lactate, and ketone bodies in proportions that shift with physiological conditions.

A modest efficiency penalty is not the same as unusability, and the same caution applies to related inferences. Ketogenesis is a regulated response to conditions favoring hepatic fatty acid oxidation, such as fasting and carbohydrate restriction, and it occurs in people whose tissues are well oxygenated, so the presence of ketones does not establish cellular hypoxia. The lower circulating T3 sometimes seen in fasting or carbohydrate restriction likewise cannot automatically be attributed to oxygen starvation, since energy availability, insulin, leptin, and hypothalamic-pituitary regulation may all contribute.

Oxygen efficiency is best placed within a broader idea of metabolic matching: the relationship among available oxygen, substrate supply, mitochondrial capacity, and the demand on the tissue. Thyroid hormone influences that demand, but the system receiving the signal sets the limit on what it can deliver.

## The Operating Range: Instability at the Extremes

These constraints suggest an operating range for thyroid activity. At the low extreme, insufficient signaling reduces energy expenditure, thermogenesis, and the metabolic processes normal tissue function requires, and the structural consequences are visible in skin and connective tissue. In myxedema, hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans, particularly hyaluronic acid, accumulate in the dermis and other interstitial tissues and bind water {{< cite "safer2011" >}}. A common intuition holds that weakened ion pumps simply let cells swell, but this is mainly a change in extracellular matrix metabolism and tissue water distribution.

At the high extreme, excessive signaling pushes energy expenditure past what the organism can comfortably sustain. Hyperthyroidism can produce muscle wasting, increased protein turnover, cardiovascular strain, and disturbed energy balance. Part of the picture is catabolic demand, and part is likely mitochondrial uncoupling: under excess T3, more oxygen is consumed and more energy is dissipated as heat per unit of ATP generated, so throughput rises without a proportionate gain in usable work. The difficulty is not necessarily that mitochondria have become incapable of respiration, but that sustained stimulation raises both metabolic throughput and the demands on tissue maintenance.

The two extremes therefore fail by different mechanisms. Too little signaling undermines the processes that sustain normal tissue activity; too much raises energetic and material demands beyond what maintenance can cover. Between them lies a range in which metabolic activity and structural preservation remain compatible, and that range moves with tissue, developmental stage, nutrition, oxygen supply, and workload. For this reason I hesitate to treat thyroid activity as a single axis running from undesirable sluggishness to desirable acceleration. Speed alone is not the meaningful variable; the relationship between activity and the capacity to sustain it is.

## Serum Hormones and Tissue-Level Regulation

Circulating TSH, T4, and T3 are important measurements, but individual tissues regulate their own exposure to active hormone through transport, receptor expression, and local metabolism. The deiodinases are central to this. Type 1 deiodinase (D1) contributes much of the peripheral, largely hepatic and renal, conversion of T4 to T3 that feeds the circulating pool, while D2 and D3 shape hormone availability inside particular cells. D2 converts T4 into active T3, raising local availability. D3 inactivates thyroid hormones, converting T4 into reverse T3 and T3 into 3,3′-T2. Their relative expression helps determine how much thyroid signaling a given cellular environment receives.

One especially interesting connection runs through hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α). HIF-1α binds directly to the *DIO3* promoter and induces D3, and hypoxia-induced D3 reduces T3-dependent oxygen consumption in cultured neuronal and hepatocyte cell lines {{< cite "simonides2008" >}}. In a rat model of right ventricular (RV) hypertrophy progressing to failure, HIF-1α and D3 were selectively upregulated in the hypertrophic RV myocardium, lowering local T3 content and signaling {{< cite "simonides2008" >}}. A tissue under oxygen stress can thus reduce its own thyroid-dependent metabolic activity, which may help constrain energy demand when oxidative metabolism is limited. It also shows why serum measurements cannot capture every aspect of tissue-level signaling.

The limits of this finding need stating as plainly as its implications. It is sometimes extended to the claim that low circulating T3 universally conceals excessive hormone turnover elsewhere, or that every metabolic stress state engages the same deiodinase program. Neither follows from these data, and both would require direct measurement. What is established is narrower: local thyroid hormone availability can be regulated independently of circulating concentrations, and hypoxia-associated signaling contributes to that regulation in particular tissues. Read this way, thyroid signaling is part of a system of metabolic adaptation, not a process running independently of cellular conditions.

## The Guardian Depends on What It Guards

T3 can raise metabolic throughput. It regulates mitochondrial activity, ion transport, thermogenesis, and tissue-specific gene expression, and through these actions it helps coordinate the processes by which living structures are built and maintained. What it does not do is supply the structural substrates those processes require. It cannot stand in for an appropriately organized mitochondrial membrane, an adequate supply of [essential fatty acids]({{< relref "essays/essential-architecture" >}}), functional respiratory proteins, or the machinery of repair.

These systems are interdependent. Membrane architecture influences respiratory capacity, respiratory capacity limits the energy available for maintenance, nutritional supply determines whether damaged components can be replaced, and oxygen availability sets the conditions under which oxidative metabolism can run. Thyroid hormone regulates activity within that environment. When the relationships stay compatible, higher metabolic activity can support growth, differentiation, repair, and adaptation; when they become mismatched, the same signal may coexist with impaired maintenance, altered tissue function, or compensatory reductions in metabolic activity.

The hypothesis I want to preserve is that thyroid hormone helps coordinate the energetic conditions required for biological form, and a stronger version of it is experimentally testable. If thyroid signaling contributes to structural maintenance through its effects on cellular energy systems, then changes in local thyroid activity should be examined together with mitochondrial respiratory capacity, membrane lipid composition, ATP-dependent transport, and measurable tissue function. The informative experiment would not stop at asking whether more T3 increases oxygen consumption, since that measures acceleration. It would ask whether a given change in thyroid signaling improves a tissue's capacity to maintain or restore its organization, and under which metabolic and nutritional conditions it does so.

Form is not preserved by a hormone acting alone. It emerges from the coordinated regulation of energy production, material supply, structural organization, and repair, and thyroid hormone matters because it influences how these processes operate together.

The guardian depends on what it guards.
